### There is no posttraining data commons

#### [Submission URL](https://openathena.ai/blog/there-is-no-posttraining-data-commons/) | 116 points | by [lukasschwab](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=lukasschwab) | [26 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50352641)

**Pretraining got a ready-made commons of documents; posttraining needs tasks, and nobody has been archiving those.** David Hall's argument is that Wikipedia, ArXiv, Stack Exchange, Gutenberg and Common Crawl (about 10 petabytes since 2007) were lying around when LLMs needed text. Nothing equivalent exists now that frontier work has shifted to posttraining and agentic RL. His own lab's open models, along with Nemotron, still draw most of their pretraining data from that commons, so truly open LLMs depend on continued access to it.

The core distinction: **pretraining consumes documents, while posttraining consumes tasks**, and a usable RL task is a frozen abstraction of what humans actually do. Human action is situated, conditional and unrepeatable, like an oral story told once. An RL task has to preserve three things: a world, an intent, and tools for acting on the world. It also needs a grader to score the outcome.

Software is the exception, which Hall offers as the reason agentic AI hit coding first. Version control, CI, unit tests, and containers have spent decades making work durable and replayable from an identical starting state, which is exactly what RL wants. The imperfections are real (Debian has chased reproducible builds since 2013 and still doesn't call itself fully reproducible), but open-source software is still a commons of preserved work.

The excerpt cuts off before the author's conclusion, so what he proposes for building a posttraining commons outside software isn't visible here. The framing alone is useful: the bottleneck isn't raw data volume but making action durable, and only one domain has put in that effort.

Balestriero and LeCun's argument is that the Platonic Representation Hypothesis is a matter of how you pick positive pairs, and that scale alone doesn't get you there.

The theorem: JEPAs trained separately on any number of modalities learn the same representation up to a single rotation, provided their positive pairs are two observations of one scene ("good views") and misalignment is bounded otherwise. Next-token prediction fits the same framework as a JEPA with *bad views*, since its positive pairs are different parts of one document. That is the paper's explanation for why language models only align with vision as they scale.

The empirical claims, validated on setups from 145k captions up to the texts of 940M web images:

- **Efficiency:** good views reach the same alignment with 42x fewer training samples than next-token prediction on the same corpus.
- **Scaling law:** a law of alignment that predicts unseen runs within seed noise.

- **Pair-free zero-shot:** no paired data is needed even for that rotation. On the latest pair-free benchmark, the text JEPA gets 64% top-5 on ImageNet-100, against at most 56% for zero-shot results with language models up to 8B.

The theorem is conditional. It holds when views are good and misalignment is bounded, and the abstract doesn't say how often real web captions meet that. The comparisons also set a small purpose-built JEPA against general-purpose LLMs, which weren't trained for alignment. That is the paper's point, but it is not a like-for-like contest.
