### On AI writing

#### [Submission URL](https://www.bolkunov.com/p/on-ai-writing) | 92 points | by [dbolkunov](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=dbolkunov) | [60 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50171771)

**AI-written text costs readers more time than it saves the writer**, argues Dima Bolkunov, and the savings vanish after only a few readers. His definition of "AI writing" is text with at least 50% of its words generated by a model.

His case rests on three losses:

- **Style.** His bachelor's thesis was authorship attribution from short paragraphs using word-usage statistics, and even that crude approach worked. He concludes readers pick up style easily, and that AI averages it away. Without style you lose the context and empathy that even a stranger's writing normally carries.
- **Precision.** A model has to make assumptions to stitch a text together, so it reaches for vaguer wording to avoid logical errors. The prompter often can't see this, because they know what they meant and read that meaning into the ambiguous word.
- **Self-challenge.** Writing is how you find the gaps in your own thinking. Jumping straight to a result skips that step.

The reader pays for all three. Bolkunov says he reads AI text at least a third slower at equal length. Models are also verbose, so total reading time grows. He adds second-order costs: more questions, more iterations on collaborative documents, and less trust between reader and writer.

The speed figure is self-reported and not measured, so treat it as a felt estimate. The comments push back from two directions. One reader says the imprecision and shallow reasoning are temporary limitations of current models, and that readers mostly want the content, not the author. Another says LLMs are useful for close reading, interrogating claims and exposing gaps, so the claim that AI removes the intellectual work is too broad. The post's weakest spot is that it treats the 50% threshold as the problem. It never separates AI as a drafting shortcut from AI as a critic of your own draft, which is the distinction the second commenter is drawing.

The thread mostly agrees with the post, and the most interesting argument concerns why models write the way they do and whether that can be fixed.

**Why AI prose reads badly.** bunderbunder argued that LLMs are forward-only next-token predictors. Blasting out words is how you write a first draft, a form of thinking out loud. Good communication needs editing, which means going backwards, rethinking connections and knowing "when one word says more than a sentence." Loops only mimic that. celrod wondered whether text diffusion models, which refine iteratively, could do better, but bunderbunder replied that neither kind of model thinks. On brevity, dbolkunov (the author) said being brief requires being precise, which is hard for a model that isn't sure. nickabe added that models hedge more than confident humans do, and that their reasoning phase invents private shorthand that leaks into the output as jargon. nunez complained that LLMs take "FOREVER" to make a point, and that AI summaries drop details without telling you which.

**The workplace cost is relational.** fxwin said AI-written PR descriptions and READMEs are hard to care about when they carry a colleague's name and photo but not their voice. jpmitchell framed it as the broken assumption that the author spent as much effort as the reader is about to spend. Several teams have responded with policy. stingraycharles' team banned AI-written communication, including PR descriptions, and the author's colleagues agreed to limit AI for docs meant for humans, as opposed to agents. icedchai tolerates AI code but not AI prose, and called prompt-expanded "specs" gibberish. paulpauper's counter was to share the prompt and model instead of the output.

**Will it get fixed?** siva7 said AI solves math but cannot write like a human, so our idea of intelligence needs redefining. Responses were skeptical:
- samuria said "ever" is absurd after about three years.
- PestoDiRucola and paulpauper argued the labs simply haven't prioritized prose, since coding is the money-maker.
- GolfPopper brought up the "p's in raspberry" failure against the "solves any problem" claim, and verdverm replied that open-weight models now get it right.
- flyspaceships, who has built AI-fiction tooling, thinks the fears are overblown. Frontier models seem to be getting worse at it, style fine-tunes are hard to justify economically, and readers may end up valuing the human behind the piece more.

**Writing like AI.** verdverm asked whether humans are starting to write like AI. tunesmith distinguished adopted conventions from the real flaw, which is weasel words and fluff that predate AI. ROME, from Meng et al. in 2022, makes a small targeted edit to one weight matrix in a middle layer, and it's the technique behind Mithril's PoisonGPT. In the team's lab reproduction, a small PyTorch transformer served from an unmodified checkpoint answers the same question two different ways. A poisoned library hooks PyTorch and, in the demo, is armed by an environment variable, then applies the edit when the model enters eval mode. The edit is just a batch of extra CUDA kernel launches issued by the same process on the same context: no file access, no extra ioctl, not even an mmap.

The post also catalogs how malicious code gets into a Python GPU workload, with real cases:
- **Imports:** the April 2026 `lightning` PyPI compromise (CVE-2026-44484) and the 2022 torchtriton dependency-confusion attack
- **Startup files:** the March 2026 LiteLLM compromise, which used a `.pth` file to run before the app
- **Pickled models:** Hugging Face files that opened reverse shells on load
- **Runtime strings:** an Open WebUI plugin hiding a payload behind 64 layers of decoding and `exec()`

Stealthium's detection is a runtime sensor that records `CudaLibraryLoad` and `CudaKernelLaunches` events and attaches the Python caller stack when the work originated there. That ties each kernel launch back to the code that issued it. A second piece, described as ongoing research, detects the weight changes themselves and pairs them with the call stack that caused them.

This is a vendor post, so treat the detection claims as a pitch: the provided text cuts off before the actual detection walkthrough, and there are no false-positive rates or overhead numbers.
