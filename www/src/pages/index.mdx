### The best response to AI slop and online noise is from Robin Williams

#### [Submission URL](https://jayacunzo.com/blog/your-move-chief) | 394 points | by [herbertl](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=herbertl) | [219 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48703452)

Summary:
Using Robin Williams’s bench monologue from Good Will Hunting as a lens, the piece argues that the antidote to AI slop, infinite advice, and online noise is lived experience. Knowing isn’t the same as living: AI can “know” everything on the internet, but it can’t feel, risk, or be changed by the world. The author warns that a culture obsessed with knowing—courses, hacks, prompts—can make people doubt the value of what they’ve actually done and felt. The call to action: stop outsourcing judgment to content and tools; trust the irreplaceable wisdom that comes from showing up in real life.

Key points:
- Knowledge vs. wisdom: theory is not experience; information isn’t embodiment.
- AI’s limits: it can read the web but can’t “read the room” or live a life.
- Cultural risk: infinite advice erodes confidence in personal, hard-won insight.
- Concrete stakes: reading about love/war/orphanhood isn’t living them.
- The remedy: prioritize firsthand experience, then use tools in service of that, not as a substitute.

Why it matters for builders/creators:
- Taste, judgment, and context come from contact with reality—customers, constraints, consequences.
- Use AI to accelerate, not to decide what matters; let experience set direction.
- In a noisy market, stories rooted in real scars and successes cut through. AI can help draft; it can’t supply the soul.

Bottom line:
AI can know; only you can live. Your move, chief.

Here are the key takeaways from the discussion on Hacker News:

**The Paradox of the Analogy**
A highly upvoted critique quickly pointed out an irony in the article’s central example: the writers (Matt Damon and Ben Affleck) and the actor (Robin Williams) had never actually lived through the specific traumas detailed in the monologue, such as fighting on the front lines of a war or losing a wife to cancer. Some commenters argued this proves that pure "lived experience" isn't strictly necessary for great art; rather, human empathy, imagination, and acting ability bridge the gap to create deep emotional resonance. 

**The Actor's "Well" of Experience**
In response, others fiercely defended the analogy, noting that while the specific details of the scene were fictional, the *emotion* was incredibly real. Commenters pointed out that Robin Williams brought 47 years of complex life experience to that bench—including his very public struggles with addiction and the trauma of losing his friend John Belushi to an overdose. The consensus was that while a human doesn't have to live a script word-for-word, they must possess a deep reservoir of real-world pain and joy to deliver it with authenticity. 

**LLMs and the "Derivative Hollywood" Parallel**
The conversation naturally shifted to how this applies to Large Language Models (LLMs). Several commenters drew a parallel between AI and "Hollywood insiders" who only watch movies to make new movies, resulting in sterile, derivative tropes that lack real-world grounding. LLMs were described as doing something similar: rearranging ingested, heavily processed texts. One user colorfully compared ChatGPT's mimicry to "Soylent Green"—a lifeless reconstitution of humanity. True art, users argued, requires the "alchemy" of real observation.

**Art vs. Mundane Reality**
A poignant sub-thread explored the difference between cinematic dramatization and the profound, yet mundane, reality of everyday life. Commenters shared personal experiences—like sitting by a dying parent's bed or changing thousands of diapers—noting that these ordinary moments don't make for thrilling cinema, but they are the true fabric of life. As one user referenced via a song by the artist Shad, "the greatest story told is the story of an ordinary man growing in love."

**Looking Ahead: The Kasparov Warning**
Despite the agreement that AI currently lacks a soul, a few skeptical voices warned against underestimating technology's future trajectory. Some commenters questioned whether AI might eventually write scripts capable of deeply resonating with humans, drawing a parallel to Garry Kasparov confidently dismissing the capabilities of chess computers just years before being defeated by Deep Blue. 

**Bottom Line from the Comments:** 
While AI can regurgitate the sum of human knowledge, it currently lacks the scars required to give that knowledge weight. However, the mechanism of empathy allows humans to write and perform fiction that *feels* real—a "magic" that AI has yet to crack, even if it is getting better at faking the script.
