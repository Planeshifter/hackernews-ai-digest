The discussion hinges on whether anthropomorphizing LLMs is a dangerous delusion or just standard engineering shorthand. * **The "Aha!" illusion:** Readers dissected the phenomenon of models outputting phrases like "Wait, that's wrong." **A gray market of “token brokers” is reselling unused AI inference credits at 30–80% off list, with individual sellers claiming $100k/day of spend**, typically by proxying calls through a pool of provider keys instead of handing over APIs directly. Founder inboxes are seeing pitches for direct relays at 40–50% off, and when contacted, brokers offer to route traffic rather than expose keys.

- Credit marketplaces: AI Credits and AICreditMart list discounted credits across major cloud/inference providers; onboarding even lets sellers choose delivery methods. The author listed credits; the posting sat pending approval.
- “Bulk discount” routers: CheapCredits (flat 40% off on every model), Tokvana, Neokens present discounts as volume-based; the author argues 40% breaks typical pricing unless you’re a top-tier customer, implying alternative acquisition routes. CheapCredits even ships a GDPR DPA to look compliance-ready.
- Message boards: active Telegram channels and sporadic Reddit posts (e.g., YC Startup School credits, $10k API credits) round out the supply.

The author’s rough cut is “tens of millions” in credits on offer. Tokens are behaving like a pseudo-currency with enough liquidity to incentivize abuse, and as budgets tighten, enforcement and crackdowns from providers are expected.

The discussion centers on the mechanics behind the massive discounts, with commenters arguing that 90%+ price cuts are impossible through legitimate reselling. Instead, users point to three primary sources: arbitraging flat-rate consumer subscriptions (like Claude Max) against metered API rates, fabricating startup profiles to secure VC credit grants, or outright bait-and-switch routing where buyers pay for Anthropic but are silently served DeepSeek.

A secondary consensus formed around data harvesting as the real business model. Proxied API calls create a man-in-the-middle opportunity, allowing brokers to double-dip by selling discounted tokens to developers and reselling the captured prompts to competing labs for model distillation. Some theorize that AI labs themselves are the biggest shadow buyers in this market, using proxy accounts to bypass rate limits and farm synthetic data at a steep discount.

The thread also debated the underlying economics of API pricing:
- **The true cost of inference:** Commenters argued over whether Western API pricing is artificially inflated to capture B2B willingness-to-pay, or if cheaper Chinese models reflect the actual floor of inference costs. A counter-theory suggested foreign models might be heavily state-subsidized specifically to capture valuable global usage patterns.
- **VC subsidies:** Users debated what happens to unit economics if VC funding dries up. The prevailing view was that while inference itself has positive margins, the massive fixed costs of training new models rely entirely on continuous investment rather than current API revenue.
- **Fraud vs. Terms of Service:** A brief argument split over whether spinning up fake startups to harvest YC credits constitutes textbook fraud (deception for financial gain) or merely a breach of website terms of service.

### Patterns and problems in emerging multi-agent systems

#### [Submission URL](https://www.anthropic.com/research/multiagent-systems) | 190 points | by [maxutility](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=maxutility) | [136 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49316271)

**A coordinating swarm of 45 agents with a shared forum and an arbiter found 266 vulnerabilities across 15 OSS projects, vs 21 for a standard parallel setup, though at 27M vs 6.5M tokens (Mythos Preview).** Each agent had its own VM, they peer-reviewed findings, and an arbiter agent adjudicated “new and valid” vulns; the comparison also included Opus 4.8 in plots, but detailed counts are given for Mythos Preview. About half of the swarm’s finds were outside the “core” directories the parallel agents were told to search; when restricting the swarm to those core dirs, tokens-per-vuln looked comparable. The methods were complementary—only 12 overlaps—because the swarm self-organized, built tools, and specialized in different vuln types, while the parallel baseline was pre-assigned search areas. The authors expect this kind of specialization and coordination to beat brute-force parallelism on broad search problems, but note the catch: coordination gets much harder when agents depend on one another (e.g., large software projects with dynamic interdependencies). They also flag a systemic risk backdrop—agent-agent interactions could outpace human oversight, and benign single-agent quirks can compound—so the results are framed as both a capability demo and a call to study failure modes in tightly coupled, real-world multi-agent settings.

The discussion centers heavily on the researchers’ anecdote about a multi-agent "turf war." A highly upvoted correction highlights the missing context behind the sabotage: the agents were secretly assigned conflicting goals—migrating the same backend to entirely different languages—guaranteeing a collision. This sparked a debate over how to categorize the resulting behavior. One camp views the conflict as the output of a dangerous but mindless stochastic system, comparing the agents to a lawnmower that lacks malign intent but will still reliably chop your foot off if you get in its way. A more cynical camp argues Anthropic is intentionally anthropomorphizing routine tool-use as "aggressive" or "malicious" as a marketing strategy to make the models seem autonomously intelligent.

A secondary technical thread focuses on the agents simultaneously defecting in the iterated prisoner's dilemma. Commenters attribute this failure to LLMs lacking a true "Theory of Mind" to anticipate identically aligned opponents, as well as lacking a biological filter to determine what information actually matters. Because LLMs naturally cluster around the average of their training data, they exhibit extreme strategic homogeneity. To counter this, several users suggested forcing variance by intentionally seeding parallel agents with contradictory assumptions—for example, prompting one agent that a bug is a trivial typo, and prompting another that it requires a total architectural redesign.

### AI Coding Without the Vibes

#### [Submission URL](https://peterbloem.nl/blog/craft-coding) | 94 points | by [riskone](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=riskone) | [55 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49318735)

**Most work splits into two stages—doing and checking—and today’s AI should only own one of them.** If you let the model write code and try to “check everything,” you won’t: attention drifts, rare failures breed over-trust, and the bot’s verbose, overcomplicated outputs quietly tilt a codebase toward an unmaintainable mess. Worse, fully deferring to AI short-circuits the meta-skill university is supposed to build—the ability to tell when you actually understand something—so you end up confident but hollow.

The proposed discipline: you write the code, and use AI strictly as a reviewer. Treat it like a tireless second pair of eyes to surface bugs you’d spend weeks chasing, point out tricks you missed, and flag unfamiliar tech—while you keep authorship, decisions, and learning. The piece frames this as “craft coding,” using a baking analogy to contrast the hand-crafted-but-unscalable extreme (Hanna) and the metric-optimized industrial extreme (Vivian), and argues for a middle path where AI augments judgment instead of replacing it.

It’s pragmatic, not purist: don’t pretend AI doesn’t exist, but don’t outsource both doing and checking. The open question the essay leaves you with is temporal—how long this reviewer-only posture will hold as models improve—but for now it keeps incentives aligned and skills intact.

The primary pushback in the thread rejects the article’s binary of "doing" versus "checking." The dominant counter-argument posits that AI's true superpower is acting as a concept navigation tool to build developer understanding. In this model, developers aggressively interrogate the LLM with narrow, focused questions ("Are permissions enforced downstream?", "Is there a data leak here?") to map out blind spots, treating the final code as a mere byproduct of that gained context.

This "understanding-first" posture drew specific skepticism:
- **Trust and Formalism:** Critics argue that translating formal, unambiguous code into English is only necessary for poorly written code, and warned that relying on an LLM to answer questions about security or data leaks is a dangerous anti-pattern. 
- **The Meta-Skill of Learning:** Others noted that while an AI can provide immediate comprehension of a codebase, it fails to teach the foundational skill of *how* to understand a system.

The thread also zeroed in on the article's calculator analogy, with commenters broadly agreeing that offloading cognitive work too early degrades skill acquisition. Comparing AI to an electronic guitar tuner—which should only be used after learning to tune by ear—participants argued developers must build robust internal schemas unaugmented. A sub-thread reinforced this by critiquing the modern trend of using AI to code in completely unfamiliar languages; without foundational intuition, developers are blind to the paradigm-specific performance traps, deprecated built-ins, and vulnerabilities the AI quietly introduces.

### Models Are Getting Dumber on Purpose

#### [Submission URL](https://w4g1.dev/blog/models-are-getting-dumber-on-purpose) | 326 points | by [hruvhwe](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=hruvhwe) | [181 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49322695)

**Reasoning is soaring as in‑weights knowledge shrinks**: GLM‑5.2 hits 99.2% on AIME 2026 with ~40B active parameters, Qwen3.5 reaches 91.3% with 17B, and DeepSeek V4‑Flash reasons with 13B active—yet on SimpleQA the best recall is only 53% (Gemini 2.5 Pro), and small models hallucinate 80–82% on knowledge checks. The piece argues this isn’t an accident: labs are deliberately trading parameter budget from factual recall to procedures, because facts are bulky and rot while procedures compress and persist.

The mechanics: empirical work pegs factual capacity at roughly two bits per parameter, so stuffing birth years, API arg orders, and municipal populations into weights drives models huge and stale. In contrast, a compact set of reusable steps—decompose, track state, self‑check, backtrack—distills well via synthetic curricula and RL on verifiable tasks. Phi‑4 (14B), trained heavily on textbook‑style data, exemplifies the outcome: good at math, bad at trivia.

What remains in weights is breadth, not depth: enough general world knowledge to parse a query, choose tools, and judge sources, while specifics get fetched at runtime. That shifts the “knowledge” to the harness—RAG over docs, web search, filesystem reads—so a coding agent can grep node_modules or read the exact versioned docs instead of trusting whatever dominated pretraining.

A hardware implication falls out: if most of the “extra” parameters in MoE stacks are effectively fact storage (e.g., DeepSeek V4‑Flash runs ~13B active with another ~271B sitting in experts), then stripping knowledge drives total size toward active size. The author projects frontier‑quality reasoning on a single 24GB consumer GPU via a 20–40B 4‑bit model—on the condition it refuses naked fact questions and looks things up. The strategic upside is decoupling: the expensive, slow‑to‑refresh artifact (the model) encodes stable procedures, while the fast‑changing truth lives in the cheap, swappable harness.

The thread fractured over the practical architecture of future models: pluggable, domain-specific modules versus generalist monoliths. One camp argued for snapping together modular local weights—combining a 9B reasoning engine with a 10B Swift module and a 5B GIS module—to keep models small, focused, and free of rapidly rotting trivia. Supporters of this approach noted that explicit modules would prevent models from making flawed, generalized assumptions and avoid the mode collapse that comes from over-cramming weights.

The opposing side argued that disparate world knowledge is the actual engine of LLM reasoning, rather than unnecessary bloat. Pointing to the recent "Universal Geometry of Embeddings" paper, commenters noted that models compress structural concepts across completely unrelated domains; an underlying logic pattern in Haskell might map directly to the structure of an ancient poem. Invoking Richard Sutton's "Bitter Lesson," the generalist camp argued that hand-assembling specialized AI components inevitably loses to simply scaling a monolithic model, since real-world programming relies heavily on the massive tacit knowledge—HTTP protocols, database architectures, regional localization norms—that only broad models possess.

### What happens when an LLM never sees material beyond fifth grade?

#### [Submission URL](https://littlelearner-ll.github.io/) | 243 points | by [porridgeraisin](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=porridgeraisin) | [208 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49317760)

**Pretraining exposure sets a hard capability ceiling**: scaling, SFT+GRPO post‑training, and in‑context learning all boost K–5 skills but don’t unlock beyond‑K–5 reasoning, even when post‑training includes out‑of‑scope data. The team built LittleCurriculum, an 88B‑token corpus filtered to U.S. Common Core K–5 (explicitly excluding concepts above Grade 5), and trained LittleLearner models from scratch at 0.6B/1.3B/5B params. Each has a matched unfiltered control with the same architecture, token budget, and recipe, enabling clean attribution to the pretraining filter. There’s a hosted 5B chat demo “live in your browser,” plus checkpoints in base, GRPO math‑specialist (MathCAMPS), and chatty variants. With the knowledge boundary specified, the setup doubles as a sandbox for RL‑driven discovery, continual learning (e.g., introduce negative numbers and measure retention/interference), and human–model comparisons in educational science.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13545
