It builds on Qwenputer by Divyansh Agrawal, which compiled a CHIP-8 and then a RISC-V CPU into Qwen3.5-4B. nesformer reuses that compiler and its central trick: the Gated DeltaNet state serves as the machine's memory. The correctness claim is concrete. For the bundled test games, every frame matches a conventional 6502 emulator exactly, and a `check` command runs weights and reference CPU side by side, stopping at the first divergence.

The catch is speed and scope:

Setup is light: Python 3.11+, numpy, scipy, numba, and pillow. Unzip the code and weights archives side by side, and `python -m nesformer run` saves an animated GIF. ### A tiny world model running games on a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/SheepSeb/tinyworldformer) | 268 points | by [airinei](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=airinei) | [18 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50295450)

The trick is weight reuse. One transformer block is applied 1–4 times with the same weights, and every pass is trained against the next frame, so loop count becomes a runtime speed/accuracy dial. The Pico runs two loops, costing 2–8M multiply-adds per frame depending on the game. The world model is int8-quantized, and both networks run on the RP2350's core 1 while core 0 handles USB and display.

Exact-frame accuracy on held-out transitions (every cell must match the real numpy game, with frames right after random events excluded) at two loops:
- **Flappy Bird:** 100%
- **Breakout:** 99.99%
- **Pong:** 99.45%
- **Pac-Man lite:** 86.4%
- **Doom-lite:** 83.6%

Doom-lite's number is pessimistic by construction: one misshaded pixel out of 768 cells fails the whole frame. Its loop ablation is the clearest demo of the loop idea, with one pass at 59.8% and two at 83.6%. More loops don't always help, though: Pac-Man peaks at two and Doom-lite at three.

Extras: a streaming RL agent (stream-x) can replace the pretrained actor and learn from scratch on the board, inside its own dream. Any button press hands control to you. The README also lists a browser version and a Rust port directory (`tinyworldformer-rs`), and you can play the real games in a terminal via `uv run python -m tinyworldformer.games.play`.

The catch is scope. These are 20×16 to 48×48 token grids with hand-built games whose hidden state is drawn into the frame, so this shows a world model fits on a microcontroller, not that it generalizes beyond them.

- **Breakout:** four ball tokens, one per diagonal, and the ball moves one cell per step.

### Sleep framework: A new way for LLMs to retain knowledge in continual learning

#### [Submission URL](https://research.google/blog/the-sleep-framework-a-new-way-for-llms-to-retain-knowledge-in-continual-learning/) | 74 points | by [acossta](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=acossta) | [6 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50272479)

**Google Research proposes giving deployed LLMs a periodic offline "sleep" phase** where short-term context gets distilled into permanent weights, aimed at ending both catastrophic forgetting and the fixed knowledge cutoff. The authors claim the model can retain long-context knowledge up to 10M tokens, absorb new facts, and improve reasoning.

The mechanism has two stages that run with the model offline:

- **Consolidation:** newly unlocked low-rank experts in a Mixture-of-Experts (using Google's Hope architecture) sit in slow-changing memory blocks, so old parameters aren't overwritten. Knowledge from fast in-context memory is transferred into them via on-policy Generalized Knowledge Distillation plus an RL imitation objective that rewards similarity (Levenshtein distance) to teacher text. The temporary high-frequency parameters are then reset.
- **Dreaming:** the MoE router deliberately activates non-standard experts to generate synthetic samples that mix domains. Gradient-based selection filters these, and the model fine-tunes on the top-rated ones, checking whether downstream performance improves.

The neuroscience framing (NREM, REM, synaptic pruning) is branding over a scheduled post-training loop. The post says as much: everything runs inside a fixed architecture under human-defined training rules.

The catch: the capacity grows with each sleep cycle, since consolidation depends on pre-allocated expert blocks being unlocked. The excerpt doesn't say how that scales over many cycles, and the benchmark details were cut off before the results section, so the 10M-token claim is unverified here. It's also a research proposal, not a shipped product, and it needs a specific architecture rather than working on arbitrary existing models.

The thread is thin on debate. Most of it is people trying to understand the mechanism, plus one practitioner's account of a competing approach.

- **Audit and lineage as the deployment problem:** gopalv's team built a similar system, "Dreamtime", earlier this year. It distills learnings across sessions from user corrections of unsatisfactory responses, not from the token or KV-cache layer. It works as a sawtooth: accumulate context, check for dissonance, compress it back, retire the old version. Their view is that customers' real blocker is auditability. You have to be able to ask "when did this change, and who changed it?", and that is hard to answer from weights unless lineage back to the original sessions is retained. They also don't want a trained model to become an "accomplice" for a user nudging an agent repeatedly to hide financial crimes. gopalv also found it odd that the paper's diagrams build on the KV-cache, which they contrast with Databricks' RAFT.
- **Mechanism, plainly restated:** imimayj1337 read it as reserved, unpopulated parameters in the architecture that get filled during sleep and reinforced by the dreaming step. They guessed that only a vertically integrated player like Google could pull it off, and that compute-constrained organizations would struggle. gavinray roughly agreed and added that short-term memories are scored, and the useful ones are integrated through a transform of the old weights. That is meant to avoid dumping whole memories into the weights additively.
- **Enthusiasm:** mdp2021 called it a crucial missing piece toward AGI, needing only critical thinking added during cleanup. No one picked up or challenged that claim.
- **References:** macleginn linked the actual arXiv paper and called DreamCoder a favorite ML paper that this reminded them of.

- The hands-on argument is that the rankings contradict experience. People say GLM 5.3 beats GPT-6 Astra on backend and frontend work, and that Haiku 5.5 outranking every open model except Kimi K3.1 "is not true".
- hgoel's structural version is that whatever OpenAI and Anthropic optimize for becomes the definition of intelligence, and saturated older tests get discarded.

**The less conspiratorial readings**
- rbehrends doubts either bias or benchmaxxing is the main cause. The coding index alone looks plausible, for example Terra no longer beating Fable. The new Terminal-Bench-Science hurts small coding-tuned flash models, which wouldn't be surprising. The deeper question is whether one aggregate number across such different tasks means anything.
- sourcecodeplz says smaller open models are strong at coding and tool use but lack world knowledge, which fits the HLE knowledge split.
- mzl says new releases tend to expose weaknesses in the index itself, because models ranked together don't feel equally good.
- arcanemachiner plays devil's advocate: any new benchmark gets gamed, so periodic replacement is expected, though bias toward US models is possible.
- yorwba argues individual benchmarks are noisy and saturate. In their view a weighted sum just chooses which noise to amplify, and they prefer Epoch's ECI because it models saturation and gives error bars.

**Specific methodological objections**
- **LLM judge:** chvid questions using a closed model, GPT-6 Luna, as the FrontierCode judge. iwhalen links a paper on self-preference bias in LLM judges.
- **Languages:** kamov calls the multilingual language selection odd, with broad Asian coverage but only a few Romance European languages and no Slavic, Nordic or Finno-Ugric ones.

**Side argument on open weights**
- lousken says closed models degrade after launch while open weights stay fixed. pritambaral adds that open weights can improve through fine-tuning.
- tsss counters that hosted open models vary with configuration such as KV cache, and that hosts may cut corners. dannyw replies that reputable hosts like Fireworks, Cloudflare and Baseten have a reputation to protect.

The unresolved crux is whether the shifts come from deliberate tilting or from an index so sensitive to its task mix that any revision reshuffles the rankings. Private test sets make it impossible for outsiders to settle that.

### AI execs may be too out of touch to build for the general public

#### [Submission URL](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/28/ai-execs-may-be-too-out-of-touch-to-build-for-the-general-public/) | 88 points | by [pavel_lishin](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=pavel_lishin) | [69 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50281826)

**Marissa Mayer's attempt to show off her new AI app turned into a PR problem.** In a New York Times interview about Dazzle, an assistant that learns from your camera roll, she said it helped write three bullet points about her 10-year-old daughter for a school birthday presentation: "empathetic, creative, and an animal lover." Critics noted that the description is generic enough to fit most 10-year-olds.

Mayer later defended herself on X. She said she was testing a prototype, not asking the app to explain her child to her, and that it surfaced forgotten kindergarten projects like a "snake devenomer" and a "sandcat playground." But the same interview had her pitching Dazzle for birthday themes, playdates, vacation activities and Halloween costumes. She also floated her assistant coordinating with her husband's. Asked whether a "frictionless future" planned by AI is depressing, she answered, "I haven't really thought that through."

TechCrunch's Amanda Silberling treats this as a pattern, not a one-off:

- Sam Altman said he couldn't imagine raising a newborn without ChatGPT, and pitched a podcast that explains your kids' interests to you.
- Mark Zuckerberg argued that the average American has fewer than three friends but wants about 15, and predicted AI therapists and romantic partners will become more socially acceptable.

The public isn't with them. A Pew study found that 50% of U.S. adults expect AI to reduce people's ability to form meaningful relationships, and only 5% expect it to make connections easier.

Silberling's thesis is that Silicon Valley's efficiency culture, the same one behind sleep-tracker optimization and peptide stacks, is being applied to relationships, where friction is the point. It's an opinion column, and it picks the most quotable examples. Still, Mayer's "haven't thought that through" answer undercuts her own defense.

The thread quickly moves away from Mayer's app and into a larger argument over what AI and robots should take off people's plates. Most commenters share the sense that tech executives are out of touch, but they split on what caregiving automation would actually mean.

**The "out of touch" framing.** The top comment points to Elon Musk's pitch that robots will look after your dog, kids, or aging parents, calling those "the things normal people with healthy relationships want to be doing." Others piled on with Altman's podcast-about-your-kids idea. One called the executives "sad psychopaths," and another called them "deeply anti-human." A counter-joke: Mayer was perhaps busy A/B testing blue shades. The sharpest version of the complaint is that robotics belongs in work, not in places where human experience is the value. A reply to that: "Or maybe we want the robots to do our jobs so we can spend time with people." The rejoinder was that people would then be unemployed and scrounging for food.

**Caregiving is hard, so is the pain the robots' target?** Commenters did not dispute that raising a child while parents decline is brutal. They disagreed about the cause.
- One side blamed structure rather than the people involved. Care is unpaid full-time work squeezed around a job, nuclear families lost community help, and dual incomes are now required. One reply: "And the result is no kids."
- A sub-thread argued over whether people should simply not have kids if they can't handle it. Others pushed back that parental illness is something life throws at you, not a choice, and that childcare is unaffordable for almost everyone. One noted it's free in Germany. The poster arguing for fewer kids said the real fix is government-funded childcare, which won't happen without a demographic crisis.
- TheOtherHobbes suggested falling birth rates partly reflect that the internet shows what parenting involves. By this view, robots doing chores and bureaucracy would free time for the relationships themselves.

**Does robot elder care actually make sense?** This was the thread's real fault line.
- Supporters said it has been pursued for decades, and that nursing homes face severe labor shortages. One wants to live independently into their 80s rather than burden their only kid. Another would rather control a robot than depend on a stranger.
- Critics said that's a stretch. If you can afford and maintain the robot, you could probably pay humans. They argued the fix is better pay and breaking up large care companies. Another said family caregivers act from love, culture, and obligation, which mercenary caregivers (or machines) won't replicate.
- A definitional fight ran under all of this. One commenter said "robot care" just means chores like trash, medication reminders, and finding keys, and nobody objects to a dishwasher. Others said people mean showering, toileting, companionship, and monitoring. And nurses do far more than chores: they handle medication, meals, outings, and checking on well-being. Companionship, most agreed, is where the line falls.

**Cost reality check.** Nursing homes already exist and are expensive. One commenter cited roughly £5k per week for an aunt's end-of-life care. Another pointed out that wanting to care personally and being able to are different things.

The thread never settles whether the technology would free people to be present or merely replace the presence itself. That turns on what you assume gets automated.
