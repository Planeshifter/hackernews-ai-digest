### The efficient frontier of LLM inference

#### [Submission URL](https://www.baseten.co/blog/the-efficient-frontier-of-llm-inference/) | 146 points | by [philipkiely](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=philipkiely) | [41 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49529898)

**Inference engineering splits into tradeoff-tuning versus frontier-shifting techniques**, and the choice determines whether you’re sliding along the latency–throughput curve or moving the curve itself. The frontier here is jagged: tiny config changes can cause outsized jumps, so teams find cutoffs empirically with sweeps.

- Tradeoff-tuners (move along the frontier):
  - **Batch sizing**: token-level continuous batching avoids queueing delays, but configured batch size still sets per-user latency vs total tokens/GPU (cost).
  - **Parallelism strategy**: Tensor Parallelism lowers latency despite costly all-to-all (fast over NVLink); Expert Parallelism helps both latency and throughput (narrow EP for speed, wide EP—even rack-wide—for throughput); Attention Data Parallelism replicates attention to raise throughput at the expense of per-request speed.
  - **Quantization (hybrid)**: lowers latency and boosts throughput—effectively pushing the serving frontier outward—while introducing a separate, jagged quality–efficiency frontier. Microscaling formats like MXFP4/NVFP4 can yield large efficiency wins with little-to-no quality loss.

- Frontier-shifters (push the entire curve out):
  - **Kernel optimization and runtime improvements**: faster CUDA kernels and tighter forward-pass runtimes reduce resources per token, compounding gains across the stack. These wins multiply with hardware advances (e.g., 2× from software and 2× from hardware ≈ 4× effective serving).

Assuming agentic coding workloads on GLM-5.3 or Kimi K3 with KV cache reuse and KV-aware routing, the practical playbook is to allocate efficiency where it matters—per-user speed for latency-sensitive paths, or throughput for batch/low-cost flows—while hunting the compounding wins that permanently expand your options.

- **The engine divide:** The discussion grounded the article's theory in the practical frustrations of current software. Commenters highlighted a gap between datacenter-first engines like vLLM—which crash frequently on mixed consumer setups like paired RTX 4090s and 5090s—and highly compatible tools like llama.cpp, which currently struggle with high concurrency due to rigid VRAM utilization and a lack of decode/prefill segregation.
- **Speculative decoding security:** Comparisons between LLM speculative decoding and 90s-era CPU speculative execution prompted questions about potential Spectre-style vulnerabilities. Engineers clarified the mechanisms are fundamentally different: LLM speculation merely proposes text tokens that the main model strictly and losslessly validates, meaning it cannot leak out-of-bounds system state. 
- **The next architectural shifts:** While some argued the core inference playbook has been static for years, the article's author and other practitioners pointed to Prefill/Decode (P/D) disaggregation as the most significant recent shift, allowing providers to scale compute-bound prefilling and memory-bound decoding onto entirely different nodes. Others flagged "recursive depth" strategies—driving internal state changes within the transformer rather than executing full forward passes—as the next major efficiency unlock.
