### Using LLM for confidence scoring is a bad idea

#### [Submission URL](https://rumple.github.io/blog/2026/10/20/asking-llm-for-confidence.html) | 193 points | by [jimauthors](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=jimauthors) | [97 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50176082)

**A number an LLM writes when asked "how confident are you?" is just more generated text**, the author argues, not a readout of anything inside the model. It's an opinion piece aimed at agent designers who use self-reported scores to gate decisions.

The argument rests on a forecaster analogy. A trustworthy confidence needs two properties: it reflects how much information the prediction is based on, and it has been checked against outcomes on the same kind of question. Calibration means that of all the times a forecaster said 70%, it rained about 70% of the time. Calibration alone isn't enough, since a forecaster who always says the seasonal base rate is calibrated but useless. Without a written record of forecasts and outcomes, you can't know either way.

An LLM's "0.85" has neither property. Pretraining scores the model on predicting the next token, not on whether its stated confidence matches how often it's right. It learned what confidence scores look like in text, not how to make them track accuracy. The author lists symptoms you can reproduce yourself:

- Ask the same question twice and the number changes (0.85, then 0.7), though the information didn't.
- Rephrase to "Are you sure?" and the score moves with nothing new in the problem.
- If the model has already written "This is clearly the right answer," a high score is the natural continuation, so the number follows the prose rather than the evidence.

The text I was given cuts off mid-list, so I can't say what the author recommends instead. The framing, though, points toward measuring confidence empirically, logging predictions against outcomes per task type, rather than asking the model to self-report. That's the part of the argument worth taking to your own agent design.

The thread's core fight is over whether an LLM has any internal confidence it can report. The more productive branch is about how to elicit a usable signal anyway.

**Does a latent confidence exist?** orbital-decay opened by claiming the model does have a consistent internal sense of its confidence, and that the author just asked badly. lynndotpy, geraneum and others demanded a source. lynndotpy called treating softmax output as a probability a "first-year PhD student misconception" and said LLMs generally can't report their own activations. Reubachi's defense was that LLMs run on statistical inference and so are "aware" of their confidence. Commenters pushed back that every neural net or linear regression does statistical inference, which says nothing about being able to articulate it. seurimas and SketchySeaBeast added that the brain runs on electrical signals without giving us insight into them. ShinyLeftPad objected to the word "aware." The best a skeptic would grant is that token logits are a sketchy proxy. Nobody produced a citation for the strong claim.

**Coarse rubrics beat 0-100.** Even the skeptics largely accepted orbital-decay's practical advice.
- bunderbunder said a Likert scale with a rubric works decently, though it won't be calibrated. Fine-grained confidence scores may be a "skeuomorphic" habit carried over from regression models.
- rpdillon had the same experience: 3-5 levels, each with a sentence describing what earns it, give accurate scores, while 1-100 gives arbitrary ones.
- Others added that narrow binary decisions are easier to tune and troubleshoot, and that if binary isn't enough you probably need more axes.
- truculent asked whether a literal probability should also avoid 0-100. bunderbunder said it's fine if it's well calibrated, but scores like cosine similarity get misread as percentages.
- suddenlybananas asked for evidence that these rubric scores are actually accurate, and nobody answered directly.

**Training can fix it.** rahimnathwani gave the main counterpoint to the post's premise. The "never scored against correctness" claim is true of pretraining, and of RLHF/RLVR with their binary rewards. But RLCR adds a Brier penalty to the reward, which beats the "always say the base rate" loophole. He worked an example: a model right 60% of the time scores 0.24 expected penalty by always saying 0.6, but 0.15 if it separates easy from hard questions. That means training can push toward instance-level confidence.

**Side threads.** The YouTube star-rating tangent split between people who say nobody knows what to do with 1-5 scales (cedws, kergonath) and NikolaNovak, who has a very clear personal rubric for them. stingraycharles noted Airbnb hosts post explainers for what each star means, which is the same rubric problem. madrox, a statistics graduate, observed that most of stats training was interpreting model output, a discipline that big-data ML tended to forget.

### Show HN: Omnara – Open-source alternative to Claude Managed Agents

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/omnara-ai/omnara) | 107 points | by [kmansm27](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=kmansm27) | [32 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50183892)

**Omnara runs the agent loop as stateless workers over a Postgres event log**, so an agent is just rows in a database: it survives restarts and costs nothing while idle. It's Apache 2.0 and positions itself as the self-hostable counterpart to Claude Managed Agents and OpenAI's Agents API, with your own models and your own machines.

The design departs from harnesses like Claude Code, Codex, or OpenCode, which keep one long-lived process and a JSONL or SQLite log. Here each loop iteration (one model call, or the tool calls it produced) runs on whichever worker is free, then commits in a single transaction: model output, tool calls, usage, and the next wakeup land together or not at all. If a worker dies mid-iteration, its lease expires and another retries. Agents waiting on a user, tool, or retry hold no worker, so one worker can multiplex thousands of agents.

Execution is decoupled from the loop. A small daemon, `omnarad`, runs on any macOS or Linux machine (laptop, VM, container, cloud sandbox) and dials out, so no inbound ports are needed. An agent can have several machines attached. The author's example is a laptop plus a cloud sandbox on the same agent: when the laptop goes offline, the agent pulls the latest pushed changes into the sandbox and keeps going.

**Getting started:**
- Cloud: write an `agent.yaml` (instruction, model provider config, machine pool) and run `npx omnara agents launch --file agent.yaml`.
- SDK: `@omnara/sdk` (TypeScript) plus a REST API, with an event stream for watching tool calls.
- Self-host: `docker compose -f compose.yaml --profile app up -d` on a single machine. The README warns the local defaults are intentionally insecure, so production needs the self-hosting guide.

Harness features include file and shell tools, subagents, compaction, permission prompts, MCP, skills, and memory, plus secrets and RBAC. Models can be OpenAI, Anthropic, OpenRouter, or self-hosted.

The team started as a phone remote-control app for Claude Code and Codex, and moved to this after concluding that relaying to a local agent wasn't the end state. The Postgres-as-queue approach is a conventional, sturdy pick for durability, but throughput limits at scale are the thing the README doesn't address.

Most of the thread is the Omnara authors (kmansm27, cmsparks, isehgal) answering questions. The independent content comes from commenters placing the design in a wider trend, and from a few pointed questions about where it actually fits.

**Is durable-by-default now the norm?** Swyx linked a related talk, "Log is Agent", and several people said the industry is converging on this. asherdale1 listed pi-durable, Cloudflare agents on Durable Objects, and Vercel's durable harness. kmansm27 argued that durability must be native to the harness. He called bolted-on versions like Vercel's "neutered", because they drop whatever can't be reliably made durable. hobofan was skeptical that this is novel at all, since a DB-backed chat history is how most people build harnesses. cmsparks replied that this is a recent shift: last year Claude, Codex, and others stored everything in one big JSON file on disk.

**"Use what you know."** naribe made the most deflationary point. Durable agents plus distributed tools are just the event-driven architecture we already have. He builds cloud agents with on-prem or at-home services that listen for work, which avoids unstable infra and avoids opening inbound ports. The same thing can be done with Lambda Durable Functions (`waitForCallback`) or Step Functions (`waitForTaskToken`), or any compute pool with a queue and persistent state. So you don't strictly need a harness to build an agent.

**Why separate the loop from the machines?** aquark questioned the premise: the loop mostly waits on the model, and the stateful machines running commands are the real constraint, so why pool loop workers? kmansm27 agreed that one worker can run thousands of agents. The split exists because machines are the expensive part after tokens, and most harnesses tie one VM to each agent for its whole life. Decoupling lets an agent use zero, one, or many machines, and a machine can sleep without stopping the agent.

The catch is that this puts a third party in the hot path of every agent API call, so latency (the demo shows 142ms) and availability become your problem. SOC 2 is still "in progress," which matters for a product whose pitch is passing security reviews. A planned LLM gateway with guardrails is "coming soon." The category is real, though: if you've hand-rolled per-user token handling for agents, this is the plumbing you were building.

Most of this thread is founders answering pointed questions, and the answers show where the design is strong and where it's still thin. The last comment (jeswin) is cut off mid-sentence, so there's nothing to report from it.

Kevan (a founder) says the model layer is assumed compromisable, so everything is enforced at the proxy, which is the only holder of the credential. An injected agent can't route around it because it never sees the key. For irreversible actions he stacks three controls:
- Method and path allowlists, so a DELETE never reaches the provider.
- Conditional human approval, where the request is frozen server-side so the payload can't be swapped after the yes.
- Audit logs that include denials.

He also argues that a proxy can give fine-grained read-only access even when the provider only offers full-power keys. snarkytables asked what happens if the agent can bypass the proxy. His agents generate Terraform-style plans, so he can't filter at the proxy and instead uses a compliance subagent plus OPA rules and human approval for high-impact changes. Kevan warned that an LLM reviewer reads the same untrusted context and can be injected too. Alter treats its AI review as advisory: it can escalate to a human but can't deny or allow anything on its own. He added that policies are tested and applied like code, with `policy test`, `simulate`, and a Terraform-style `plan`/`apply`. Luis agreed and is moving his checks toward deterministic OPA.

**Money and retries.** Per-action caps and conditional approval (for example, require approval when amount > 1000) work today. A rolling daily spend budget doesn't exist yet, only call-count quotas. On retries, approved calls run at most once, and a re-sent call creates a new approval. If a POST might have reached the provider, such as after a timeout, it is marked "outcome unknown" rather than resent.

**Memory leakage between users.** souvik1997 raised the case where a CEO's session writes sensitive data to agent memory and a contractor later reads it. Alter says it partitions memory by end-user plus agent identity, and the Substrate founder (ptkachykby) described a similar tag-and-filter approach.

The post also catalogs how malicious code gets into a Python GPU workload, with real cases:
- **Imports:** the April 2026 `lightning` PyPI compromise (CVE-2026-44484) and the 2022 torchtriton dependency-confusion attack

- **Pickled models:** Hugging Face files that opened reverse shells on load
- **Runtime strings:** an Open WebUI plugin hiding a payload behind 64 layers of decoding and `exec()`

### Introducing Clairic, a new System One class of extraction models

#### [Submission URL](https://blog.cloudflare.com/clairic-extraction-model/) | 69 points | by [shishy](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=shishy) | [7 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50155907)

**Clairic answers extraction questions in a single forward pass with no output tokens**, so you pay only for input and get typed values back, usually in under a second. It's hosted on Workers AI as `@cf/cloudflare/clairic`, in preview, with open weights promised "soon" but undated.

It extends the "System One" idea TypeScript-adjacent vendor TypeSafe introduced a month ago with its Jev model. Jev and Cloudflare's Clef are decision models: you list the options and they return a probability for each. Clairic covers the case where you can't enumerate the answers in advance, such as an invoice total, a delivery date, days late, a repair cost, or the next 24 hours of traffic.

You send text (plus up to four optional images) and a map of named, typed fields, each with optional plain-language instructions. Each field comes back as one of four kinds of value:

- **Extracted:** a span copied from the input, with character offsets and a confidence score.
- **Computed:** numbers, dates, times, durations and lists read or derived from the input, following their type's rules (two decimals for cents).
- **Estimated:** values the input only implies, returned as a distribution within bounds you declare, with a median and an 80% interval.
- **Forecasted:** future steps of a series, each with its own 80% interval.

Because nothing is generated token by token, every answer is a valid value of the declared type or `null`. Cloudflare says that rules out mistyped output and LLM-style hallucination, though the model can still be wrong, which is what the confidence scores are for.

The claim of "significantly higher accuracy, faster latency, and cheaper cost than traditional LLMs" comes with no numbers in the text provided. Treat it as unverified until benchmarks or the weights appear. The structured-output contract is the real draw: no JSON parsing or retry loops, and calibrated intervals instead of a bare guess.

Cloudflare's pitch is a pipeline: extraction models turn raw input into typed values, decision models turn values into choices, and LLMs turn decisions into words.

### Harness2 by Google Research – Recursive Agent Harnessing

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/google-research/harness2) | 77 points | by [irthomasthomas](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=irthomasthomas) | [18 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50151059)

**Harness² tunes the agent's scaffolding, not its weights.** It rewrites the prompt, tools, memory, and other components around a frozen model at inference time, with no ground-truth feedback. Google Research's repo reports a single step lifting GLM-5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash over their base harnesses: up to **11.5 points on WorkBuddy-Bench**, 4.5 on JobBench, and 11.8 percentage points on LAB's partial pass rate.

The mechanism runs on two recursion levels:

- **Task-level:** a contrastive Propose–Probe–Compose step proposes edits, runs them on the same task, and composes a refined harness from the behavioral differences between executions.
- **Harnessing-level:** a persistent improvement harness keeps the adopted harnesses and evidence-backed lessons from each task, so later tasks start with better guidance.

Eight components are editable: system prompt, guardrails, memory, tool guidance, subagents, skills, scripts, and plugins. The `--k` flag sets the task-level budget. Parallel mode probes K general and K domain-specific candidates and widens the search, while sequential mode chains K refinements and goes deeper. The README says more budget brings further gains, though it gives no figures for that.

**Setup:**
- Needs Linux, Python 3.12, and uv.
- `harness2-demo` runs both recursion modes with deterministic editors and needs no credentials.
- Real runs use OpenCode (default) or Codex executors, Vertex AI model configs, and per-benchmark dependencies: Pandoc and Poppler for LAB, Docker for WorkBuddy-Bench.
- Model-backed runs cost API money, so start with `--max-tasks 1`.

The caveat is in the repo's own warning. Executor network access is unrestricted and Codex runs with its sandbox off. Public benchmark rubrics may be online, so you have to lock down the network yourself or a run can find the answers and grading criteria. The repo has only three commits, and the headline numbers come from the authors' benchmarks, so treat this as a research release, not a hardened tool.
