The author's case is that Google reversed its 2023 promise that Workspace-extension content "is not seen by human reviewers." The evidence is Google Help Answer 16836988 and an October 16 edit to the 2023 blog post. Both warn that reviewers "may just be the email or file" and tell users not to connect apps containing confidential information.

The mechanism is ambient tool-calling. Mentioning a trip, a bill, or a name in casual chat can trigger a background lookup. Because that's probabilistic, a single test can land in one of four states:

- **Zero retrieval**, with no personal data pulled.
- **Metadata or summary**, such as "you have 3 emails about your flight."
- **Partial extraction**, meaning a 2–6 sentence verbatim quote, often with PII.
- **Full-file ingestion**, the entire email including headers.

**Reproduction steps:**
- Enable Connected Apps in consumer Gemini.
- Select Flash with High Thinking or better.
- Ask something casual about a topic in your inbox, with no @ tags. The author used "I wonder if I'm paying too much for internet service" against an ISP bill.
- Prompt Gemini to print the exact text the retrieval tool returned.
- Delete the conversation between trials so history can't answer for it.

The author reports seeing all four outcomes, including account and confirmation numbers.

The caveats are real. This is one person's testing, with no trial counts, and it shows what the model *retrieves*. It doesn't show how often those sessions actually reach a reviewer. That link rests on Google's own help text, and the claim that its regex and NER scrubbers can't de-identify narrative data is the author's assertion. The practical takeaway is Google's own warning: if Connected Apps is on, treat anything in Gmail or Drive as potentially readable by a contractor.

**It's an agent skill that scrubs AI tells in a first pass, then rewrites what's left into short, active-voice sentences.** The thesis: most humanizers only delete phrases like "Great question!" and "seamless", leaving long, hedge-stacked sentences that still read like a model wrote them.

The second pass borrows from Karpathy's tip to ask an LLM to explain things in "80% of the way to ASD-STE100", the controlled English of aircraft maintenance manuals. Pass 1 applies 26 tells from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" (via blader/humanizer's catalog). Pass 2 applies 15 structure rules: one idea per sentence, active voice, simple tenses, and instructions kept apart from descriptions.

The "80%" is a deliberate loosening. Strict STE caps sentences at 20 words for instructions and 25 for descriptions, uses only about 900 approved words, and bans "may" and "should". The default mode keeps the structure but allows plain common words, "check" and "test" as verbs, "may" and "should" for real uncertainty, and contractions in casual text. The README's worked example turns a Kubernetes liveness-probe paragraph with an opener, em dash, and "could potentially" into six short sentences that keep the technical content, including `initialDelaySeconds` and `failureThreshold`.

- **Install:** `npx skills add Dodaios/ste80-humanizer`, with Claude and Cursor plugin manifests in the repo. It runs as `/ste80-humanizer` or triggers when you ask to humanize, simplify, or de-AI text.
- **Traceable edits:** it reports changes by rule number (e.g. "§1, §8, S2"), so you can audit each one against the reference files.
- **Scope:** the README says the goal is readability, not beating AI detectors.

It's a prompt bundle, not a model or classifier, with 8 commits on the main branch. Expect results to depend on the host LLM following the rules. The tradeoff is that STE-style output is clear for docs and explanations but flattens voice, so it suits technical writing better than essays.

Azure's own A100 PCIe 80GB runs $3.67 at one GPU per VM and $4.78 at two or four.

The author spells out what the cheap row gives up:
- **InfiniBand.** Azure's ND SKU includes 400 Gbps per GPU of Quantum-2 with GPU Direct RDMA, which multi-node training needs. It's why ND costs more than Azure's single-H100 NC SKU at $6.98.
- **An SLA.** RunPod Community is third-party hosts renting out their own hardware.
- **Enterprise extras.** That means compliance and committed capacity.

So the practical read is that a single-node fine-tune is overpaying for an interconnect it won't use, while a 64-node run can't consider the cheap row at all.

The caveats cut against the headline. These are on-demand list prices only, and reserved, committed-use and spot pricing would all lower the hyperscaler numbers. Spot would narrow the gap a lot. Each cloud is a single region (us-east-1, eastus). GPU counts come from vendor docs rather than price labels. Availability isn't priced, and a cheap H100 you can't get isn't a price. Lambda is excluded from the headline because its published rates take effect 2026-11-16, with current rates behind a login.

The repo is reproducible. `collect.py` pulls the AWS Price List and Azure Retail Prices APIs without auth, though the AWS index is about 1 GB. Neocloud prices are hand-transcribed into `vendors.json` with source URLs and read dates, which makes that half the weakest link.

The thread skipped the pricing method and fought over one question: is the cheap row safe for your data? It began with a warning that hosts on RunPod Community and Vast peek inside VMs, and it split into three arguments.

**Does secrecy matter for your workload?** Several commenters said many jobs don't need it. Examples were fine-tuning a strawberry-picking model, public datasets, and models so tied to one product's inputs and outputs that a competitor couldn't use them. One person pointed out that a 1/5 price can justify being watched, as with trading Onshape privacy for a free license. Counterpoints:
- Privacy should be the default.
- The training data can be sensitive even when the model isn't, such as unredacted support tickets.
- Pre-extracting features offline doesn't reliably hide inputs, because latents are often built to let you recover them.

**Would trade secret law protect you?** This got heated. Some said trade secrets sit outside enforcement. Others cited the DTSA, state statutes like CUTSA, and the Levandowski/Uber case, and linked the civil and criminal statutes. A separate camp said this misses the point: the law requires "reasonable measures" to keep something secret. Putting it on a host with no confidentiality agreement may forfeit the claim, much as Coca-Cola couldn't leaflet its recipe and still call it secret. Someone noted that a competitor in China wouldn't be deterred anyway, and another asked how likely that is. Several commenters flagged that they weren't lawyers.

**Are the big clouds actually safer?** One commenter argued the risk is higher with large operators like Amazon or OpenAI, citing Amazon Basics and AWS's use of open-source projects. Another replied that big firms are the least likely to snoop on corporate customers. They have the most to lose, a whistleblower would leak it, and controls must be designed in. Pushback:

A commenter who works at a big cloud said peeking doesn't happen routinely and that safeguards keep growing. Another replied that "it definitely does happen" occasionally.

Smaller points:

The thread never settled where the line sits between "cheap and watched" and "expensive and trusted." Most agreed it depends on whether the data or the model is the sensitive part.

### USB: A trust layer for AI skills and MCP servers

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/PeepSick/usbplus) | 11 points | by [peepsick](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=peepsick) | [3 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50432661)

**USB pairs a skill/MCP catalog with a verifier that checks whether a skill does what its description says.** The catalog side is an installer (`npm install -g @peepsick/usb-cli`) that auto-detects your AI runtimes. It currently lists 529 skills across 16 targets: 4 native installs (Claude Code skills, a Cursor `.mdc` rule) and 11 portable bundles.

The trust layer is the interesting part, and it's two commands:

- **`usb secscan`** statically reads each skill's declared name and description, then scans the body for capabilities it never mentions: credential access, piping network responses into a shell, destructive deletes, process execution, persistence hooks. Findings come with file:line evidence and CI exit codes. A baseline mode (`--write-baseline`, `--baseline`) fails only on new findings, so you can adopt it on a messy repo. It never executes what it scans, and it can fetch a GitHub repo read-only with `--repo`.
- **`usb mcprobe`** launches an MCP server with explicit consent and observes what it does at startup, comparing that to the README's claims about `tools/list`. It never calls a tool.

The install flow also swaps blind `curl | bash` for download, sha256 verify, read, run.

The catch: secscan is a static, declared-vs-observed check, so it catches undeclared capabilities, not malicious ones that are plainly declared or cleverly obfuscated. mcprobe only sees startup behavior, not what a tool does when invoked. Both are useful as a CI gate, but neither is a sandbox. MIT-licensed, v1.0.8, and it stays backward compatible with the older Universal Skill Bridge (v0.4.x) commands.

The thread is thin: two critical replies and a note from the author.

- **Limits of verification:** One commenter says the deeper problem is that AI produces so much text that people don't read what a skill claims, and most install without reading anything. On this view, checking a skill against its claims is a good idea, but it only helps if someone reads the claims or the check runs automatically.
- **Naming:** Another commenter objects to "USB" as a name because it is hard to search for and hard to talk about. They offer no alternative.

The author's own comment only restates the motivation (skills that are easy to install but hard to inspect) and doesn't answer either point.
