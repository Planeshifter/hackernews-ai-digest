### AI Chip Architectures

#### [Submission URL](https://www.jepeake.com/ai-chip-architectures) | 135 points | by [Finbarr](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=Finbarr) | [44 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49405657)

**The central constraint is moving data fast enough to feed matmuls — the memory wall —** so the post evaluates each AI chip by four concrete questions: where data lives, how it moves to compute, what the compute units look like, and how chips interconnect at scale.

- Training and prefill: many tokens are stacked against the same weights, turning layers into large GEMMs that are compute-bound with high arithmetic intensity.
- Decode: one token per step collapses matmuls into GEMVs; every step rereads all weights plus the KV cache, slashing arithmetic intensity and making long-context inference bandwidth- (often KV-) bound. Continuous batching, speculative decoding, and multi-token prediction recover intensity by turning GEMVs back into GEMMs and pushing up Ops/byte, but each request still hits its own KV.

From Hennessy and Patterson’s DSA thesis (TPU v1: 29× CPU throughput, 80× energy efficiency) to today’s landscape, the survey tallies what’s actually deployed: GPUs (NVIDIA leads; AMD follows with 6 GW commitments from OpenAI and Meta), systolic-array accelerators (TPUs train Gemini and target up to a million chips for Anthropic; Anthropic also runs Claude on over a million Trainium chips), Cerebras serving OpenAI inference, and the Groq LPU folded into NVIDIA via a $20B acquihire.

On GPUs, the throughline is a stable CUDA model layered with ever-richer primitives: massively parallel SMs, deep memory hierarchies, and per-generation adds like Tensor Cores (Volta onward), TF32/FP8/FP4 and Transformer Engine, NVLink evolutions, HBM3/4, MIG, chiplet packaging, and scale-out racks — all aimed at keeping highly parallel matmul engines fed without breaking the programming model. The broader takeaway: each architecture is a different bet on beating data movement, not just adding FLOPs.

The discussion pivots from the sheer scale of current AI chips to whether the underlying digital paradigm is a dead end, sparking a debate over power efficiency versus brute-force economics.

- **Analog and Neuromorphic Alternatives:** Frustrated by massive power footprints and the inherent inefficiency of digital floating-point multiplication, commenters pointed to experimental hardware to break the memory wall—in-memory analog computing (RRAM, FE-FETs), clockless asynchronous CPUs like SpiNNaker, and pulse-based race logic. Several argued that approaching the human brain's 20-watt envelope requires abandoning synchronous digital arrays entirely.
- **The Brute-Force Feedback Loop:** Skeptics countered that novel architectures cannot overcome the sheer capital momentum of GPUs. They noted that analog hardware struggles with the signal fidelity required for training and often requires massive wafer area to reach parity. More fundamentally, commenters pointed out that current LLM architectures were chosen specifically *because* they map so well to scalable, brute-force digital hardware, creating a lock-in effect that sidelines more elegant hardware designs.
- **Formatting Distractions:** A significant portion of the thread abandoned the hardware discussion to critique the article's presentation. Readers heavily criticized the excessive use of italics and thin fonts, with many dismissing the piece as AI-generated, though defenders pushed back to call it an exceptionally accurate technical primer regardless of its authorship.

Highlights from his agent.md playbook:
- **Be terse, no fluff**: minimal words, no praise—“cold hard truth.”
- **No magic numbers/strings**: extract to consts/enums; always constant-ize spec values.
- **Flatten code**: prefer early returns/continues; reduce indentation; always use {}.
- **Naming and params**: keep function names under 30 chars; use enums instead of boolean params.
- **Readable flow**: whitespace between logical blocks; short comments explain what/why; ASCII diagrams for systems.
- **Encapsulation first**: default everything to private; treat visibility changes as breaking and ask for approval.
- **Architecture boundaries**: enforce strict layering; never punch through intermediate services/drivers.
- **Minimize diffs**: don’t touch unrelated code or add drive-by comments.
- **Commit messages (7 rules)**: blank line after subject; ~50-char imperative, capitalized subject with no trailing period; wrap body at 72; body explains what/why, not how.
- **Bug fixes via tests first**: write failing test, then fix, then verify pass.

It’s not a magic bullet—LLMs still hallucinate and require verification—but this shifts review effort to architecture and design rather than cleanup. To combat “context dilution” (Lost in the Middle), keep sessions short per feature and explicitly reload the file—“Reload agent.md” is enough—to keep these constraints salient.

### Training AI to Paint with Code

#### [Submission URL](https://surya.website/rling-qwen-to-paint-with-code) | 37 points | by [Tiberium](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=Tiberium) | [3 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49411800)

**By replacing absolute 0–10 scores with pairwise judgments against a hand‑rated reference pool, the authors broke a 0.65‑reward plateau and compressed generated code from ~13.5k to <2k tokens.** A language model (Qwen 3.5 35B) writes full p5.brush JavaScript sketches from a text prompt; the sketch renders to PNG in a sandboxed Puppeteer environment; a judge compares it to two randomly sampled references from a curated set; GRPO applies the reward update and the loop repeats.

The original nine‑signal rubric (compile/use‑brush gate, code‑length ramp, HPSv3, prompt adherence via a GPT‑5.4/Gemini “council,” and four quality judges) overfit to what it double‑counted: the five “quality” signals were 0.85–0.95 correlated, the length ramp saturated by step ~30, and HPSv3—the only signal with real variance—was underweighted (0.10). Rollouts converged to the same flat, five‑petal clip‑art flower while the scalar reward crept up.

They fixed both the question and the ground truth:
- Built a 1,664‑image pool rated love/okay/nope; the 117 love‑tier works seeded comparisons.
- Switched to pairwise wins against two sampled references per rollout to expand dynamic range and make the judge’s task concrete.
- Collapsed the rubric to four parts with explicit weights: compile+uses‑brush gate (0.05), length check (0.05), HPSv3 (0.30), pairwise vs. the reference pool (0.60).

With the same base model and data, training hit the old plateau ~3× faster, then kept improving; the model “learned” that strong compositions don’t require verbose code. Because the artifact is code, outputs are directly editable instead of re‑prompting, and the work surfaces a broader RL lesson for creative tasks: relative judgments anchored to a curated standard provide signal where absolute, overlapping metrics collapse. The next step they outline is a small reward model trained on the ratings to apply this aesthetic sense without explicit comparisons each time.
