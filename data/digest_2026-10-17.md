**The Codex Desktop macOS app is silently filling disks with Crashpad minidumps**: the reporter saw 4.9GB and 54,504 files pile up in one day under `~/Library/Application Support/com.openai.codex/web/Crashpad/pending`, all while the app stayed fully usable. Nothing visibly crashes, so there's no warning.

The pattern is paired `.dmp` and `_sidecar.json` files (27,252 of each) that keep appearing during investigation, with no upload, rotation, or cap. The reporter deleted the backlog, which dropped the directory to 6.4MB and 70 files, but new pairs showed up immediately. That rules out stale leftovers: something is still crashing or being reported as crashing, continuously. The root cause isn't identified. Two `browser_crashpad_handler` processes, both orphaned under PPID 1 since May 30 and pointed at the same database, are a suspect, but that's an observation rather than a diagnosis.

Other users confirm it, and one reports **59GB** of dumps, with the process still trying to write after hitting the 65,535-file filesystem limit. The issue is open, labeled bug/performance/app, and the version affected is 26.527.60818 (Electron/Chromium 148.0.7778.179) on macOS 26.3.1, arm64. It links to earlier reports of stale Crashpad helpers surviving updates.

If you run the app, check that directory now. Clearing it is a workaround that buys time, not a fix. The reporter withheld the dumps publicly because they can contain memory contents and local paths, which is worth remembering before attaching any to a bug report.

The unresolved question is whether better models outrun the compounding mess or whether structure and human architectural judgment stay necessary.

### The math that OpenAI keeps for itself

#### [Submission URL](https://newsletter.danielpaleka.com/p/the-math-that-openai-keeps-for-itself) | 52 points | by [dpaleka](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=dpaleka) | [19 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50134211)

**Statistics, ML theory, optimization, and numerical analysis make up about 20% of arXiv theorems but under 1% of OpenAI's 700+ new math results**, according to Daniel Paleka's analysis. He pulled the numbers with a coding agent and a Semantic Scholar API key, after commentators noticed cryptography was missing too.

The obvious rebuttal is that those fields just lack open problems. Paleka checked: restricting the baseline to arXiv papers that solve open problems shrinks the gap but leaves it statistically significant. He also dismisses the idea that OpenAI doesn't know the problems, pointing to Sebastien Bubeck's 2025 gpt-5-pro result on a convex optimization bound. His preferred explanation is that these are the fields closest to what labs would use internally, so every lab has reason to withhold them. It's an inference from the pattern rather than anything OpenAI has said.

The more interesting argument is the forward-looking one. Paleka expects mathematical capability to feed back into LLM training, and says math is the domain where the usual bottlenecks don't apply: synthetic data is easy, verification is cheap, curriculum learning is trivial. In his words, "compute in, math out." The one result that would have interested his 2020 self is Max-Cut's 0.878 inapproximability under P != NP, and that falls out of a Unique Games Conjecture proof in the same batch.

The post is written as a call to rethink what's worth working on. Proving deep-learning theorems is still not worth it, he says, but asking questions about this shift now is. The framing is partly personal, since he left mathematics around 2020 because of AI.

The thread mostly argues over one question: would OpenAI really sit on results in the withheld fields? A smaller side-debate asks whether the math matters at all.

**Is withholding plausible?** The top comment says no. Anything a prompt can prove, someone else will find, and OpenAI needs the publicity (Wall Street "street cred"), so nobody is hiding secrets. Replies push back from several directions:
- **Capability:** dmitriy_ko and satvikpendem say the proofs came from models and compute the public can't access. satvikpendem adds that labs distill internal large models into the public ones. quicklywilliam notes that even governments lack access, and worries about what happens if the jagged frontier moves from theory into the physical world.
- **Incentives:** nairboon compares the dollar value of publicity against an optimization trick that cuts training or inference costs by millions. testerius makes the general argument that a company with a profitable tool keeps it quiet. omgwtfbyobbq agrees, but only where profitable applications exist.

**Cryptography.** Several commenters read the omission as deliberate, with different explanations:
- DonsDiscountGas thinks any published result could help bad actors more than good ones, so labs stay away.
- qnleigh guesses labs have either tried LLM attacks internally or are barred from trying, and that the US government will get first access before any release.
- asanineassasin speculates that public-key cryptography may already be broken, citing the old "export" controls. They later ask whether parallel construction would be used to hide it. MisterMunchkin says "the NSA won't give you that choice." All of this is speculation; nobody offers evidence.

**Does the math matter?** vitorsr quotes Hugo Duminil-Copin's view that only the mathematicians involved are changed by a big proof, because human understanding is what benefits society. bemtevi, a former mathematician, argues that decades of deep abstraction (geometric Langlands, higher topos theory) haven't improved lives, and that AI-driven abstraction is "just a mental game" that wastes energy. Replies split. cultofmetatron points out that number theory had no applications for centuries before it underpinned modern cryptography, so usefulness can't be judged in advance. dieselgate says the "better future" framing ignores people without clean water or electricity.

A few comments were off-topic: complaints about Claude-style site design and AI-slop prose, a skeptical aside that the work might be brute-forceable with compute, and a Trump joke.

### Show HN: Llmspeed – feel how fast a local LLM runs before you buy the hardware

#### [Submission URL](https://llmspeed.dev/) | 316 points | by [albinstman](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=albinstman) | [86 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50127979)

**Llmspeed lets you pick a GPU, Mac, or APU and a model, then shows how fast generation would feel**, so you can sanity-check a purchase before spending four figures on VRAM.

The hardware list is broad: everything from an RTX 3060 12GB and a used Tesla V100 or MI50, through RTX 4090/5090 and Radeon cards, to Strix Halo boxes, DGX Spark, RTX PRO 6000, M-series Macs (up to a 512GB Mac Studio), a CPU-only Ryzen 9950X build, and cloud H100/H200 for reference. Models run from Llama 3.1 8B and Qwen 3.5 9B up to GPT-OSS 120B, Gemma 4, GLM-5.3, and DeepSeek V4 Flash.

The honesty caveat is in the fine print. The page says estimates are for a short prompt on a single device. Some numbers are measured, pulled from community benchmarks like r/LocalLLaMA and localscore.ai, while others are derived from similar setups or projected from specs. Projected figures for hardware that barely exists are guesses, so check which cells are measured before trusting them. Long-context prefill, which is where Macs and CPU builds tend to hurt, is outside what a short-prompt estimate shows.

The project is open source on GitHub. The provided text doesn't spell out how the "feel" is rendered, so the exact interaction is unclear from the page copy alone.

The thread's main argument is that calculators like this undersell what old or modest hardware can do, especially with MoE models. The commenters also swapped practical tuning advice.

**The "benchmarks understate local" camp.** The top commenter runs a 4-bit fine-tuned Qwen 3.5-35B-A3B on a 9-year-old laptop (GTX 1070 Max-Q 8GB, 16GB RAM). He uses a llama.cpp fork with TurboQuant KV-cache quantization and gets 131k context at 16-19 tok/s generation and 350-500 tok/s prefill, dropping to about 9 tok/s past 100k context. His claim is that a frontier LLM or a tool like this would tell him to run a "crappy 9B." Another commenter backed this up: MoE models with few active parameters are faster and easier to run, and "behave a lot more like their peak size than the active parameter size." Their example was a large Qwen coder MoE on a 3080 10GB with 32GB RAM at 40-50 tok/s with 65k context. For MoE, system RAM caps the quantization you can use. One commenter reported that the tool "won't run" a model on an RTX 4080 that he runs fine, and called the numbers "pure slop." Another pointed out that the tool defaults to tiny quants, which cuts against the "too pessimistic" complaint.

**Dense vs. MoE for coding.** A newcomer asked whether MoE really matches dense models on coding. The answers:
- Dense models give better results but are out of reach for most hardware, and MoE generates far faster.
- Parameter count and architecture predict capability poorly. One commenter said a ~125B Qwen "Flash Next" with 6B active beats the 27B dense model, while a much larger Meta MoE doesn't reach it.

**A correction on "A3B."** One commenter said A3B means each expert is 3B parameters, so a 4-bit quant needs about 1.5GB per expert. Another corrected this: A3B is the total active parameters per forward pass, including shared weights, attention and embeddings. Individual experts are tiny (about 3M each, with 8 routed plus 1 shared per layer), and only around 1.1B of the 3B active are expert weights. The original poster added that fit-in-VRAM arithmetic misses why a 35B-A3B is usable on Pascal now when it wasn't a year ago. He credits engine and fork improvements.

**Where the limits show up.**
- One user with a 12GB RX 6700 XT saw the agent loop on an open-ended "what would you improve" prompt with a 16k context. Others told him agent work needs at least 32k, ideally 65k-100k+. Dropping quantization or reasoning effort frees context. Whole-project review is a poor fit for local models, since even 1M-context frontier models can't take in a whole project. Work file by file instead.
- Users of very small cards (GTX 750 Ti 2GB, 8GB cards) said small models write bad code and that free-tier Gemini or ChatGPT still beats anything they can run. The suggestions were Gemma 4 E2B/E4B or Qwen 3.5-9B, a check that CUDA is actually in use, and OpenCode Zen's free models. One commenter's rule of thumb is that MoE models under about 19B parameters disappoint on coding even at Q4.
- A suggestion for a 6GB-card owner was to use the iGPU too. Run a Vulkan llama.cpp build for small MoE models on the iGPU and a CUDA build for dense models on the discrete GPU. The commenter said quantized models still disappointed him for coding but worked well for NLP and speech tasks.

The open question is whether any calculator can capture the engine, fork, quant and context choices that make people's real results diverge from the tool's numbers.
