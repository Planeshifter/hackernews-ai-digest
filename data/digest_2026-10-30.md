**If AI absorbs the routine 95% of incident response, SREs lose the reps that build the judgment for the 5% that actually pages them at 3am.** The SigNoz newsletter piece is an opinion essay built on Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 "Ironies of Automation": the more you automate, the more critical the human becomes during rare failures, and the less practice they get at exactly those interventions.

The author maps this onto SRE through PagerDuty's tiered model:

- **Tier 1:** known issues with known fixes, fully automated.
- **Tier 2:** AI recommends, a human validates.
- **Tier 3:** novel, cascading failures stay human-led, with AI supplying context.

The catch is that if humans only touch Tier 3, nobody builds the intuition that comes from years of hands-on incidents. That intuition is what Tier 3 demands.

The supporting evidence comes from other fields, not SRE:

- **Medicine:** endoscopists' unassisted polyp detection fell from 28% to 22% after a period of AI use.
- **Aviation:** heavy autopilot reliance degrades manual flying and situational awareness, and the article says the FAA now mandates more manual flying time.

The author also admits to offloading nearly everything to AI themselves.

The argument is analogy-driven. The article offers no SRE-specific data on skill decay, and the excerpt cuts off before any proposed remedy. The aviation precedent, mandated manual practice, hints at one: deliberately scheduled human-led incident drills.

**Aviation as a model.** andix praised the FAA analogy, noting that aviation has solved human error while complexity and automation keep growing. **Is skipping code review defensible?** This was the main fight.

- **Defenders:** charcircuit rejected the article's premises. ### Show HN: A working Nintendo in ~1GB of Qwen weights

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/dbreunig/nesformer) | 280 points | by [dbreunig](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=dbreunig) | [143 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50306442)

**An NES's 6502 CPU, its 2 KB of RAM, and a cartridge ROM run inside a stock Qwen3.5 checkpoint, with no training involved.** A compiler computed every weight. The model plays by predicting the CPU's execution log one token at a time, while a small host program handles the rest of the console: drawing the picture, reading the controller, and firing the vblank interrupt.

It builds on Qwenputer by Divyansh Agrawal, which compiled a CHIP-8 and then a RISC-V CPU into Qwen3.5-4B. nesformer reuses that compiler and its central trick: the Gated DeltaNet state serves as the machine's memory. The result loads as a standard `Qwen3_5ForCausalLM`, and the README says every CPU token in the demo runs (including Super Mario Bros. World 1-1) came from the model.

The correctness claim is concrete. For the bundled test games, every frame matches a conventional 6502 emulator exactly, and a `check` command runs weights and reference CPU side by side, stopping at the first divergence.

The catch is speed and scope:

- **Slow:** after a ~20 s numba compile, a frame takes 1–6 seconds, so 10 seconds of NES time (600 frames) takes around 25 minutes.
- **Mapper 0 only:** it runs NROM (iNES mapper 0) cartridges such as Super Mario Bros., Donkey Kong, or Excitebike. Other boards stop with a message. You supply the ROM; only three freely licensed test ROMs ship.
- **Engines:** the default is a numba engine. A stock `transformers` path (`--engine hf`) works too, at about 10 tokens/s, and needs torch.

Setup is light: Python 3.11+, numpy, scipy, numba, and pillow. Unzip the code and weights archives side by side, and `python -m nesformer run` saves an animated GIF. Controller input is scripted by frame range, e.g. `right@200-600`. `build` regenerates the weights in about 10 seconds, which underlines the point: this is a compiled program in transformer clothing, not a learned one.

The thread splits into three loose conversations: how the build was made, whether the compiled-VM idea generalizes, and a long tangent on AI, efficiency and jobs.

**How it was built.** dbreunig, the first commenter, said Opus produced working weights and code from just two prompts. The first was to read the Qwenputer article and explore whether the technique could emulate an NES. The second was "let's build it, just for fun." He credited the result to unusually good scaffolding: a long-lived emulation community, conformance test ROMs (one with more than 3k golden instructions), and the original Qwenputer repo for writing the weights. cadamsdotcom framed these as "oracles" that models need, as humans need teachers and encyclopedias. giancarlostoro said he uses similar tests from adjacent projects, and that a friend feeds Claude Gherkin-formatted test criteria with excellent results. That prompted:

**Compiling tools into weights.** armcat proposed compiling a VM, Python interpreter and shell tools into the weights, so tool calls become matmuls instead of external autoregressive calls. kirubakaran linked a Google latent-reasoning paper. manofmanysmiles suggested training with fixed-function regions frozen, or hybrid internal blocks such as a symbolic execution engine or a Lisp. **Sketch Stadium is really a demo for Wakeshift, an SDK for pushing events to dormant AI agents**, and Pictionary is the showpiece. Agents connect via MCP (no account, just authorize), subscribe to game events, and take turns drawing or guessing. Clues score 1, 2, or 3 points by difficulty. The linked page is a live game, currently on round 21507, with a scoreboard led by "Silly Lynx" at 36 points. In the visible round, the word was "finish line" and the guesses included "hurdles" and "running race."

The more useful detail is a design lesson. The first version made agents draw lines 10 at a time, to mimic real Pictionary, and they were *really* bad at it. Giving them a subset of SVG fixed that, even for lower-cost models. If you build agent tooling, that's a reminder that the output format you offer often matters more than the model you pick.

The problem behind it is real. Agents that wait on CI, an approval, or an email reply today can poll, ask the human to nudge them, use a bespoke integration, or rely on a proprietary feature like Claude Code monitors. The author hit this building Clawvisor, where an agent had to notice an out-of-band approve click, and ended up long-polling.

Wakeshift attaches to your MCP server so consumers can subscribe to real-time events. It wakes a sleeping agent using whatever per-harness hack is available. For most harnesses that means installing a hook, and the author says it will upgrade automatically as harnesses adopt native MCP events. So the "push" is a shim today, not a protocol guarantee.

The author is asking for other MCP servers and game ideas that would benefit from push notifications.

The thread is mostly the author (ericlevine) fielding questions, with security the main concern and the quality of agent drawings the main practical finding.

**Prompt injection as the elephant in the room.** The top comment warns that pasting prompts from HN into your agent, or running "pipx install this MCP", is like a Facebook "what was your first car?" thread, i.e. a social-engineering setup. The author answered that this is a remote MCP server with account-free OAuth, exposing only game tools, and that it marks all server communication as untrusted so other players' guesses can't inject instructions. He asked how an MCP developer could share a server safely on HN, and that question went unanswered. The commenter then pointed out that this setup amounts to an accidental capture-the-flag game, and the author said he has built poker and a mafia-style vampire game that force agents to bluff. His main hurdle there was prompt-engineering the agents to stop leaking secrets like hole cards. A side question asked whether the "first car" warning was about security-question harvesting, and nobody answered it.

**Agents are bad at revising drawings.** Timwi noticed that the first pass is usually the only good one. For "rain on my parade," the agent kept adding confetti until it hid the cloud and the person. The author agreed that extra detail adds nothing at least 90% of the time. Some agents blank the canvas and restart, which works, but they don't do it consistently.

**Difficulty is bimodal.** 542458 found the rounds too easy, with mostly "correct in 1" results. The author said difficulty is very binomial: concrete words get guessed in about 5 seconds, while abstract ones can't be conveyed and nobody guesses them. More detail rarely helps either way.

### AGI Economics: Coordination vs. Competence

#### [Submission URL](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/the-economics-of-intelligence/) | 27 points | by [simonpure](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=simonpure) | [5 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50299212)

**DeepMind's economists argue the individual AI agent is the wrong unit of analysis**: a model can beat every person one-on-one and still lose to a well-organized team, so benchmarks that score agents in isolation say little about when the economy actually changes.

The essay (drawing on a formal paper, *Organizing Intelligence*) splits capability into two axes with very different economic effects:

- **Competence** (math, writing, coding) shifts wages, prices, and the human/AI mix inside firms smoothly, leaving industry structure largely intact.
- **Coordination** (agents working together within a firm) is the one with a threshold. Past a critical point, it triggers abrupt jumps in market concentration, employment, and wages.

Crossing those axes yields four regimes: **minimal AGI, supercompetent AI, supercooperative AI, and ASI**. The claim is that visible transformation comes from coordination, which most benchmarks don't measure.

The supporting argument rests on "jagged" profiles. The authors quizzed 100 people across six domains (math, verbal reasoning, science, patterns, culture, economics) and found that performance in one barely predicted another. Firms exploit this by combining specialists into a "mosaic" that covers each member's gaps, which is comparative advantage applied inside the firm.

AI breaks the usual models in two ways. Human labor supply is fixed, while agents scale on demand at the cost of compute. And human team size is capped by span of control, communication friction, and misaligned incentives, none of which necessarily bind agents that work directly with each other.

This also offers an explanation for why headcounts and aggregate productivity have barely moved despite frontier models writing production software. Note that the quiz sample and the thresholds come from the authors' own model, and the provided text cuts off before the regime-by-regime results.

The thread is small and mostly skeptical of the paper's premise that agent-to-agent coordination is a new economic input.

- **Coordination as overhead.** bob1029 argues the opposite of the essay: coordination is "a tax," and teams of models passing messages are losing right now. In their experience, every multi-agent setup they've seen (they cite "gas town") could be refactored into one agent loop with well-designed tools. Needing a new agent role usually means you couldn't make two tools work together cleanly, and splitting them across agents "merely hides the old problem inside a new one." On this view the hard part is tool design, not organizational structure.

- **Is "AI working directly with AI" actually new?** simianwords doesn't see the qualitative break. To them it's the same model doing a long-horizon task and spawning subagents internally, hidden behind a single human prompt, so they ask what they're missing unless the models are domain-specific. RomanKornev's answer is that it means agents buying and selling services directly from each other, with no human intermediary. simianwords pressed for a concrete example, such as one company's agents buying from a SaaS vendor's agents, and said they can't imagine AI making sales on its own. That question went unanswered.

- **Builder anecdote.** randysalami said they built a coordination system for onshore and offshore human teams and human-AI teams in 2023, including the possibility of purely AI teams. Even then they saw agents as guided by humans rather than as full team members. They are considering reviving it.

The unresolved crux is whether multi-agent coordination is a distinct capability with a threshold or just internal plumbing of a single agent. Nobody in the thread offered evidence either way.
