### Maxwell: A new kind of model that sits between code and hardware

#### [Submission URL](https://heymaxwell.ai/) | 124 points | by [laginimaineb](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=laginimaineb) | [60 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50152466)

**Maxwell runs an AI model inside the running program, rewrites the binary for the exact hardware and data it sees, and claims to formally prove every rewrite equivalent to the original.** doubleAI calls this a "Code Execution Model." It sits between code and silicon, with no humans in the loop and no source changes.

The headline numbers are vendor-reported:
- **38×** faster data processing pipelines and **4.25×** faster AI inference kernels.
- LTX-2.5 4K video generation drops from 250s to 134s per clip on two B200s (1.87×). LTX is a named customer and the pipeline was already hand-optimized.
- Whisper-small on an RTX 4090: 106.23s down to 49.82s for 180 transcriptions, with identical transcripts.
- NetworkX betweenness centrality on CPU: 61.44s down to 17.50s, with identical output.
- Linked research claims Maxwell beats NVIDIA's PyTorch baselines on 90% of SOL-ExecBench's Blackwell kernels after one day of search. It also claims a drop-in cuGraph replacement that is 3.6× faster on average, and a from-scratch rewrite of pandas' kernels.

The mechanism is profile-then-swap. Maxwell collects traces, profiling data, and input characteristics, then hot-swaps proven optimizations tailored to that machine while the program keeps running. The pitch is that re-optimizing code per hardware target and data shape, as people do for inference engines like vLLM, doesn't scale. The proof requirement is what makes autonomous binary rewriting plausible at all, because with nobody reviewing, the model "must never err."

The catch is that the proofs are the whole product, and the page I have doesn't show how equivalence is specified or checked. That matters most for floating-point kernels, where "identical output" is a hard bar. The demos are also short, vendor-run scripts. Access is early-access only, and pricing and supported stacks aren't stated.

The thread splits between people who think the idea is sound but the marketing oversells it, and people who want hard technical details the page doesn't give. The doubleAI founder (laginimaineb) answered in-thread, but most answers were promises of more detail to come.

**What the founder clarified**
- Results are cached rather than recomputed, and debugging is said to be handled the way CPython's Tier 2 JIT does it.
- Swaps happen in memory at runtime, not on disk. Outputs are signed and the equivalence proofs are externalized.
- The formal framework "embeds" the source language (e.g. Python) and the replacement (e.g. raw SIMD) in one system, including side effects and what "state" means in dynamic languages. Bend, suggested as an alternative, was dismissed as too narrow.
- Formalizing CPython reportedly turned up several memory-safety bugs in core CPython. Details are promised later.
- Versus "just have Claude profile it": Maxwell can touch underlying libraries like libc where you lack source, and it re-optimizes when data flow shifts, such as changing concurrency or sequence lengths in inference.

**Open worries**
- *Lock-in and offline use:* calgoo asked whether the output is effectively a black box tied to Maxwell's cache and cloud, and whether profiles or the data itself leave the machine. Those questions went unanswered. fc417fc802 countered that a formal proof is exactly what makes a black box acceptable, and that proofs of compiler output could even replace reproducible builds.
- *Visibility:* epsylon wanted to know why the optimizations aren't surfaced back at the source level. The founder replied that rewritten regions often have no source-language equivalent.
- *Skepticism on the gains:* julesrms argued that for already-compiled code there's little margin beyond what a modern compiler or LTO gives, and that his own JIT's SIMD specialization yielded only marginal wins. The thread cut off before a reply.

**Marketing versus substance**
mccoyb liked the idea (verification, hardware-specific superoptimization, live patching) but objected to the language. He read "must never err" as conflating the probabilistic model with its verification harness. He also objected to "self-improving code" (he suggested "dynamically-improving") and to calling an agentic LLM with tools a "model". He added that GPU nondeterminism and floating point make reproducing performance subtle.

krmmalik had hoped the "code execution model" would reduce bugs rather than just speed things up. sfink read the writeup as a heartfelt, human-written piece that overclaims novelty. In sfink's view it is an optimizing JIT with a superoptimizer, a shared cache and AI-guided search, and none of those parts is new. Still, a Python-wide equivalence model spanning CPU and GPU is impressive. sfink also noted the missing JIT basics: compile-time overhead, steady state, and what gets slower or bigger.

**"Brilliant" versus "just a JIT"**
gavinray found it brilliant, a combination of a Python formalization and an LLM that swaps in verifiably equivalent code. amelius pushed back that compiler people have long considered LLM-guided, correctness-preserving transformations. alirezaxdehghan called it "JIT with extra steps". pfg_ gave the best reply: a pre-optimized version can't be trusted unless you can prove it matches the original.

The central unresolved question is whether the proof machinery is as general and trustworthy as claimed, since the whole product rests on it and none of it has been shown.

### Timestral: Mistral's time-series foundation models

#### [Submission URL](https://mistral.ai/news/timestral/) | 613 points | by [groar](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=groar) | [118 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50150434)

**A 209M-parameter model tops all three major public forecasting leaderboards** (GIFT-Eval, TIME, fev-bench) as a single zero-shot checkpoint with no ensembling, beating the previous state of the art with about two-thirds of the parameters. Mistral says it ranks first across all five metrics among pretrained, zero-shot models.

The family comes in four sizes: 5M, 28M, 123M and 209M. Mistral claims every size sits on the accuracy-vs-size Pareto front. The 28M model reportedly beats Chronos-2, which is roughly four times larger, on GIFT-Eval and TIME. The 209M model beats TimesFM-3 and Falcon-2.0 with fewer parameters.

**It runs on CPU.** On an 8-thread server CPU, forecasting one day ahead from three weeks of 15-minute history takes:
- 10 ms with the 5M model
- 140 ms with the 209M model
- about three quarters of that latency for the 123M model, which lands within 0.2% of the 209M score

Functionally, it produces probabilistic forecasts (full quantile sets) and handles multivariate series jointly. It also takes past-only covariates, known-future covariates, and categorical covariates directly, without encoding them as numbers. The recipe combines synthetic pre-training, covariate-aware attention and long-context learning.

Mistral pitches this as a component for industrial and finance monitoring pipelines, with an anomaly detector feeding language-model agents. Domain fine-tuning goes through Mistral Forge. The excerpt doesn't state weights availability or a license, which matters a lot for anyone planning to self-host. The benchmark claims are Mistral's own, though results have been submitted to the GIFT-Eval and TIME leaderboards.

The thread splits over whether a generic, zero-shot time-series model is worth using at all, and most of the argument comes from people who already forecast for a living.

**The skeptics' case (joefourier and others).** Joefourier, who works in audio and vision, argued that lumping all time series together looks backwards. Known-future covariates like holidays, weather and schedules should be the foundation of a forecaster, not an add-on. A 5M model also has no idea what a "bank holiday" means; it just sees a flag flip from 0 to 1. In his experience, conditional generative models beat unconditional ones, so he asked why the model doesn't cross-attend to descriptions or labels. Y_Y answered that large nets learn to recognize a sequence and continue it in one pass, so with enough data outside information should become less necessary. Autuni added that with several years of history, models do pick up holiday effects from the data itself. Their hard problem was long-term baseline drift, such as solar panels and EVs shifting electricity demand.

Practitioners backed the skepticism with experience:
- jc4p does ecommerce projections and finds seasonal naive usually beats AutoARIMA, let alone foundation models.
- niltecedu runs about 400k models every 30 minutes and says one XGBoost model per series has never been beaten, and is faster too. Logancbrown countered that 2025 research shows time-series foundation models (TSFMs) winning in some scenarios. Dcl asked whether those series are stationary.
- jldugger noted that load-balancer scaling is already well served by ARIMA or Holt-Winters, and that Kubernetes HPA doesn't even implement full PID. The hard part is finding use cases where accuracy matters enough to pay for inference but not enough to justify a custom model.
- jonesn11 cited theorems implying many series can't be forecast more than a few steps ahead before collapsing to a straight line. They'd rather spend compute learning a mapping from proxy indicators than from past values.

**The defenders' case.** Logancbrown said the value is scale. With 10k series, per-series XGBoost models have little history each, while a single shared model has to handle scale mismatches and per-series feature work. A TSFM sidesteps both. Tdb7893 added that these models learn a procedure for looking at datasets, so they are no more "lumped" than any other model class. Kiann suggested hierarchical Bayesian models as the classical middle ground for the same many-series problem.

**Other points:**
- **Baselines:** btchashpoint's rule is to freeze each forecast as it stood on the day it was made, and to put the persistence ("tomorrow looks like today") baseline in the same table. A small benchmark gain means little without it.
- **Synthetic noise:** sheepscreek suggested using such a model to generate realistically shaped noisy data for robustness training.
- **Where covariates don't exist:** vintermann described ship-engine failure prediction from onboard sensors only, a case with no external information to add.

The open question is whether any TSFM beats a tuned per-series model or seasonal naive often enough to matter. The thread's concrete evidence leans toward the simple baselines, with TSFMs favored mainly for many-series, low-history setups.

### Agentic Monitoring

#### [Submission URL](https://benjamincongdon.me/blog/2026/10/19/Agentic-Monitoring/) | 115 points | by [bcongdon](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=bcongdon) | [22 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50153225)

**Agents can replace much of the human skill in production monitoring, and the author says they already do in his own prototype.** The post was sparked by an aside in an Anthropic blog post. Anthropic's team runs per-component "superfan" Claudes in Slack, each fixated on one system (a cache, a daemon, a merge queue). They read telemetry and logs like an engineer with nothing better to do, and they catch subtle, novel issues that traditional alerts miss.

Congdon's argument is that systems are increasingly "grown" rather than "designed." Code volume is rising while the amount of code any human has actually read is falling, and observability has always been expensive and slow to learn. He is surprised practitioners aren't writing about this, since the AI SRE startups have produced products but little field reporting.

His case study is change-point monitoring: close watch on binary releases and config pushes, where the SRE book's "~70% of outages" come from changes. The old approach had a bad tradeoff. Motivated teams are already safe, so you only reduce toil. Broad opt-in across all services runs into noisy health metrics and alert fatigue. Agents break the tradeoff because they will happily write per-change queries on the fly and scan logs indefinitely, which gets good precision and recall cheaply.

His prototype has three parts:
- An agent that reads service code and proposes a monitoring plan.
- A scaffold that executes the plan.
- An investigator agent for triggered monitors, with escalation to humans.

He says it caught regressions in systems he doesn't understand, with precision and latency he considers impossible a year ago.

The design lesson is that a pure agent loop isn't the best shape. Let the agent write a monitoring spec and hand it to a deterministic runner. That also keeps costs and behavior predictable. The evidence is one author's early prototype, with no published numbers in the visible text, so treat "it just works" as a strong anecdote rather than a benchmark.

The central dispute is scale and cost: can an agent really watch production telemetry, or is this "draw the rest of the owl"?

**The skeptics.** nevon argues that with terabytes of logs a day, countless metric series and thousands of repos and pipelines, you can't just point an agent at it. Even "p95 latency rose, which repo owns the code?" is hard with read access to everything. jeffbee says the energy and token cost of an LLM analyzing every data point is orders of magnitude too high unless operations are trivial. jubilee33 sees a related waste in agents run on cron for fixed tasks where a plain script would do.

**The rebuttals.**
- srdjanr says nobody proposes feeding raw data to the agent. It runs many queries, as humans do, and nobody reads terabytes of logs.
- suncemoje says segmenting and correlating data is exactly what simple agent systems with sub-agents and a few tools do well.
- t0mas88 agrees the harness is the hard part. Billions of identical log lines are useless to an LLM, but a trend change is not. Datadog and Grafana-style queries should become agent tools.
- antupis suggests a hybrid: scripts or classical ML do the large-scale crunching and raise alerts, and agents write the initial report and maybe a first PR.

This largely converges on the post's own design lesson, a deterministic runner plus an agent-written spec. suncemoje quotes that passage and asks how often the specs run and what a spec contains (just queries?), a question the thread leaves unanswered. cloudrkt asks whether a cheap "system one" model could pre-classify logs.

**Who builds it.** kelseydh expects the value to land in vendors like Datadog (Bits AI), not in-house builds. theshrike79 guesses that comes with a hefty price tag. forgotmypw17 points to an existing product, tasksmind.

**Small-scale practice.** Several people described their own versions:

- yegle: a daily Antigravity task that dumps Android logs over ADB and Tailscale and looks for crashing apps.

**Open gaps.** 2bird3 notes the post ignores high-cardinality data. The replies on how Anthropic ingests data were speculation (Kafka, a big analytics org), plus rihegher's remark that Claude Code regularly calls a Datadog API. esafak asked what anomaly detection sets the alerts, with no answer.

That prediction is the unresolved crux: whether the cost curve will outrun the skeptics' objections.

### CERN and Mistral sign a statement of intent to establish a strategic partnership

#### [Submission URL](https://home.cern/cern-and-mistral-join-forces-to-accelerate-ai-for-science/) | 354 points | by [kensai](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=kensai) | [60 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50153630)

**The target is a single pretrained "Large Physics Model," billed as the first foundation model for collider physics**, trained on billions of simulated proton-proton collisions from the LHC. The deal is only a statement of intent, not a signed contract. There are no funding figures, compute commitments, timelines, or release terms.

The pitch is to replace the current reconstruction pipeline. Today, a chain of specialized algorithms turns raw detector signals into the particles and objects physicists analyze. One model would learn across that whole chain and handle several tasks:
- reconstructing collisions
- identifying particles
- flagging unexpected signals
- evaluating future detector designs

The scale argument is concrete. CERN has stored roughly one exabyte of LHC experimental data, and collision counts are set to rise tenfold with the HL-LHC, expected around 2030. CERN brings the data and physics expertise, and Mistral brings large-scale training experience.

Two details are worth watching. Training is on simulated collisions, so how well it transfers to real detector data is the obvious open question, and the announcement doesn't address it. CERN's CIO also says the capabilities should "be shared" with the wider physics community, but nothing says whether that means open weights. Mistral describes itself as an open-weight lab, so that is plausible but unconfirmed.

The thread splits into two threads: a long insider exchange on why particle physics distrusted black-box methods, and a broader argument about whether modern AI has produced "real science" yet.

**Why CERN analyses were conservative about ML.** A former CERN PhD said only boosted decision trees were approved for Higgs analyses. Another physicist pushed back with ATLAS and Tevatron papers, including the Higgs discovery paper, that used neural nets for photon ID and b-tagging. The original poster conceded, noting that the core analyses were mostly cut-based.

Two commenters who had worked on ATLAS or D0, or followed that work, explained the caution as statistical hygiene rather than bureaucracy or budget:
- **Look-elsewhere and cherry-picking.** With flexible enough cuts you can keep trying regions until simulation and data diverge by chance. A BDT is "essentially just a trained list of simple cuts," so it is easier to justify. Blind analysis, where real data is only examined once everything is locked in, is the other safeguard.
- **Sim-to-data mismatch.** Simulation misses some correlations and doesn't model the hardware triggers perfectly. A net could latch onto an artifact, such as a pixel that runs hotter in the real detector than in simulation, the same way the classic "horse photographer's signature" classifier cheated. The usual check is to compare black-box methods against more explainable ones.
- Several noted that this is not a ban. People tried NNs, but you still need internal peer review and the sign-off of hundreds of collaborators.

### Introducing Clairic, a new System One class of extraction models

#### [Submission URL](https://blog.cloudflare.com/clairic-extraction-model/) | 69 points | by [shishy](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=shishy) | [7 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50155907)

It extends the "System One" idea TypeScript-adjacent vendor TypeSafe introduced a month ago with its Jev model. Jev and Cloudflare's Clef are decision models: you list the options and they return a probability for each. Clairic covers the case where you can't enumerate the answers in advance, such as an invoice total, a delivery date, days late, a repair cost, or the next 24 hours of traffic.

You send text (plus up to four optional images) and a map of named, typed fields, each with optional plain-language instructions. Each field comes back as one of four kinds of value:

- **Computed:** numbers, dates, times, durations and lists read or derived from the input, following their type's rules (two decimals for cents).
- **Estimated:** values the input only implies, returned as a distribution within bounds you declare, with a median and an 80% interval.
- **Forecasted:** future steps of a series, each with its own 80% interval.

Because nothing is generated token by token, every answer is a valid value of the declared type or `null`. Cloudflare says that rules out mistyped output and LLM-style hallucination, though the model can still be wrong, which is what the confidence scores are for.

The claim of "significantly higher accuracy, faster latency, and cheaper cost than traditional LLMs" comes with no numbers in the text provided. Treat it as unverified until benchmarks or the weights appear. The structured-output contract is the real draw: no JSON parsing or retry loops, and calibrated intervals instead of a bare guess.

Cloudflare's pitch is a pipeline: extraction models turn raw input into typed values, decision models turn values into choices, and LLMs turn decisions into words.

The thread is short and mostly enthusiastic. The one substantive exchange is about whether "System One" models are actually new.

- **Novelty vs. prior art:** zatkin, self-described as a non-expert, asked whether these are a refresh of old techniques or something new. They also floated shipping tiny classifiers, deciders and extractors inside Docker images for CI/CD, at roughly the cost of storing the image. The only reply, from _menelaus, said the real difference is the base. These models are built from capable LLMs trained on internet-scale data, so they work out of the box on almost any classification task. That is a contrast with training your own "brittle classifier." Nobody addressed whether the Docker-sized deployment is realistic, and nobody disputed the answer.
- **Missing benchmarks:** cat-whisperer pointed out that no benchmarks have been published. This echoes the gap in the submission's accuracy, latency and cost claims, and nobody offered numbers.
- **Likely uses:** spdustin guessed it could do well at named-entity recognition and intent/slot-filling. dstu called it a good middle ground between the Jev-style decision models and full LLM calls, and hoped "soon" for the open weights means days. giancarlostoro also wants the open weights, and nico welcomed the trend toward small task-specialized models.

### Show HN: Qwen3-4B with the AI writing tics trained out

#### [Submission URL](https://100toolkit.com/blog/deslop-model/) | 154 points | by [erikkannike](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=erikkannike) | [110 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50151928)

**Slop score down from 121 to 12.5 per 1000 words, with em dashes at zero**, on 40 held-out prompts, using a LoRA distilled from only 161 filtered answers. The released model is qwen3-4b-deslop, a Qwen3-4B-Instruct fine-tune. The Q4_K_M build is 2.5 GB and runs on a laptop via Ollama or llama.cpp.

The slop score comes from the author's own regex detector, built from a style guide of AI tics: em dashes, "it's not X, it's Y", lists of three, self-answered rhetorical questions, "here's the thing", "delve". Human prose usually scores under 15, and 68% of the model's answers land in that range. Because a regex can't judge quality, the author also tracked drift on 22 human passages, a 30-question factual quiz (still 30/30), and rule-based checks for broken output.

The more interesting part is the failure story. Round two hit a score of 9.9 and wrote badly:
- opinion pieces with no opinion
- a hallucinated University of Technology Sydney study
- ", which" joins at 28x the original rate

That last tic came from the author's own teacher prompt, which told the model to join sentences with "because, so, but, which". Two rounds of imitation amplified it.

The fix was a second filter for task fit: opinion pieces must take a position, personal pieces must use first person, emails need a greeting, and length must match the request. Only about 30% of teacher answers passed all filters. The score rose slightly to 12.5, but task fit returned to 80% (original: 78%), and invented studies nearly vanished.

The method distills a teacher built from earlier findings: a four-neuron patch, a plain-style prompt, and a direction-steering experiment that was dropped because it produced lowercase, off-topic text. The student needs no prompt or runtime hooks. Drift on human prose is about a third of what runtime steering causes.

The caveats are the author's own: the voice is "plain to the point of flat," and it still invents names and places. Training was done on a MacBook Air, so this is a 4B proof of concept. Scaling to larger models is untested.

### Harness2 by Google Research – Recursive Agent Harnessing

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/google-research/harness2) | 77 points | by [irthomasthomas](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=irthomasthomas) | [18 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50151059)

**Harness² tunes the agent's scaffolding, not its weights.** It rewrites the prompt, tools, memory, and other components around a frozen model at inference time, with no ground-truth feedback. Google Research's repo reports a single step lifting GLM-5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash over their base harnesses: up to **11.5 points on WorkBuddy-Bench**, 4.5 on JobBench, and 11.8 percentage points on LAB's partial pass rate.

The mechanism runs on two recursion levels:

- **Task-level:** a contrastive Propose–Probe–Compose step proposes edits, runs them on the same task, and composes a refined harness from the behavioral differences between executions.
- **Harnessing-level:** a persistent improvement harness keeps the adopted harnesses and evidence-backed lessons from each task, so later tasks start with better guidance.

Eight components are editable: system prompt, guardrails, memory, tool guidance, subagents, skills, scripts, and plugins. The `--k` flag sets the task-level budget. Parallel mode probes K general and K domain-specific candidates and widens the search, while sequential mode chains K refinements and goes deeper. The README says more budget brings further gains, though it gives no figures for that.

**Setup:**
- Needs Linux, Python 3.12, and uv.
- `harness2-demo` runs both recursion modes with deterministic editors and needs no credentials.
- Real runs use OpenCode (default) or Codex executors, Vertex AI model configs, and per-benchmark dependencies: Pandoc and Poppler for LAB, Docker for WorkBuddy-Bench.
- Model-backed runs cost API money, so start with `--max-tasks 1`.

The caveat is in the repo's own warning. Executor network access is unrestricted and Codex runs with its sandbox off. Public benchmark rubrics may be online, so you have to lock down the network yourself or a run can find the answers and grading criteria. The repo has only three commits, and the headline numbers come from the authors' benchmarks, so treat this as a research release, not a hardened tool.
