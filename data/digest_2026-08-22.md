### Why your local LLM feels dumber than it is

#### [Submission URL](https://forum.level1techs.com/t/why-your-local-llm-feels-dumber-than-it-is/253917) | 442 points | by [felineflock](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=felineflock) | [181 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49402232)

**Small shifts in next‑token probabilities from your inference stack** can flip THE→NE→XT into THE→NEW→DAY, which is why identical weights “feel” different off-host. The post walks through how hardware instruction sets, kernel/back‑end selection, precision choices, and sampler settings cumulatively move the logits distribution during decode. vLLM alone rides on a sprawling dependency tree (a nightly container with 734 packages, 252 via uv/pip), so two “same” setups almost never take the same code path.

Instead of judging by a few zero‑temp prompts, the author suggests representative benchmarks and long‑context/tool‑calling evaluations, and—mathematically—tracking drift via KL divergence of output distributions. The warning: KL numbers are meaningless without full methodology (reference checkpoints and runtime, eval text, context length, sample positions, KL direction, vocab truncation, and aggregation).

They start by isolating one big lever: prefill attention back‑ends. Using the official BF16 Qwen3.6‑27B checkpoint on an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell (TP=1, BF16 KV cache, no weight/activation/KV quant), with a pinned nightly vLLM, eager execution, CUDA graphs/prefix caching/MTP disabled, and 2k‑token chunked prefill, they compare how different attention implementations trade speed for precision—and how that choice alone shifts the logits.

Practical checks to cut the “feels dumber” gap:
- Match the model card’s sampler settings and chat template (e.g., temp, top‑p); too‑low temp can cause Qwen THINK loops.
- Benchmark your actual workload, not just zero‑shot trivia.
- If you use KL divergence, report enough context for others to reproduce and interpret the number.
- Be explicit about attention back‑end and precision per GPU SM; that’s where a lot of divergence starts.

The discussion validates the original post with concrete war stories about how minor stack details silently derail model logic, alongside debates over deployment hygiene:

- **Parser-induced lobotomies:** One user detailed a recent llama.cpp bug where a rogue `\n` leaking from a `<think>` block steered the model into an endless loop of "Actually..." self-corrections during long agentic sessions.
- **Lax test tolerances:** A developer warned that PyTorch ecosystem unit tests frequently cargo-cult high error tolerances (`rtol=5e-03`), which can allow entire attention matrix rows to be zeroed out while test suites continue to pass.
- **Ghost models and context limits:** When a commenter complained that a "Qwen3.8 4B" model felt remarkably stupid compared to Llama 3.2, peers diagnosed two classic local deployment traps. First, Ollama silently defaults to a 4K context on lower VRAM setups, causing reasoning models to forget their own prompts mid-generation. Second, there is no official 4B Qwen 3.8; the user had unknowingly downloaded an aggressive third-party distillation that peers mocked as "model homeopathy."
- **AI-assisted setup:** A sharp divide emerged over using Claude to configure local inference engines. Purists argued that users are wasting hours feeding runtime errors to AI instead of reading a single-page README. Defenders countered that LLMs excel at fixing obscure environmental states, correctly diagnosing Vulkan-build mismatches and undocumented `settings.json` schemas that manual Googling fails to surface.

Ultimately, the consensus points back to the article's core advice: to avoid chasing ghosts, users must stop evaluating models via manual chat interactions and instead build private, automated benchmark harnesses directly into their pipelines.

* **Methodology confusion:** Multiple readers criticized the benchmark's opaque terminology and inconsistent effort settings (e.g., Fable 5 evaluated on "high" while Opus 5 was on "max"), pointing out that the underlying methodology is difficult to parse without cross-referencing Prime Intellect's supplementary blog post.

### Anthropic appears to be A/B testing reduced effort levels in Claude Code

#### [Submission URL](https://twitter.com/argofowl/status/2091150597374537729) | 201 points | by [matthieu_bl](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=matthieu_bl) | [180 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49401549)

**Some users may be getting lighter‑touch code assistance while others see the usual depth**, which would make behavior inconsistent across sessions and accounts. If Anthropic is A/B testing lower “effort” settings, that affects perceived quality, latency, and cost, and can skew head‑to‑head tool comparisons. The practical risk is misattributing variability to your codebase or prompts when it’s actually treatment effects from the experiment.

The thread is dominated by heavy user dissatisfaction with Anthropic's recent Opus models, with near-consensus that versions 4.8 and 5 represent a steep usability downgrade from the peak of 4.6. Users report the models have become wildly verbose, prone to out-of-scope "snipe hunts," and saddled with florid prose. One developer noted a simple config file patch took Opus 5 43 minutes of spinning up containers and evaluating the whole repo, compared to under two minutes on version 4.6. Others reported older versions like 4.8 suddenly "nosediving" mid-session and ignoring explicit CSS design guides.

The debate centers on *why* this degradation is happening despite models performing well on benchmarks:
- **The Cost-Cutting Theory:** Many suspect Anthropic is silently replacing older, pinned models with cheaper, quantized versions to survive intense compute demands, or that the economics of inference are forcing them to heavily load-shed to accommodate free or lower-tier users. 
- **The Agent Overfit:** Others argue the models aren't technically getting dumber, but are being optimized for autonomous agent benchmarks rather than human interaction. For example, the severe verbosity might be an ad-hoc memory mechanism injected so the model can track its own revisions during long automated tasks, which completely ruins the daily chat UX for human developers.

While a few users defended the newer models' ability to write complex code (such as a GLSL fractal renderer), the prevailing sentiment is that actual developer utility has formally decoupled from benchmark success.

### AI has failed to win people's trust. Its makers? less trusted

#### [Submission URL](https://www.euronews.com/next/2026/08/20/ai-has-failed-to-win-peoples-trust-its-makers-even-less-trusted) | 28 points | by [billybuckwheat](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=billybuckwheat) | [5 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49404869)

**52% of Americans now say they’re more concerned than excited about AI, up from 37% in 2021**, and only 9% are more excited than concerned. For the first time since Pew began tracking it, a majority of under‑30s (55%) are in the “more concerned” camp.

Distrust is spilling onto the people building AI: a CNBC Generation Lab poll found majorities distrusted every named tech leader, with Satya Nadella faring “best” yet still distrusted by 65%, Alex Karp worst at 81%, and Dario Amodei and Sundar Pichai around 75%. Despite that skepticism, only 36% picked an independent expert body as the rule‑setter for AI.

Across Europe, worries skew toward jobs: EY reports 42% of employees fear AI could put their roles at risk, and a Commission‑backed survey says 66% of the public expect more jobs destroyed than created. Yet attitudes are split rather than bleak: half see AI helping with big challenges; 55% expect a positive impact on daily life over 20 years (vs 35% negative); 84% want careful management for privacy and transparency; 62% view AI at work favorably (32% don’t), while 60%+ don’t feel informed about research risks.

In the US, the discontent is showing up as local backlash to new AI data centers, driven by a perceived lack of personal benefit and visible downsides like classroom cheating and artists’ work folded into training data without consent. The trust gap is now as much about who wields AI as what it does.

The primary debate among commenters is whether the AI industry actually needs public trust to succeed. One side points to the unprecedented adoption rate of AI tools—comparing its velocity in software development to `git`—as proof that utility trumps sentiment. In this view, executives can be actively distrusted even by their own paying subscribers, much like petroleum companies or politicians who are widely disliked but functionally indispensable. 

Countering this, skeptics argue that current usage metrics are a mirage created by companies forcing AI integrations into existing products and workflows. They warn that ignoring public sentiment under a "move fast and break things" philosophy carries delayed but heavy costs, pointing to Meta's current legal liabilities over youth safety as a preview of what awaits the AI industry once the public decides the technology is causing active harm.
