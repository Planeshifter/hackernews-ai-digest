### Scaling Hierarchical Networks

#### [Submission URL](https://www.cartesia.ai/blog/from-tokens-to-bytes-part-1) | 90 points | by [guybedo](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=guybedo) | [11 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50278253)

**Cartesia says its H-Nets, which learn to chunk raw bytes inside the model instead of relying on a tokenizer, reach up to 3-5x compute efficiency over tokenized Transformers** in a controlled 1-stage setup. The result comes from a standard scaling ladder of tokenized Transformers used as the baseline.

The pitch is the bitter lesson applied to the last hand-engineered piece of the LLM stack. H-Nets recursively compress data through dynamic chunking (characters to words to phrases to concepts), and the whole process trains end-to-end. Tokenized models and other byte-level alternatives typically use multiple training stages.

Headline claims from this first post of two:
- **1-stage H-Nets:** 3-5x compute efficiency gains, attributed purely to learned tokenization.
- **2-stage H-Net:** beats a matched open-source model with 10x less compute and data.
- **New behaviors:** the models show in-context tokenization, which tokenized LMs can't do.
- **Beyond text:** ongoing work in biology and vision, with no numbers yet.

These are Cartesia's own results, and the excerpt cuts off before the experimental details. It doesn't say which open-source model was matched or at what scale, and the 10x figure depends on both. The second post promises a wider model sweep to explain why the efficiency gains are so large, and that sweep is where the claim will be tested.

The thread splits into two arguments: whether bytes are a sensible base unit, and whether learned chunking can compete with BPE in practice.

**Are bytes the right "atoms"?** jephs opened by arguing that bytes aren't a natural or raw encoding. Much of what BPE does is undo arbitrary Unicode choices. A Latin letter is one byte while a visually identical Cyrillic letter is two, and CJK characters take 3-4 bytes. jephs listed alternatives (IPA, bitmaps, SVG programs, Ideographic Description Sequences, raw codepoints) and noted that no thorough survey exists. jaen countered that H-Nets only need some indivisible base unit, and bytes are about the best expressivity-per-compute tradeoff. Raw bits would cost 8x more computation with no extra expressive power, and bit-level structure like ASCII case is trivial to learn. vatsachak put it as "bytes are raw data and the architecture chooses the tokens." jephs held that real raw data is speech, handwriting, and keystrokes, and bytes are several abstraction layers downstream. Neither side conceded. The disagreement is mostly about whether "good enough base alphabet" or "natural base alphabet" is the right standard.

**Will it hold up against BPE?** augment_me argued that BPE exploits multi-character redundancy and decodes faster. On code or English, they expected a byte-level model to underperform, and they thought speculative decoding (they'd seen 32-token spec-decode in frontier models) widens the gap. They saw H-Nets as useful only for niche domains like tabular data. Three replies pushed back:
- yorwba said H-Nets also learn multi-character patterns, just not hard-coded ones, and that speculative decoding still applies. The question is empirical.
- kavalg framed the premise as a three-way tradeoff. Byte-level isotropic models transfer well but cost too much. Tokenizers are efficient but lose semantics. H-Nets aim for both, and the authors also show DNA and images where ready-made tokenizers don't exist.
- nivter said generality is the main point, and a common tokenizing paradigm across modalities would help scaling.

Left unresolved: no one had measurements on decoding speed or on code and English at scale, so the speed objection stands as a prediction rather than a finding.

One lighter comment: vatsachak enjoyed the Haskell quicksort tokenization example.

### Sleep framework: A new way for LLMs to retain knowledge in continual learning

#### [Submission URL](https://research.google/blog/the-sleep-framework-a-new-way-for-llms-to-retain-knowledge-in-continual-learning/) | 74 points | by [acossta](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=acossta) | [6 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50272479)

**Google Research proposes giving deployed LLMs a periodic offline "sleep" phase** where short-term context gets distilled into permanent weights, aimed at ending both catastrophic forgetting and the fixed knowledge cutoff. The authors claim the model can retain long-context knowledge up to 10M tokens, absorb new facts, and improve reasoning.

The mechanism has two stages that run with the model offline:

- **Consolidation:** newly unlocked low-rank experts in a Mixture-of-Experts (using Google's Hope architecture) sit in slow-changing memory blocks, so old parameters aren't overwritten. Knowledge from fast in-context memory is transferred into them via on-policy Generalized Knowledge Distillation plus an RL imitation objective that rewards similarity (Levenshtein distance) to teacher text. The temporary high-frequency parameters are then reset.
- **Dreaming:** the MoE router deliberately activates non-standard experts to generate synthetic samples that mix domains. Gradient-based selection filters these, and the model fine-tunes on the top-rated ones, checking whether downstream performance improves.

The neuroscience framing (NREM, REM, synaptic pruning) is branding over a scheduled post-training loop. The post says as much: everything runs inside a fixed architecture under human-defined training rules.

The catch: the capacity grows with each sleep cycle, since consolidation depends on pre-allocated expert blocks being unlocked. The excerpt doesn't say how that scales over many cycles, and the benchmark details were cut off before the results section, so the 10M-token claim is unverified here. It's also a research proposal, not a shipped product, and it needs a specific architecture rather than working on arbitrary existing models.

The thread is thin on debate. Most of it is people trying to understand the mechanism, plus one practitioner's account of a competing approach.

- **Audit and lineage as the deployment problem:** gopalv's team built a similar system, "Dreamtime", earlier this year. It distills learnings across sessions from user corrections of unsatisfactory responses, not from the token or KV-cache layer. It works as a sawtooth: accumulate context, check for dissonance, compress it back, retire the old version. Their view is that customers' real blocker is auditability. You have to be able to ask "when did this change, and who changed it?", and that is hard to answer from weights unless lineage back to the original sessions is retained. They also don't want a trained model to become an "accomplice" for a user nudging an agent repeatedly to hide financial crimes. gopalv also found it odd that the paper's diagrams build on the KV-cache, which they contrast with Databricks' RAFT.
- **Mechanism, plainly restated:** imimayj1337 read it as reserved, unpopulated parameters in the architecture that get filled during sleep and reinforced by the dreaming step. They guessed that only a vertically integrated player like Google could pull it off, and that compute-constrained organizations would struggle. gavinray roughly agreed and added that short-term memories are scored, and the useful ones are integrated through a transform of the old weights. That is meant to avoid dumping whole memories into the weights additively.
- **Enthusiasm:** mdp2021 called it a crucial missing piece toward AGI, needing only critical thinking added during cleanup. No one picked up or challenged that claim.
- **References:** macleginn linked the actual arXiv paper and called DreamCoder a favorite ML paper that this reminded them of.

Nobody tested the paper's claims, such as the 10M-token retention or how capacity scales over many sleep cycles. The one concrete objection is gopalv's: weight-level consolidation is hard to audit.

The method works because a byte-match against the binary gives a clear pass/fail test, and agents can attempt every function in parallel. Note that the Zoetermeer result is described as a working port with reconstructed source. The post doesn't claim a byte-matching decompile, the standard the author sets for projects like Paper Mario.

The author's high-score tip comes from ChatGPT: fill every empty tile with churches.
