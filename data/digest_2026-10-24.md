### GPU-unlock: FP/BF16 instruction issue rate on Nvidia 3000's cards

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/Zagitta/gpu-unlock) | 324 points | by [fragebogen](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=fragebogen) | [74 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50219700)

**A Linux kernel module that lifts the FP/BF16 issue-rate cap on consumer Ampere cards**, so GeForce 3000-series GPUs run tensor math at the rate of their professional equivalents. On a 3090 Ti at 450 W, dense 8192³ GEMM with FP32 accumulation goes from 87.5 to 157.4 TFLOPS in BF16, 87.5 to 118.4 in FP16, and 42.6 to 82.6 in TF32.

It builds on the CMP unlocker's V67/PLM technique and the pre-driver timing of an earlier UEFI approach. The module writes the speed selectors (SS0 `0x88888888`, SS1 `0x8`) and performs a function-level reset before the stock NVIDIA driver binds the card. It is neither a UEFI app nor a patched driver, so you don't re-patch on every driver release. It targets GA102, GA103, GA104 and GA106.

**User reports are the evidence here.** Results are crowd-sourced per card, and the benchmarks differ per report:
- **Unlocked:** 3090 Ti, 3090 (+25–67%), 3080 10 GB and 20 GB, 3070, and 3060 12 GB (about +80%).
- **Doesn't work:** one OEM 3080 Ti 20 GB completes the unlock but stays at half BF16 rate.
- **Untested or no reports:** the 3070 Ti (the unlock worked from a UEFI port on Windows) and GA103.

The lowest gain, +25%, comes from cards capped at 225 W, so the power limit matters.

**Only matrix-heavy work benefits.** LLM prefill gains 14–36%, and diffusion and FlashAttention speed up. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and INT8 is unchanged, so both stay flat. Reports that compared outputs found them bit-identical before and after. Multi-card setups (up to four), display-driving cards, and vfio-pci passthrough all have reports.

**Setup:** run `make install`, which detects Arch, Debian/Ubuntu or Fedora/RHEL, builds a package, and puts the module and its firmware into the initramfs. Then add `gpu_unlock.targets=all` to the kernel command line. The build downloads pinned NVIDIA/CachyOS inputs over HTTPS and checks their SHA-256. The distributed source and packages contain no NVIDIA archives or generated firmware.

The catch: this deliberately writes hardware registers that NVIDIA gated off, and the results span a wide range across cards. Test your own card before relying on it, and note that a card driving the display goes blank briefly at boot.

**Power and heat dispute**
Jacquesm cautioned that real gains need a raised power limit of up to 450 W, which is beyond what stock coolers are designed for. Tiberium pushed back that the unlock works regardless, and he saw 40–50% gains in some workloads at the stock limit. Jacquesm said his seven different 3090s all needed more power to exceed about 25%, and asked for card and cooling details. Zagitta reconciled the two views. The unlock only flips two bits that halve the issue rate of certain instructions. More issue throughput draws more power, so the card drops down its frequency curve to stay within the limit. How much headroom remains depends on the workload and on thermals, which is why water-cooled cards (his two 3090s have run a month without issues) fare better. ### Neural chip decodes interactions between brain regions

#### [Submission URL](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/neural-chip-decodes-interactions-between-brain-r-2/) | 134 points | by [giuliomagnifico](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=giuliomagnifico) | [47 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50222525)

**A 4x2 mm implantable chip now does cross-region brain synchrony analysis and state classification on-device, in under 200 microwatts.** EPFL's LogSync, from Mahsa Shoaran's lab, targets the gap in closed-loop deep brain stimulation: most adaptive systems read from a single region, while the lab's premise is that psychiatric disorders come from disrupted communication between regions.

The chip records neural activity and extracts two kinds of biomarkers: oscillation strength and synchrony between regions. A machine-learning classifier runs on those to separate cognitive and emotional states, then fires stimulation triggers when it detects a target state. The power savings come from hardware-algorithm co-design and logarithmic computations, which simplify the math behind connectivity analysis. A conference version of the work (VLSI 2025) listed 32 channels at 196 µW.

Validation is real but early:
- **Human data:** previously recorded psychiatric-study datasets, one on cognitive engagement during a mental task, one on emotional arousal while watching video clips. The algorithm told the states apart from neural recordings alone.
- **Rats:** at the University of Minnesota, the implant tracked ongoing oscillations and emitted stimulation triggers at predefined phases of brain activity.

The catch is that the human evidence is offline, retrospective recordings, not a patient with the chip implanted. Classifying arousal during video clips is also a long way from detecting a depressive episode. No accuracy figures appear in the press release.

The work is published in IEEE JSSC and funded by a CHF 6.6 million NIMH grant aimed at mood and anxiety disorders. The team also pitches it for adaptive DBS in movement disorders and for closed-loop non-invasive and focused-ultrasound stimulation. In parallel, the lab is moving toward learned representations over hand-picked biomarkers, with an ICML 2026 self-supervised model (RECTOR), and a spin-off, Infera Neuro, is involved.

The thread splits into a few strands: personal experience with the technology, a skeptical read of the paper's numbers, and a long tangent on ADHD and meditation.

**The only first-hand account of closed-loop implants came from a NeuroPace RNS user.** AbstractH24 pointed out that much of this already exists and has been in their skull for four years. A former member of the RNS research team asked how it had worked. The answer was mixed:

- Its main value turned out to be diagnostic. Their doctor could see subclinical seizure-like activity stop during titration and adjust other meds precisely, so the data mattered more than the stimulation itself.

Their conclusion was that implants are powerful for collecting data, while their ability to reliably alter brain function is "questionable." That sits awkwardly with the closed-loop-stimulation pitch.

They said it is impossible to judge these without the base rates and without comparisons to other classifiers. A simple verbal probe of the subject might predict the state better than the EEG does. They also doubted that a single chip beats full EEG headgear for richness of information. They suggested it would make sense mainly where the dysfunction is known to be confined to specific regions.

### Why is AI generated language so hard to understand?

#### [Submission URL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29162) | 235 points | by [canjobear](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=canjobear) | [191 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50223647)

**Frontier LLMs write with the syntactic habits that slow human readers down, and the authors blame training pressure for density over clarity.** Across a new multi-domain corpus, all nine frontier and near-frontier models used more inanimate subjects with animate verbs and more low-frequency n-grams than human writers. Most also produced longer dependencies, more stacked nouns, and more reduced object relatives in conversation.

The features come from psycholinguistics, where they are known to correlate with processing difficulty. The models also beat round-trip-translation paraphrases on these markers, so the difficulty isn't just a side effect of rewording. Model families differ substantially, which means "AI prose" isn't one monolithic style.

The proposed cause is that human language is shaped by the need to be understood, while post-training increasingly optimizes for math, code, and reasoning, where compressing information is rewarded. As a proof of concept, the authors fine-tuned a language model under a length penalty and saw it drift away from human-like linguistic behavior.

The causal evidence is thin. A single small fine-tuning experiment shows density pressure *can* produce this drift, not that it's what happened inside the production models. The abstract also reports feature frequencies rather than direct reader-comprehension results, so "harder to understand" rests on the known psycholinguistic correlates. It does give labs a measurable target: these features could be tracked or penalized during training.

Authors: Clark, Martin, Mahowald.

The thread starts from the study's claim but quickly becomes a debate about what "good writing" means, and whether rule-following tools make prose better or worse.

**Why models write this way.** Technical writer dividefuel guesses that RLHF rewarded raters' first impressions: lists, headings, punchy phrases and length are easy to spot and prefer, but they say little about whether the text helps its audience. Taek gave a parallel from Gmail's writing suggestions, which averaged about 1.3 per sentence and were almost all bad. He compared them to a style textbook applied clause by clause, with no sense of pacing, emphasis or emotion. He thinks frontier-model fine-tuning has the same flaw: rewards for conciseness and no filler, with no counterweight for how the whole piece flows. Tanoc went further and argued that readers don't absorb text linearly, while LLMs organize and translate linearly (A to B to C). He thinks that is what makes their prose feel strange.

**The "their Google" sidetrack.** Taek's examples set off the thread's longest argument.
- **Prescriptive camp:** Izmaki, who learned English as a second language, was taught never to use "their" for a corporation. He saw the tool's corrections as accurate, and said they simply clash with everyday usage.
- **Descriptive camp:** SwellJoe argued that expressing intent matters most, and that hard-to-read English is never "correct" however grammatical. Sharlin and CalRobert pointed to British usage ("Nike have announced…", "The Parliament have…"). Borealid framed "Google" as a metonym for its staff. Bluefirebrand noted that a corporation is just a group of people.
- **North American counterpoint:** silverFork cited a Canadian government style guide that calls for a singular verb when a group acts as a unit. Sharlin replied that even that guide allows plural agreement when members act individually, so no single rule is universal. silverFork conceded that British usage differs ("The army are advancing").
- **Unresolved:** Izmaki asked whether the goal is correctness or comprehension. He prefers precise sentences that need no social context to decode. SwellJoe answered that his ambiguous friend's problem was failed intent, not failed grammar, and that perfect grammar can mislead just as easily.

Taek's closing point was that his "their" was a deliberate choice of voice, and an editor that "fixes" it erases the personality. He argued that this is also why LLM prose reads as it does: it follows the rules without the messiness that carries meaning.

### Making My Blog Readable by AI Agents

#### [Submission URL](https://deallocatedobjects.com/posts/making-my-blog-readable-by-ai-agents) | 33 points | by [ingve](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ingve) | [8 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50225443)

**A static nginx blog can serve markdown to agents with one `map` and a rewrite**, no framework needed. Send `Accept: text/markdown` to any post URL (or append `.md`) and you get the post as plain markdown with YAML frontmatter (title, canonical URL, author, dates, description) instead of HTML.

The negotiation hinges on two details. Every response carries `Vary: Accept`, so intermediate caches don't serve HTML to an agent or markdown to a browser. The `.md` responses also send a `rel="canonical"` Link header to the HTML page plus `X-Robots-Tag: noindex`, so Google doesn't treat them as duplicate content.

The rest of the evening's work is mostly generated files:

- **`/llms.txt`**: blog summary, how to get markdown, feed locations, citation preference, and every post with a markdown link and one-liner. A Link header on every response points to it. `/llms-full.txt` bundles all posts into one ~225 KB file, and `/index.md` is the homepage.
- **`robots.txt`**: explicitly allows GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, Google-Extended and about a dozen others, with `Content-Signal: search=yes, ai-input=yes, ai-train=yes`. The author wants citations; a product site might not.
- **Feeds**: the Atom feed gains summaries and a markdown alternate link, and a new JSON Feed uses a custom `_markdown_url` key per item.
- **Metadata**: descriptions, canonicals, Open Graph/Twitter tags, and JSON-LD (BlogPosting, breadcrumbs, WebSite/Blog/Person) on every page.
- **IndexNow**: a ~30-line script pings Bing on publish. Google doesn't participate, but Bing's index feeds several AI search tools.

The cleanup of 2014-era problems is the most reusable part. Stored descriptions had paragraphs mashed together ("…your app.The RevealWhat do I mean…") and some contained raw `<img>` tags, so summaries are now built from rendered text. `maximum-scale=1` was blocking pinch-zoom and is gone. With no `og:image`, X and Threads grabbed the first image on the page, an old banner, so each post now has a 1200x630 card.

The pitch is that none of it is hard. The post itself is fetchable as markdown with the same curl trick.

The thread's main argument is that the whole exercise may be aimed at readers who aren't there. The top comment cites two log-analysis studies. One found no sign that GPTBot, ClaudeBot or similar crawlers request or prioritize `.md` content, even when it's listed in `llms.txt`. The other covered 50 sites over 200+ days and logged zero `/llms.txt` requests from any major AI user-agent. A later commenter pointed to a third experiment where no bot touched the file in three months.

Anecdotal evidence pushed back only weakly:
- One user said their agent tool (oh-my-pi) once read a site's `llms.txt` during research.
- Another gets the odd attempt on a personal site, but mostly bots just fetch `robots.txt`.

Nobody offered data showing real adoption. The open question is whether `Accept: text/markdown` negotiation works better than `llms.txt`, since it serves interactive agent fetches rather than crawlers. The thread didn't test that.

Two other points came up:
- **robots.txt gotcha:** Groups don't merge. If you give GPTBot or another named bot its own group, it stops obeying `User-agent: *`, so every disallow line has to be repeated in each named group.
- **Ethical objection:** One commenter asked why you'd help AI companies take your writing for free. A reply argued that a human is usually still on the other end of Claude or ChatGPT, trying to solve a problem or learn something, so serving them isn't just feeding a scraper. The author's stated goal of getting citations was not weighed directly.

One commenter also dropped a bare link to agents.md, with no discussion.

**Revenue per worker and profit margins are rising for the Mag 7 but flat for the other 493 companies in the S&P**, according to Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok, who reads that as zero financial payoff from AI outside tech. The behavioral claim is the interesting part. His forward-looking question is whether cheaper, commoditized models can lift revenue and margins at ordinary companies. He's skeptical. The caveat is that this is a short note built on five charts, not a study. Mag 7 versus S&P 493 is a blunt split, and it can't isolate AI's effect from everything else moving those margins. ### That AI Temptation

#### [Submission URL](https://www.tbray.org/ongoing/When/202x/2026/10/19/The-AI-Temptation) | 16 points | by [robin_reala](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=robin_reala) | [6 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50218743)

Tim Bray is writing a CBOR Flattener for Quamina, his Go pattern-matching library, and using the project to work out publicly why he's *not* handing it to Claude, even though he thinks the agent would probably do fine.

The technical setup: Quamina flattens each input into a sorted list of Field structures (`{"X\nY": 21}` style paths) and matches against that. Flattening already costs more than matching, and the main optimization is skipping fields nobody is matching on. That requires a tokenizing parser rather than a one-shot deserializer. The default Go CBOR library (Faye Amacker's) is polished but deserializes whole blobs, so the "simplest thing that could work" would be to deserialize, then walk the tree. Bray concedes that's defensible engineering right now: code is cheap, time isn't, and the success criteria are clean enough to fuzz-test.

He's skipping it anyway, for two reasons:
- **It's a hobby project, and writing high-performance tokenizers is the fun part.** He plans to borrow liberally from Faye's code and tests instead.
- **Ethics and economics.** He objects to routing token spend to AI vendors and the layoffs they enable, and he suspects current pricing is a bubble that will reprice painfully.

The same logic applies to testing. Quamina has 14K lines of test code and 47M of test data, mostly "run this JSON, expect these results," and he wants it all replayed with CBOR input. He predicts that's tedious and an obvious agent job, but it would burn tokens, so no LLM there either.

He's upfront that he's no GenAI expert and is drawing on PRs he's seen and indie devs he follows. His one direct data point: someone had Claude write a Protobuf Flattener for Quamina, and it half-worked, was buggy, and was hard to read, with the "that was six months ago" caveat noted. It's more a values statement than an argument about capability, and the piece is candid that the pragmatic case points the other way.

The thread barely touches Bray's CBOR/tokenizer question. It turns on his line about AI being "peddled by terrible people", and then on who actually controls AI.

**Who controls AI?** bemtevi opens by disputing the "terrible people" framing. In their view, human nature meeting powerful technology produces those traits no matter who is involved. LogicFailsMe replies that the root cause is concentration: roughly ten billionaires steer AI, surrounded by sycophantic coalitions that make them nearly impossible to depose. They propose a wealth tax on the top ~2,000 richest Americans. They also say LLMs are "amazing and magical", and that people who feel threatened by them have invested their egos in being the sole holders of such abilities. Gormo challenges the premise with ecosystem evidence: tens of millions of users and hundreds of thousands of organizations on HuggingFace don't look like ten people's control.

The sub-argument is over what "frontier" means:
- **sterlind:** Frontier training costs billions, so without China's open-weight releases a handful of US billionaires would gatekeep anything stronger than Llama. They question how long China will keep releasing.
- **Gormo:** Calling the frontier expensive is close to a tautology. Small groups and individuals train on affordable hardware, today's frontier becomes well-trodden ground within months, and the FOSS community is distilling proprietary models quickly.

The crux is unresolved: does the lagging edge of open models count as real independence, or does control of the frontier still matter?

**Dismissal:** drodgers calls the post "Old Man Yells At Cloud". They accept the subjective viewpoint but find too little engagement with the economics and consequences to make it worth reading. This lines up with the post's own admission that the pragmatic case points the other way.
