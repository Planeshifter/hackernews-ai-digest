### Why Micron Is Suddenly Ditching 4F² DRAM as Rivals Double Down

#### [Submission URL](https://hothardware.com/news/why-micron-is-suddenly-ditching-4f-dram) | 114 points | by [rbanffy](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=rbanffy) | [17 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50245014)

**Micron is abandoning 4F² DRAM after "over two decades" of work on a design it says it invented**, and it's doing so while Samsung, SK hynix, and CXMT all push ahead with it. CXMT claims 4F² in server DDR5 RDIMMs by the end of the year.

The case against, from CTO Scott DeBoer on Wednesday's roadmap webinar: 4F² promises a theoretical 33% density gain over today's 6F² cell, but real DRAM density is set by capacitor spacing, not nominal cell footprint. So the gain is small in practice while cost and complexity jump. Note that 6F² vs. 4F² describes cell layout, not process node, which is why the two aren't directly comparable to logic-style node names.

Micron's plan instead:
- **1δ (1-delta):** high-density, yields ramping now, with Boise as a production site.
- **1ε (1-epsilon):** in development, adds advanced wafer bonding.
- **ζÅ and ηÅ:** later planar generations, stretched out using High-NA EUV and new materials.
- **3D DRAM:** the actual destination, with stacked layers of storage cells. That's much harder than 3D NAND because each DRAM cell needs a tall capacitor and a leaky transistor, so the architecture needs a rethink.

This is a cash-and-focus bet. If rivals burn capex on vertical-channel transistors and then stall on capacitor scaling, Micron looks smart. If they ship 4F² economically at scale, Micron cedes density and cost ground in the near term. The roadmap gives no date for when 3D DRAM becomes viable, and that's the part the whole wager hinges on.

The thread opens with suspicion of Micron's framing, then splits into a business-logic debate and a technical aside on what 4F² actually means.

**Is this spin?** Danieru's skeptical reading: a company behind the curve is recasting "sticking with the old thing" as a strategic choice, and announcing it before rivals ship only makes sense as investor messaging. Responses:
- **Track record:** xadhominemx says Micron is regarded as technologically adept, with a history of competitive DRAM without competitors' manufacturing "bells and whistles."
- **Disclosure:** TrainedMonkey and brudgers argue a public company has to answer to investors. beambot disputes this, saying no one is obliged to announce anything. kjs3 counters that exiting a line of business may trigger SEC disclosure under Regulation S-K Item 303.
- **Research continues:** toast0 notes Micron hasn't stopped research, only abandoned one area. "We worked on this for decades and can't commercialize it" is a healthy outcome.

**Why walk away?** Several economic readings were offered:
- **Innovator's dilemma (bostik):** current lines are too profitable to divert capacity to unproven tech. Micron holds the 4F patents and can let others absorb the scaling cost, then use the IP for later technology transfers. bostik also questions this against the earlier claim that 2027 capacity is sold out.
- **Bubble economics (rbanffy):** the density gain is small relative to the process cost, and the AI boom rewards milking existing lines. Micron can return to 4F later.
- **Margins (InsidiousWombat):** at current markups, scaling the existing process beats more research.
- **Oversupply and rowhammer (bcrl):** focusing on 3D means keeping per-mm² cost low, as with V-NAND, where 2D feature sizes were relaxed in exchange for more layers. Smaller cells may also struggle to meet rowhammer-robustness targets. Management likely sees a real oversupply risk, and DRAM price crashes have historically led to anti-dumping fights.
- **Skeptics:** rdtsc jokes that a "fake out" is hard to explain to shareholders. SecretDreams doubts the CEO is more than a margin chaser.

**What 4F² means:** spankalee found the 8F²/6F² diagrams confusing, and adrian_b said they're misleading and pointed to Micron's 6F² patent (US 6,975,531). An ideal cell sits at the intersection of two lines of width 2F, so 2F × 2F = 4F². Naive layouts take 2F × 4F (8F²), and stacking and sharing lines gets 2F × 3F (6F²). toast0 gave a simpler account: one transistor and capacitor per bit, and 4F² puts a bit at every word-line/bit-line crossing, double the density of 8F² at the same F.

The unresolved crux, per adrian_b: Micron says the extra processing cost outweighs the smaller die, while Samsung, SK hynix, and the Chinese makers claim the opposite. The thread couldn't settle which is right.

### Fixing Tool-Call Accuracy in vLLM

#### [Submission URL](https://www.ubicloud.com/blog/fixing-tool-call-accuracy-in-vllm) | 171 points | by [coffeemug](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=coffeemug) | [71 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50242870)

**Kimi K2.6 on vLLM 0.28 produced schema-valid tool calls only 92% of the time**, meaning 2 of every 25 calls failed. Ubicloud's engineers say that failure rate explains why open-weight models in agent harnesses often look much worse than their benchmark scores.

The cost compounds in multi-turn agentic sessions. A malformed call doesn't just fail once; the model keeps building on nonsensical context afterward, so quality degrades across the rest of the session.

The fix is **constrained decoding**, which restricts token generation so output must conform to the tool's JSON schema. vLLM added support recently and exposed it as a server-side flag in 0.31. Ubicloud reports that turning it on gets newer Kimi and DeepSeek models to 100% schema accuracy.

The excerpt walks through the mechanics. An agent harness like OpenCode sends a JSON array of tool definitions (for example `webfetch`, with a required `url`, an enum `format`, and an optional `timeout`). The inference engine wraps that in model-specific tokens, such as Kimi's `<|im_system|>tool_declare<|im_middle|>`. The model then emits a call between special tool-call tokens, and that call has to match the schema for the harness to execute it.

The provided text cuts off before the constrained-decoding details, flag name, and any performance overhead, so those are unknown here. Also note that "schema accuracy" measures well-formed calls, not whether the model picked the right tool or sensible arguments. If you self-host open-weight models behind an agent, check which vLLM version you're on.

The thread's first move was to reframe the headline, and its second was a technical argument about whether constrained decoding is actually free.

**"Fixed in vLLM" vs. "configured vLLM."** Aurornis argued the title misleads. Constrained decoding and constrained tool-call decoding have long existed in vLLM, so Ubicloud mostly had to configure it correctly, and the configuration story changed across versions in ways that altered behavior. Aurornis still praised the team for having benchmarks that catch regressions, noting that some OpenRouter providers show inexplicably poor quality and clearly don't. A Ubicloud author (jesdo) partly pushed back: Kimi needed real code changes that no configuration could provide. vLLM later fixed this upstream, so now `--tool-strict-level parameter` does it. Others called vLLM fragile and over-complicated (jacquesm), and one suggested the title should say "with vLLM" rather than "in vLLM". Another reader said the article didn't confuse them. Asked whether Ubicloud sells Kimi API access, jesdo said it currently serves DeepSeek on dedicated clusters for large customers, with no public service.

**Does constraining distort the model?** hansvm wanted data on quality impact. His argument was that schema-constrained decoding changes the output distribution, and that retrying a failed call, which also reaches 100% validity without polluting context, might do better. The extra cost is small, since at a 23/25 success rate retries add roughly 9% tokens on only a fraction of total tokens.

- **Where it differs:** saurik asked why masking invalid tokens isn't equivalent to retrying. hansvm replied that it is equivalent to token-level retry, but not to retrying the whole call.
- **Example failure:** models dislike long JSON strings. A string like `"Bob is a fantastic builder, having ..."` has its ellipsis terminate the content, and constrained decoding must then close the string right there, so the output was already bad by the first period. A full retry avoids this.

- **Related points:** Creamsicle47 said forcing out-of-distribution tokens raises entropy downstream. Aurornis noted that field order can be tuned so reasoning fields come first, and that XGrammar has an any-order option, though he doesn't know whether vLLM exposes it.
- **Ubicloud's framing:** jesdo said their goal was matching first-party behavior, not maximizing quality. He acknowledged papers suggesting constrained decoding can hurt quality depending on the model, and said a follow-up post is possible.

### Cross-request KV cache exposure in the GLM-5.3 Workers AI model (155 responses)

#### [Submission URL](https://blog.cloudflare.com/kv-cache-exposure-remediation/) | 177 points | by [wondersky](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=wondersky) | [71 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50252159)

**Prompts over 681,280 tokens on Cloudflare's GLM-5.3 deployment could come back with other customers' data in the response.** 155 responses were affected between August 27 and September 9, 2026. Cloudflare found no evidence of deliberate exploitation; the remaining traffic looked like ordinary long-context use.

The root cause was two bugs stacked. Cloudflare configured a 1,048,576-token context window but gave the prefill GPUs' KV cache pool room for only 681,280 tokens. They knew about the mismatch before launch and assumed the engine would error on oversized prompts. It didn't. In SGLang's disaggregated prefill/decode mode, an over-length prompt was marked rejected and swapped for a one-token placeholder, but the request wasn't aborted. Prefill computed the cache for the placeholder and shipped it to the decode node, which saw "transfer complete" and assumed the full prompt had overwritten its pages. It hadn't, so decode attended over stale pages still holding earlier requests' data.

The deeper design issue is that KV cache pages aren't zeroed between requests, a common performance tradeoff, which makes the engine's ownership tracking the only isolation boundary.

**Timeline and fix**
- Gadi Evron of Knostic reported it via HackerOne on September 8.
- Cloudflare reproduced it the next morning and pulled the model within 40 minutes of declaring an incident.
- The model was restored September 9 after two changes.
- **Config:** the prefill pool now holds about 2.04 million tokens, roughly 2x the context window, so valid requests never hit the rejection path.
- **Engine:** rejected requests are now retired at the disaggregation admission queue with their original error, before any allocation or KV transfer. Cloudflare proposed this upstream as SGLang #38704; the maintainers also merged #38935, which rejects such requests earlier.

Cloudflare says no other Workers AI model was affected. The lesson is that sizing the config so the bug can't trigger is fragile. Quantization, tensor parallelism, or a future model version can silently shrink the pool again, so the engine-level fix is the real remediation. The post is published alongside a coordinated disclosure from SGLang.

- **Commercial intent:** The project is a commercial-minded product with ambitions of undercutting Adobe, not a library.
- **Fun and trade-offs:** Some people enjoy reinventing wheels, want different trade-offs, or dislike an existing license.
- **Bad search:** One commenter suggested search is so bad and LLMs so good that reimplementing feels easier. Others rejected this, saying it's "a scary level of AI mania" because a 60-second search or a fork would be faster, and LLM output still misses edge cases.
- **Language culture:** One commenter said Rust skews toward rewrites. Others countered that "X in Go" and "X in JavaScript" had their own waves, and that this happens whenever a language gets popular.

**Practical counterpoints.** One commenter who ingests PDFs commercially avoids `lopdf` because it handles broken files poorly, and noted that the library linked in the top comment explicitly addresses that. Another observed that the README lists `lopdf` and `pdf_oxide` as dependencies, so the project isn't a from-scratch parser. A commenter also noted the maintenance signal: printpdf's last listed release was five years ago, while `pdf_oxide`'s was two months ago.

**The security audit.** One commenter ran an analysis of the repo, noting that about 98% of commits are by AI bots, and called it not production ready. The findings:
- **Local API (high):** CORS allows any website, there is no authentication, and `/api/v1/pdf/mcp-exec` runs tools against arbitrary local file paths. Any page the user visits could overwrite files or read text from local PDFs. The docker-compose file exposes it to the network, and there is no request size limit.
- **Desktop app (high):** Search results render document text as unescaped HTML, there is no CSP, and Tauri's file scope is `**`. A malicious PDF or DOCX could therefore read and exfiltrate local files.
- **Downloads (medium):** Models and packs have no checksum verification, some come from the live `main` branch, and `pack_id` is used in a path without sanitizing, allowing `../` traversal.
- **URL fetching (medium/low):** The SSRF guard only checks literal IPs, follows redirects, and misses `[::1]`, `0.0.0.0` and `100.64.0.0/10`.
- **CI (low):** `wasm-pack` is installed via `curl | sh` and Actions are pinned to tags, not commit hashes.

Another commenter joked about waiting to exploit the CVEs in all these new vibe-coded PDF engines.

The unresolved point is whether AI makes rebuilding cheap enough to justify skipping years of accumulated edge-case fixes. The audit suggests the security cost of that choice is real.
