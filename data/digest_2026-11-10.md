### TimeNet: Open-source data infrastructure for Temporal Foundation Models

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/OpenTSLM/TimeNet) | 44 points | by [rjakob](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=rjakob) | [6 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50443264)

**TimeNet is a standardized storage format plus a client API for time-series datasets**, pitched as "ImageNet for temporal data." The goal is to give Temporal Foundation Models a common data layer, since this data is scattered across organizations in incompatible layouts.

The mechanism is a format called **TimeF**. A connector converts a raw source into an immutable, versioned TimeF package: a `manifest.json`, a `control.duckdb` file holding hierarchy and relationships, and a Parquet or Zarr plane for signal values. A registry stores those versions, and the client reads them without importing the connector. Loading is a few lines: `TimeNet().load("timenet/hello-world@1.0.0")`, with the local registry checked first. Task definitions cover classification, QA, scalar prediction, temporal localization, forecasting, generation, editing, and correspondence.

The one number offered is that training on multiple temporal datasets raised F1 per dataset by an average of 14% for OpenTSLM and 22% for SLIP. That comes from the authors' own "first experimental results," so treat it as preliminary until you've read the paper.

- **Scope:** it handles only the data layer. Training, inference, and evaluation stay in your own framework.
- **Maturity:** pre-release, and the README warns that the public API and the TimeF format can change before 1.0. The repo has about 73 stars.
- **License:** MIT for the code. Each dataset keeps its upstream license and access terms, so check the licensing guide before redistributing anything.
- **Install:** Python 3.11+, `uv add 'timenet[cli]'`, with dataset connectors and the `timenet-build` producer CLI in a separate `timenet-connectors` package.

The contributor list spans Stanford, ETH Zurich, Google, Harvard, Cambridge, and others, and an online hackathon offers up to $50k in cloud credits. The real test is whether outside organizations onboard proprietary data, since a registry only becomes ImageNet-like once it has more than the connectors its authors wrote.

### Tracking Anthropic-discovered vulnerabilities

#### [Submission URL](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/anthropic-glasswing-november-update) | 447 points | by [panic](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=panic) | [218 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50447074)

**Seven months in, VulnCheck counts 442 CVEs credited to Anthropic's Glasswing, about 0.5% of the 93,450 published this year, and only two have been exploited in the wild.** The author, who has tracked the project since April 17 in an open-source repo, calls the results underwhelming. He also argues the gap between discovery and remediation is widening.

The numbers from Anthropic's own ledger, as of its November 2 update:

- **37,966 findings**, up nearly 12,000 since September 8, but only 7,349 (19.4%) are public.
- **217 marked fixed**, a gain of just 15 over the same two months.
- **1,654 marked duplicates**, about 7.5x the fixes, up from 21 in September. Another 419 were withdrawn.
- **The claimed true positive rate rose** from 90.8% at launch to 94.2%. VulnCheck suspects duplicates and withdrawals aren't counted against it.

The author's explanation for the duplicates is that findings sitting in the queue get found and fixed by someone else first. Anthropic's own October 8 OSS Scanner post concedes the bottleneck: about 29,000 candidates, roughly 6,000 manually reviewed, and nearly 5,000 unverified reports already sent to maintainers who asked for bulk delivery. The self-service scanner shifts validation work onto maintainers. In curl's case, 19 of 34 published reports were rejected as vulnerabilities, six were confirmed low severity, and nine are still pending, which undercuts the headline true-positive figure.

The author isn't a neutral party. VulnCheck sells exploitation intelligence, and "exploited in the wild" is its own KEV metric, which is a harsh yardstick for a project that never promised exploitation. He also concedes that AI is effective at finding vulnerabilities. The stronger criticism is operational: discovery that outruns human triage and patching mostly generates maintainer workload.

The thread's center of gravity is a single first-hand counterexample to the article's skepticism, and most of the discussion is about why one experience diverges so sharply from VulnCheck's numbers.

**The counterexample.** pizlonator (Fil-C's author) got 9 reports from Anthropic. All were real bugs, with "exceptional" test cases and fixes that were "alright" (some taken verbatim, most used as inspiration). He stressed he's not a naive sample, since he has done browser-engine security defense. dwohnitmok added that curl's maintainer now says slop reports are "practically gone" and the problem is "high volume, high quality." sashank_1509 countered with a Linux maintainer who was unhappy about false positives from Mythos.

**Why the experiences differ.** Several explanations were offered:
- **Codebase age (pron):** Mature, well-used code has already had its serious bugs shaken out, so aggregate statistics could look weak even if individual cases are good. pizlonator replied that this can't explain a chasm as wide as the one he sees.
- **A crisp bug definition (pizlonator):** Fil-C has a falsifiable memory-safety boundary, so findings aren't a matter of judgment. d0liver countered that this means he did most of the hard work, and that AI is supposed to multiply attention rather than require so much up front.
- **Maintainer attitude (viraptor):** Some maintainers nitpick or dismiss reports as unlikely or user error rather than hardening anyway. grim_io argued that small issues can no longer be ignored because an LLM will chain dozens of them.
- **Incentives (20k):** Large, high-profile projects are targets for marketing-driven headline counts. A small project like Fil-C isn't.
- **Source bias (atleastoptimal):** VulnCheck sells vulnerability intelligence and has an interest in downplaying the tools.
- **Solo versus team maintainers (zulban, NewsaHackO):** The two disagreed over whether Linux maintainers are less impressed because they already get strong contributions, or because a solo maintainer faces a different load than one of many on a huge project.

**"Real" versus "matters."** gwd, working on Xen, said every bug Claude flagged was real but many were absurdly obscure, such as a use-after-free during CPU offlining. A few still crossed the line into genuine security issues. tialaramex made a similar point from Rust: "technically not OK" is far from real-world exploitable. a2dam rebutted that a perfectly persistent AI attacker makes rarity irrelevant. sheepscreek speculated that chaining such quirks is exactly what a strong model could do.

**Why nothing dramatic has happened.** sashank_1509 said nothing has happened on the scale of the CrowdStrike outage. pizlonator agreed the doom is exaggerated but gave two reasons it can coexist with real findings. First, defenders are patching under embargo. Second, capable attackers use exploits surgically. derefr guessed that long-held 0-days used by criminals and militaries are being burned faster than new ones appear.

The unresolved crux is whether the 217 fixes against 37,966 findings reflects poor report quality or a triage and patching bottleneck. The anecdotes mostly support the second reading.

### AlphaProof Code

#### [Submission URL](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/) | 668 points | by [ilreb](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ilreb) | [195 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50439193)

**Agents wrote over six million lines of Lean to prove that the compiled Signal protocol code, not just its design, meets its security properties, and DeepMind says it took four weeks.** The properties include keeping messages protected before and after a device is temporarily compromised. Verification work usually stops at the protocol-design level, while real bugs tend to hide in the gap between design and implementation. DeepMind claims this is the first formal verification of a widely deployed protocol's implementation by any method, human or AI.

The framework, AlphaProof Code, divides the labor like a research team:
- Humans plus a mix of models turn Signal's security goals into formal specs, and human experts review every one.
- Agents powered by Gemini Carbon write the proofs in Lean.
- Agents then audit their own proofs and flag about 1% of the codebase for human review. Lean's checker handles the rest.

For a sanity check, the team reintroduced two real, previously fixed Signal bugs into an isolated copy of the code. In both cases the system produced a machine-checked proof that the buggy version was insecure.

The weak point is the specifications. Lean guarantees the proofs match the specs, not that the specs capture everything you'd want, so the human review of every spec carries the trust. The "years for expert teams" comparison is DeepMind's own framing, and it covers a specific scope, Signal's core cryptographic code, rather than the whole app.

DeepMind is open-sourcing the verification artifacts, so the community can check the Lean work directly. A technical paper accompanies the post.

The thread's center of gravity is skepticism about scope: "zero bugs found" sounds too good, and commenters mostly converged on the view that the proof is narrower than the headline suggests.

**What was actually proven.** Hedora opened with the suspicion that either the theorems were wrong or only narrow properties were checked. Hedora's own experience: Claude-written "verified" crypto and storage code passed its proofs yet had many bugs that other implementations caught with ad hoc testing. Derefr gave the most precise reading, which others echoed. The result is not "no bugs" but "no current bug can break the formalized security model." It is a fail-safe claim, not a claim about memory leaks, crashes, or non-security failures like protocol deadlock. Pyrale put it as a rule: when software is called "formally proven," ask what was proven.

**Protocol vs. implementation.** Mentalgear and neuwirth stressed that this covers Signal's cryptographic core, not the app or the OS, which are the bigger attack surface. Derefr wondered whether the Lean was derived from a design spec or from the actual code. Pcthrowaway argued the reintroduced-bug test implies it was the implementation. He added that libsignal is only one of several implementations, and he still expects the verified scope is the logic around the cryptographic primitives. Dnautics asked about multithreading and about unproven assumptions regarding primitives. Melatonic asked whether SGX had ever been verified.

**The spec problem.** This was the main argument, in several forms:
- Cortesoft: this limit applies to all formal verification, since a spec can't be proven to match intent.
- Fooker: you just trade code bugs for spec bugs, a "trusting trust" scenario, and AI will happily edit the spec until the code verifies.
- Legitronics: the AI-selected 1% for human review was never shown to be a reliable filter, and nothing proves the security goals are necessary and sufficient.
- Oggy: necessity can be shown by a concrete attack when a property is omitted, but sufficiency never can. Decades of research have given a decent grip on basics like confidentiality and authenticity, while weaker-assumption and more exotic goals remain open research.
- Guenthert: machine checking of code against a spec is old (AMD's FPU after the Pentium bug, L4 variants). The hard part has always been writing the spec, which is where LLMs come in and where the trust gap remains.

**Below the proof.** Gf000 and janc_ noted that compiler and CPU bugs sit under any such proof. Lubujackson listed real-world issues like timing signals, compiler-dependent overflows, and hardware weaknesses that formal models may not capture. Gf000 countered that software can be modeled, though the model explodes in size.

**Spec-driven development tangent.** Jwpapi had abandoned spec-driven workflows because they created drift. Fooker replied that specs offer compactness, since they can be denotational ("what it is") where code can only be operational, but the benefit depends on how much correctness matters to the project.

**Heartbleed-style test.** SamuelAdams suggested validating the approach by pointing it at known-CVE code. Tsimionescu's reply is cut off in the provided text.

### Amazon saves millions by replacing Sonnet 4.6 with Strands Decider

#### [Submission URL](https://strandsagents.com/blog/amazon-saves-millions-replacing-sonnet-with-strands-decider/) | 719 points | by [aloukissas](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=aloukissas) | [256 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50436640)

**Amazon's internal ticketing team swapped Sonnet 4.6 for a small Gemma-4-based decision model and cut labeling cost about 98%**, from roughly $22,200 to $500 per million tickets, with accuracy that beat production Sonnet on a held-out ticket set. The post comes from the Strands team itself, so read it as a vendor case study, though the numbers are specific enough to check.

A decision model can't generate text. You hand it state plus a question with fixed options, and it returns a probability per option and a confidence score. Ticket labeling across 20 categories fits that shape. The team rewrote each label as a one- or two-sentence criterion, because production's label descriptions alone exceeded the model's 4,096-token window. They also tuned a confidence threshold for applying a label.

**Measured per ticket (Sonnet 4.6 vs. Strands Decider E4B):**
- Latency: 4,215 ms vs. 897 ms
- Input tokens: 6,554 vs. 2,009
- Output tokens: 170 vs. 1
- Lambda duration dropped 74% on cutover day

The decider runs on a SageMaker ml.g5.xlarge (one A10G, about 19 GiB used, ~640 ms per request) at $1.408 per instance-hour. Full utilization would be $250 per million tickets, but burst headroom brings it to about $500. Those are list prices and exclude Lambda.

**The rollout pattern is the reusable part.** An AppConfig flag with three modes (BEDROCK, DUAL, DECIDER) let them run both models on live traffic, apply Sonnet's labels, and review every disagreement. Rollback was a 45-second config change. The authors say this taught them more than offline evals did.

One gotcha: reordering JSON keys in the input shifted output probabilities by up to 0.19. They now sort keys on every request, so check this before comparing runs on any decision model.

The authors are candid about the limits. It works because the task is a closed choice from a stable catalog, at high volume, with latency pressure. If labels need explanations, the catalog changes weekly, or useful context won't fit in a few thousand tokens, an LLM is still the right tool. Note too that the comparison is against Sonnet with a long prompt generating JSON; a cheaper LLM tier or a tuned prompt wasn't tested.

The thread turns the story into a "full circle" retrospective: LLMs didn't replace classic classifiers so much as change how you get to them. The dominant view is that the expensive model is now the bootstrapping tool, and the cheap model is what you run in production.

**What LLMs actually changed**
- **Transfer learning vs. cold start.** jval43 argued that SVM-era work meant starting from scratch each time: customers wouldn't allow cross-customer training, features were hand-annotated, and data was scarce. miki123211 countered that pretrained transformers (BERT and later) solved this by pretraining once on a general task like missing-word prediction and fine-tuning on small proprietary sets. The thread also noted that LLMs work as zero-shot classifiers, and their labels can be distilled into a small model.
- **Static vs. contextual embeddings.** thesz said word2vec already did the missing-word job well. maleldil replied that it assigns one vector per word, so "bank" has the same representation regardless of context.
- **A clarification on private data.** Aurornis stressed that LLMs didn't train on private customer data. They generalized from huge public corpora. BobbyTables2 said they suspect Anthropic trained on proprietary data, citing Claude describing undocumented hardware register fields. Others offered only snark about unlicensed data, and nobody substantiated it.

**LLM as labeler, small model as runtime**
- solidasparagus, exe34, flatline and IanCal converged on the same pattern: use a big model to generate or label training data, then train a cheap classifier. flatline would hand-label nothing anymore and wants no LLM in the loop at inference.
- IanCal added uses beyond labeling: data augmentation, an LLM as the oracle in active learning, and agents that run train/test/review loops.
- evolabs warned that synthetic data is not real data and its distribution needs care. true_religion clarified that the proposal is to run the expensive model on your real data, not to synthesize inputs.
- armcat said their 2021 system (Linnaeus) started with a few dozen dev-selected JIRA tickets across about 20 classes, expanded to 120+, with daily retraining. That undercuts the claim that classic ML needs lots of data and labeling effort.

**Expertise vs. convenience**
- benrutter likened LLMs to Electron apps, wasteful but easy, since an SVM needs someone who understands labeled datasets. IshKebab said AI can now build the SVM too. benrutter wanted expert sign-off anyway, and IshKebab thought reviewing is easier than building from scratch.
- Foobar8568 said they'd trust an LLM over "pseudo experts" after seeing stats PhDs validate models on training data.

**Hybrid routing**
mrbungie proposed routing familiar cases to an in-house classifier and low-confidence ones to a zero-shot model or LLM. asgraham clarified this for a confused ctxc. pavon pushed back that in-house training is a budget problem of collecting and labeling data, not a skill problem. cogogo predicted non-technical stakeholders will grow more suspicious of anything trained in-house.

**Visibility**
matthorse suggested ML had collapsed into LLMs for consumers. miki123211 replied that non-LLM models are just invisible, like trucks and combines behind cars, with banks still using random forests and SVMs and image processing still using CNNs.

### AI usage generates a fragile codebase

#### [Submission URL](https://evanxmerz.com/post/ai-usage-generates-a-fragile-codebase/) | 628 points | by [plun9](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=plun9) | [601 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50438318)

**The author was laid off from Meta after resisting AI-heavy coding, and now argues from experience that agent-written code quietly rots.** He says he hit the 90% AI-generated minimum there anyway, saw no 2X speedup, and watched strong peers merge brittle code, like JavaScript mouseover events where `:hover` would do. He now generates 100% of his code with AI at a new employer because it's expected.

His case for fragility rests on four mechanisms:

- **Shallow tests:** agents write trivial getter/setter tests and e2e tests that cover only paths the codebase already handles, leaving gaps that line-coverage metrics don't show.
- **Over-engineering:** extra helpers and DRY applied to a fault make code harder to debug.
- **Eroding comprehension:** the longer agents write the code, the fewer humans understand the data structures well enough to spot an abstraction that doesn't fit.
- **Review overload:** each PR arrives faster than the last, so reviewers skim and small quality issues pile up.

He then lists symptoms to check for: more incidents, slower recovery because humans know the code less well, and errors that only users see because nobody ensured proper instrumentation. The text cuts off partway through that third symptom.

The evidence is anecdotal. The claim that agents are "the reason" for more incidents comes with no data, and he concedes AI handles problems that fit in its context window well. Even so, the review-throughput point is the strongest one, since it's a process problem that doesn't depend on how good the model is.

The thread splits between a pragmatist camp, who say AI is the only viable way to work and that business never rewarded quality anyway, and skeptics, who say the resulting code is brittle in specific, recognizable ways. Neither side moved the other much, and the most useful content came from the skeptics' concrete examples.

**The pragmatist case.** The top comment comes from a 20+ year engineer who now writes 100% of his code with AI. He argues that enterprise software was never about robust codebases. Timed release cadences already fought against quality, and AI just extends that. He says he has moved from writing lines to management and architecture, and that the agent can fix its own mistakes. He admits time will tell.

**The brittleness examples.**
- **Overfitting:** One commenter says AI solves a problem by fitting the exact combination of conditions in the codebase today, where a human would generalize. The fix works until any of those conditions changes.
- **Duplicated policy:** Another saw the model compute a serialized string's length with separate logic from the serialization itself, in a single Rust module. It works, but it creates a bug route that wouldn't otherwise exist.
- **No judgment about generality:** A team lead asked for one alert and got 400 lines hardcoded to that metric, so a second alert would need another 400. A reply argued that premature generality is also an anti-pattern. The lead answered that alerting is plainly something that should be generic from the start, and that the judgment call is the engineer's job.

**Delete code, don't add it.**
- One commenter says their biggest productivity gain came from shrinking the codebase, and that most could lose about 50%. Another reports a refactor that removed over half the code with the same product.
- Several veterans said deleting code is the first thing they do on a legacy codebase.
- One noted the agent will happily keep backward compatibility forever.
- Opus got praise for consolidating near-duplicate codebases.
- On architecture, one commenter found that splitting a deep tree into libraries helps the model inside a library but hurts it across the project. Another prefers fewer abstraction layers, because LLMs handle concrete code better and make abstractions cheap to experiment with.

**Who is talking.** One commenter claimed that most comments dismissing quality come from accounts only months old, and that quality was always cheaper for anything beyond a prototype. Others called this a strawman, or said it implies everyone who disagrees is a shill. One reply said businesspeople are naturally enthusiastic about AI as a selling point, with no shilling required. A second commenter said they have never seen a decent LLM-written codebase without heavy handholding and refactoring.
