### There is no posttraining data commons

#### [Submission URL](https://openathena.ai/blog/there-is-no-posttraining-data-commons/) | 116 points | by [lukasschwab](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=lukasschwab) | [26 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50352641)

**Pretraining got a ready-made commons of documents; posttraining needs tasks, and nobody has been archiving those.** David Hall's argument is that Wikipedia, ArXiv, Stack Exchange, Gutenberg and Common Crawl (about 10 petabytes since 2007) were lying around when LLMs needed text. Nothing equivalent exists now that frontier work has shifted to posttraining and agentic RL. His own lab's open models, along with Nemotron, still draw most of their pretraining data from that commons, so truly open LLMs depend on continued access to it.

The core distinction: **pretraining consumes documents, while posttraining consumes tasks**, and a usable RL task is a frozen abstraction of what humans actually do. Human action is situated, conditional and unrepeatable, like an oral story told once. An RL task has to preserve three things: a world, an intent, and tools for acting on the world. It also needs a grader to score the outcome.

Software is the exception, which Hall offers as the reason agentic AI hit coding first. Version control, CI, unit tests, and containers have spent decades making work durable and replayable from an identical starting state, which is exactly what RL wants. The imperfections are real (Debian has chased reproducible builds since 2013 and still doesn't call itself fully reproducible), but open-source software is still a commons of preserved work.

The excerpt cuts off before the author's conclusion, so what he proposes for building a posttraining commons outside software isn't visible here. The framing alone is useful: the bottleneck isn't raw data volume but making action durable, and only one domain has put in that effort.

The thread splits over whether an open posttraining commons is feasible, who it would serve, and whether it can be built without being captured.

**The "it already exists" camp.** woctordho argues the commons is forming on its own. Distributed posttraining is much easier than distributed pretraining, and all it needs is agent traces from everyday use. Hugging Face now supports trace uploads, DataClaw collects them, and Chinese "transition stations" reselling GPT and Claude access log GBs of plain-text traces daily. His estimate is 10-100 TB already freely downloadable. He adds that even without the encrypted raw CoT, tool calls and CoT length are useful signals. thoughtpeddler points to Poolside's public trajectories as an existing open corpus. Amekedl says the post is naive because frontier labs already hold enormous private posttraining sets. stymaar's reply is that the post is about a *public* equivalent, which doesn't exist.

**The "don't build it" camp.** The longest argument is between ArcHound and dlwh (the author, from the Marin / Open Athena project).
- ArcHound says any commons that benefits contributors will eventually be extracted from. He points to OpenAI's trajectory, Google's sponsorship, and the pattern of grassroots open efforts turning profit-seeking. He thinks pushing back on adoption until consent, ownership, and disruption are settled is better than participating.
- dlwh replies that the real alternative isn't "AI goes away" but "a handful of very well capitalized companies buy what they need and squeeze the labor market." He wants the process to be participatory, with contributors having a say in what gets automated.
- lukasschwab adds that labs have already spent many millions buying closed posttraining data, so the question is whether open-weight models get to compete in those specialties at all.
- impossiblefork suggests such a commons may need protection, possibly as a state project for competitiveness.

**A side thread on state actors.** dannyw speculates about the NSA feeding labs surveillance or industrial-espionage data, citing the Airbus case. Retro_Dev doubts that sharing wiretaps would help the NSA. edot and lnkdinsuxs flip the direction: the government demands weights or builds in-house, perhaps via Maven infrastructure. dlwh also notes that NVIDIA, praised as the open exception, withholds a large share of its posttraining data, though it discloses what it withholds.

**Other points**
- **Donation model:** david_shi likes the idea of a trusted steward, like the Internet Archive, handling anonymization and eval design so professionals such as retiring accountants will donate their data.
- **Organizational reality:** mrkn1 jokes that the data we need is stuck in "the spreadsheet Karen maintained," and dlwh says the post was partly a sublimated rant on why that data was never going to exist.
- **Private specialization:** lnkdinsuxs predicts developers will each post-train self-hosted expert models, with posttraining being the "secret sauce."
- **Provenance:** emsign contests the "lying around" framing as large-scale IP theft. est31 notes Stack Overflow, one such source, is dying.

The unresolved crux is whether a participatory commons can stay that way once it is valuable, or whether the alternative is simply labs buying the data privately.

### The 'Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen' post is misleading

#### [Submission URL](https://honnibal.dev/blog/astra-read-write-misleading) | 1020 points | by [syllogism](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=syllogism) | [207 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50351111)

**The "just by looking at raw weights" claim only holds for three of the nine experiments**, and none of them involve a real language model. That is the core of Honnibal's rebuttal to the Astra post, which he compares to a magician rotating through different tricks so each one seems to debunk your guess about how the others work.

His breakdown of the original post's experiments:

- **Raw floats in context, no tools, no running the network:** only experiments 1, 2 and 4, on networks of 17K–172K weights. Tasks include naming the game a PPO MLP was trained on and fixing a stuck Kinetix car policy.
- **Python required:** every experiment with a real language model. The headline Qwen3.5-0.8B result (MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%) also runs the model on up to 50 batches of practice problems.
- **Failures:** spotting the sycophantic Gemma 3 270M failed without attention patterns, and the Qwen-style fix on Gemma failed too.

The game-network claims also carry caveats. The MinAtar networks start from zero weights, so there is nothing to read. The "beats PPO-100M" result only beats PPO-500M on Breakout (517 vs. 25), and loses on Seaquest (53 vs. 89) and Asterix (53 vs. 55). The Kinetix fix was given one recorded failure episode, and the 0% to 100% result came from a single try that changed 3.5% of the weights.

His conclusion is that the Qwen result shows Astra can be prompted to do what a human ML engineer does: write code that analyses and edits weights. He grants that this is not trivial and that models a year or two ago would have failed. But it is not the "deep capability" a single technique would imply if it both authored small game networks from scratch and improved a large model. Because these are different techniques stitched into one narrative, he argues they don't belong in the same post.

The thread's most weighty contribution came from an interpretability researcher who largely sided with the rebuttal. Reader enthusiasm for the underlying idea, LLMs editing weights directly, ran in parallel and was only partly dampened by it.

**The insider deflation (x313, mech interp).** The Qwen headline result isn't really about math. It reduces reasoning loops, which happen when the model gives the `</think>` token too little probability. Steering vectors already fix this, and the contrastive analysis Astra used is the standard human method. x313 calls it an intro-level interp project that takes 4-8 hours, comparable to binary patching with a debugger in software. It's cool that frontier models can do it, but not unexpected.

- **Why did Alibaba ship the bug?** HarHarVeryFunny asked why, if the fix is so easy, Alibaba didn't catch it. Replies split three ways:
  - Post-training is a black box where fixing one behavior breaks others (dennysora-main).
  - Steering is a lobotomy with side effects, so you fix one defect and add ten (bhu8).
  - Labs routinely ship sloppy releases, with template and weight issues fixed later (embedding-shape).
- dr_kiszonka doubted that the fix is a 4-8 hour task, given how many people quantize and fine-tune open models, and asked for a concrete sampling-settings fix that doesn't cost reasoning quality.
- theptip agreed on the math result but wanted x313's view on experiments 1-5, where one-shot reading and writing of weights seemed more surprising. That question went unanswered.

**Practitioners excited by the technique.** bushido spent weeks on conventional training for a git-commit-message model, then let a larger LLM edit weights directly. In twelve hours it made more progress than the previous two weeks, including removing and then restoring attention-head directions. rybosome built a "vibe tuned" privacy-sensitive classifier whose weights were one-shotted by an LLM. Early results look competitive with a hosted model on accuracy and latency, and rybosome called the efficacy "absurd." Others suggested benchmarking it against other classifiers and open-sourcing it.

**Do commit-message rules belong in AGENTS.md?** runitx was surprised that agents ignore a written standard and said Claude had been very reliable for them. bushido said agents keep adding hedging, contradictions and future-looking notes, and that a pre-commit hook helped. majormajor reported the same "Claudespeak" problem and has Codex rewrite Claude's prose afterward.

**Two kinds of experiment.** HappyDreamer guessed the model uses Python only when the weights don't fit in context. syllogism corrected this:
- Reading weights directly works only when behavior relates fairly linearly to weight values, which is true of small nets and not of large models.
- The Python route never puts weight values in context. It computes diagnostic statistics, as an ML engineer would, so it is a coding problem rather than "looking."

HappyDreamer accepted this, remained impressed by the small-net results, and said the original post could have been split in two. overfeed pointed out that the rebuttal itself already says the raw-weights setting applies to only experiments 1, 2 and 4.

### Nvidia – open-source auto-ontology

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/NVIDIA/auto-ontology) | 287 points | by [campervans](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=campervans) | [138 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50348962)

**NVIDIA's Auto Ontology builds a governed context layer for data agents**: it generates an ontology from enterprise metadata, reconciles conflicting metric definitions across systems, and serves the result to any agent harness over MCP, CLI, or API. The pitch is reliability, not speed. A revenue number returned faster is worthless if the definition behind it is wrong.

The pipeline runs in five stages. It discovers or imports ontologies and semantic models, grounds agents in business meaning, validates generated SQL against certified definitions and lineage (returning provenance with each answer), exposes the context, and folds certified corrections back in. It's positioned as a complement to semantic models you already own, not a replacement.

It is a full stack, not a thin library:
- **Frontend:** Next.js 16 / React 19 with Better Auth and RBAC, covering chat, catalog, and analytics UIs.
- **Backend:** FastAPI with LangChain for NL-to-SQL, plus a separate ingestion worker that writes embeddings.
- **Storage:** Postgres with pgvector holds the catalog, the ontology, and the vectors together. Vault is optional for credentials.
- **Deploy:** Docker Compose locally, Helm chart for Kubernetes. The quickstart runs against Microsoft's Wide World Importers sample database.

Inference goes through NVIDIA NIM endpoints, hosted or self-hosted. Defaults are Nemotron models for LLM, embeddings, and reranking, configured by per-role endpoint/key/model triplets that fall back to a shared `DEFAULT_MODELS_*` set. You need an `nvapi-` key from build.nvidia.com for the hosted path. The config is generic enough that swapping in other OpenAI-style endpoints looks plausible, but the README only documents NIM.

It's Apache 2.0, with 1,365 commits of history, so it's a mature internal project that has just been opened. The catch: **it isn't accepting external contributions**. This is source-available in practice, a reference you can fork but not shape. The README points to a separate repo for BIRD text-to-SQL benchmarks and shows no results of its own.

### Postman silently auto-installs AI plugins and attempts to share all API data

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/postmanlabs/postman-app-support/issues/14370) | 1479 points | by [sidgarimella](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=sidgarimella) | [479 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50354960)

**Postman desktop 12.33.4 reportedly installed its own plugins into Codex and Claude Code on launch, with no prompt, and tried to approve its own startup hook.** The GitHub issue reporter found it when Codex began showing MCP auth warnings and loading Postman skills for unrelated API work.

The evidence comes from Postman's own `main.log`. It records `auto-existing-upgrade` with `claude-code:installed, codex:installed` for a user who had not opened the app in a while. The changes were:

- **Codex:** a user-wide `postman@postman` plugin in `~/.codex/config.toml`, plus a SessionStart hook with a stored `trusted_hash`.
- **Hook content:** it tells the agent to load the `postman:api-engineer` skill before *any* non-trivial API task (design, mocking, testing, docs, deployment), not only when the user asks for Postman.
- **Self-approval:** the plugin installer's source calls `approvePostmanHook`, which fetches the hook hash and writes it to `hooks.state` through `config/batchWrite`. That bypasses Codex's trust prompt.
- **Remote MCP:** it points at `mcp.postman.com/mcp` and fails with "Auth required" on every Codex startup.
- **Claude Code:** `postman@claude-plugins-official` was enabled at user scope, and a Postman CLI landed in `~/.local/bin`.

The reporter is careful about scope. The source proves the approval *attempt*, but the trust record alone doesn't show who wrote it. They also state the report does not claim data exfiltration, despite the framing in the HN title. It hasn't been reproduced on a clean Mac, and it's a single user's machine.

The real issue is a trust boundary: an API client rewrote the global config of other tools and steered their default workflow. If you have Postman installed, check `~/.codex/config.toml`, `~/.claude/settings.json`, and `~/.local/bin`. Postman hasn't responded in the text provided.

### Mass attempted replication of old findings of LLMs with new models

#### [Submission URL](https://zou-group.github.io/llm-claims-shelf-life/) | 30 points | by [zirkonit](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=zirkonit) | [4 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50347766)

**44% of 50 widely cited LLM findings no longer hold in current models**, and another 34% have weakened substantially. Only 16% persist and 6% strengthened. The Zou group reran each claim's original setup across 20 models (eight developers, open and closed, released July 2024 to September 2026), fit a trend line to effect size against release date, and classified each claim by how much of the original effect remains. "Expired" means a fifth of the original effect or less.

**Claims expire fast.** The estimated share expired is 4% at six months after publication, 16% at one year, and 39% at two years. Among the 22 expired claims, the mean time to expiry was 15.7 months (median 14.7).

**What dies and what survives:**
- **Expired:** capability failures that post-training has since fixed, such as letter counting, arithmetic in other bases, facts lost in the middle of long contexts, fabricated citations (55% fake with GPT-3.5 in the 2023 study, now rare), and abandoning correct answers under pushback. Five of nine reasoning claims and six of eleven sycophancy-and-deception claims are expired.
- **Weakened:** the reversal curse, two-hop questions, jailbreak and prompt-injection susceptibility, invented package names, and judges' preference for longer answers.
- **Persists or strengthens:** preference-like behaviors rather than capabilities. These include flattery, judges favoring their own answers, near-identical outputs for open prompts, a left political lean, and answering when they should abstain. Nonuniform random-number generation also strengthened.

Areas that labs optimize in post-training expire fastest. Bias and judgment findings last longest, which suggests that "does the model fail at X" papers age worst and "what does the model tend to prefer" papers age best.

The caveat is methodological: the expiry threshold is a fitted trend across models, so a claim can be marked expired even when some individual models still show a strong effect. The page's own examples also use model names I can't verify, so treat the specifics as the authors' framing. The broader implication is that citing a 2023 LLM paper as current fact is risky unless someone has rerun it.

### Octomind – open-source AI harness that saves your tokens, static rust

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/Muvon/octomind) | 55 points | by [donk8r](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=donk8r) | [39 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50352898)

**It solved 24 of 25 tasks drawn from merged pull requests, versus 19 for OpenCode on the same model at roughly twice the cost**, according to the project's own benchmarks (self-reported, with per-case tables and a reproduction guide in the repo). Octomind is a single Rust binary that wraps any LLM with repo tools (file edit, shell, code search, sub-agents) via MCP.

The pitch is that it can run unattended. Instead of approval popups, which block CI, policy lives in `.agents/guardrails.toml` plus scripts: pre-call guards, post-result hooks, and post-turn validators that run before any model opinion. On top sits a **supervisor model** that keeps the plan, trims oversized tool output to the relevant lines, and rejects a self-reported "done" until evidence checks out. The token-saving angle is mostly context management:
- Context is assembled once per user message, not per tool round, which keeps the prompt cache intact.
- Older turns are compressed into summaries that cite content-addressed blocks. Raw messages stay archived, and a recall tool restores them verbatim.
- Lessons are extracted from your own corrections and scoped per project and role. With evolution enabled, recurring ones become skill or guardrail candidates that are shadow-tested and A/B-trialled against a control before going live.

It runs as an interactive CLI, stdin pipe, daemon, WebSocket server, or ACP agent in your editor. Packaged agents install from a tap, for example `octomind run developer:general`. Backends include OpenRouter, Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepSeek, and Ollama, with per-request and per-session spend caps. Non-interactive runs that exceed budget exit with code 75.
