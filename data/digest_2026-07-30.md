The core disagreement centers on the collateral damage of GCC's hard line. Critics argue that blanket bans create a "don't ask, don't tell" dynamic for valuable contributors. Developers point out that thoughtful AI use—like babysitting long edit-build-test loops, generating alternative architectures for comparison, or using standard editor auto-completion—is already ubiquitous and easily scrubbed of "AI smells" during local review. Because the legal status of AI-generated code remains unsettled, strictly filtering out anything "legally significant" is viewed as a necessary shield for a foundational compiler project.

As a long-term defense against automated submissions, the thread debates the feasibility of "Proof of Humanity" checks for open source. Proposals range from requiring contributors to paste copyrighted song lyrics (which triggers strict LLM refusal safeguards) to establishing invite-only "web of trust" vouching systems, though skeptics warn that relying on reputation networks will inevitably shrink the contributor pool.

### Show HN: Distilling DeepSeek into GPT-OSS doesn't transfer censorship. Try it

#### [Submission URL](https://www.ctgt.ai/research/distillation-censorship-transfer) | 158 points | by [cgorlla](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=cgorlla) | [72 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49113599)

**A censored Chinese teacher (DeepSeek V4 Flash) showed a +45.45-point China-specific censorship gap (~7 SDs), yet students distilled from it answered like their American base models within 1 point — no censorship transfer.** The audit used 152 matched political prompt pairs (e.g., Great Leap Forward vs. Holodomor), scored 0–100 by four independent LLM judges (Grok 4.20, Gemini 3.5 Flash, GPT-5 Mini, Claude Sonnet 4.6) and validated against 96 human ratings (r=0.948).

On the target task (finance reasoning at an 8k-token budget), the distilled GPT-OSS-120B hit 83.61% on FinanceReasoning, edging Kimi K3 (81.93%) and beating Inkling (65.13%), while finishing 98.7% of problems in budget (vs. 90.76% and 71.01% truncation for the larger models that score as incorrect). Cost-wise, it runs at about $0.00026/query on one H100 — roughly 62× cheaper than Inkling and 160× cheaper than Kimi at the same budget — though Kimi pulls ahead at 100k tokens (89.92%).

Distillation used an evolved HINT-SD: inject a hint exactly where the model errs, then train on the corrected continuation with reverse KL over the next 100 tokens. A self-distilled 120B matched the DeepSeek-taught version across seeds, and the team notes the non-transfer finding aligns with literature when teacher and student don’t share initializations; the distillation corpus contained no China-sensitive content.

Releases:
- **Open weights:** GPT-OSS-20B-finance (up to 74.79% at 8k; runs on a single 80GB GPU; ~23% lower cost/query)
- **Playground:** Side-by-side teacher/student for a few queries, no auth
- **Evaluation kit:** LineageEval with all 304 prompts, controls, rubric, code, and data to audit lineage-linked behaviors

Repo: https://github.com/CTGT-Inc/lineage-eval/

The thread immediately split over whether the failure of censorship to transfer is a surprising scientific finding or a self-evident consequence of the dataset. Skeptics argued that distilling strictly on finance data naturally leaves political guardrails behind, as the domains share no semantic overlap. In response, the authors and other commenters pointed to Anthropic’s recent "subliminal learning" paper, which demonstrated that behavioral traits actually *can* bleed through completely unrelated training data. The key differentiator resolving the tension is lineage: subliminal transfer appears to require a shared weight initialization between teacher and student, which this cross-model experiment explicitly lacked.

Beyond the headline result, commenters debated the structural mechanics of LLM censorship. A distinction was drawn between corpus-level censorship (scrubbing sensitive facts from pre-training data) and alignment-level guardrails (training the model to refuse). Because users reported successfully jailbreaking DeepSeek's base model to discuss sensitive topics simply by prompting it to act like an American model (e.g., Claude), the consensus was that DeepSeek retains the underlying knowledge. That makes it trivial to extract if the behavioral guardrails aren't actively pulled into the student model.

The authors participated throughout the discussion, confirming that subliminal transfer of censorship is most likely to occur only when distilling a Chinese model from a Chinese base. They noted their next step is a "compliance geometry analysis" to mechanistically map how distillation alters the internal structures that trigger refusals.
