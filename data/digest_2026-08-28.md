**A reimplemented SwiftUI engine compiled to WebAssembly streams a binary draw‑command protocol to a JS Canvas host**, resolving layout up front and reconciling only views that read live state each frame. The host rasterizes layers once to offscreen canvases keyed by content hashes, then composites on later frames, yielding smooth, install‑free iOS‑style previews in the browser. Apps link against Dactyl’s SwiftUI (not Apple’s) and can reach a broad surface area—Charts, SpriteKit, SceneKit, RealityKit, ARKit, MapKit, StoreKit, MusicKit, PencilKit, WidgetKit, UIKit, CoreGraphics, Metal, Vision.

SwiftWasm’s static linking would force shipping the ~100 MB engine on every edit, so Dactyl splits the build: a content‑addressed base module (engine + stdlib) is fetched/compiled once; your app ships as a tiny position‑independent side module (8 KB–300 KB) that hot‑swaps under the running preview. Imports are rebound, Swift type metadata is registered, and @State is snapshotted and restored so scroll position and text fields survive code edits.

On Android, the same engine compiles natively (aarch64-unknown-linux-android24) into a shared library. A Jetpack Compose host drives it over JNI and reads the command stream directly from native memory (no copy). Where the web paints pixels, Compose mounts real Material controls at the resolved rectangles—with native focus, ripple, keyboard, and accessibility—pins density to the engine’s layout scale for exact alignment, and maps SF Symbols to Material Symbols via a generated table. Per‑app APKs stamp a template with the shared library, app name, and icon. iPad is just a different device descriptor; viewport, safe areas, and size classes change and SwiftUI adapts.

Engine development is automated: agents build an API reference from Apple SDK artifacts, render scenes in a booted iOS simulator, and pixel‑diff Dactyl’s output against the screenshots; when a new iOS ships, they re‑render the ground truth. Screenshots miss motion, so a filmstrip runner exercises moving UI.

The throughline: keep SwiftUI as the single layout truth, stream a compact draw protocol, and use native controls where possible—avoiding React Native/Flutter/webview compromises while making Swift code previewable on the web and shippable to Android.

### EPA says power for data centers can sidestep pollution laws

#### [Submission URL](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-issues-permitting-guidance-further-president-trumps-agenda-promoting-data-centers) | 255 points | by [Levitating](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=Levitating) | [254 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49478103)

**EPA guidance carves out “islanded” on‑site data center power plants from the Clean Air Act’s Acid Rain Program** — so long as they neither sell electricity nor trigger DOE generating‑unit reporting. The agency frames this as enabling faster siting and build‑outs while keeping utility customers insulated from new costs, tying it to President Trump’s expanded Ratepayer Protection Pledge that requires companies to pay the full cost of their energy and supporting infrastructure. The policy is explicitly linked to maintaining U.S. “artificial intelligence dominance” and reducing strain on community grids. If an islanded facility later connects to the grid, ARP provisions may then apply, limiting the exemption.

The debate fractures over whether AI companies exploiting the Clean Air Act exemption represents blatant corporate villainy or a rational bypass of broken infrastructure permitting. Critics view the maneuver as an abuse of a regulatory technicality that forces the public to absorb the negative externalities of private AI development. Conversely, defenders argue that AI companies are constrained by time rather than money, and are leveraging the loophole specifically to escape years of bureaucratic gridlock and NIMBY-driven deployment delays.

The thread’s most clarifying insight traces the origin of the loophole itself. Commenters note that the carve-out was originally intended for emergency backup generators—like those for municipal sewage lift stations—and temporary or highly remote industrial sites. Policymakers historically left the exemption open under the assumption that massive, permanent commercial operations would always naturally prefer the economics of grid connection. The consensus suggests that the AI industry’s hyper-capitalized, time-desperate push for compute has simply broken a 25-year-old regulatory assumption, turning a pragmatic safety valve into an industrial-scale loophole.
