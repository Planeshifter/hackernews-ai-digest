### Sub-1-Bit LLM Compression via Latent Factorization

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/SamsungLabs/LittleBit) | 87 points | by [brainless](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=brainless) | [26 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50005608)

**The factorization recipe targets 0.1 bits per weight:** each dense matrix is split into low-rank latent factors, those factors are binarized, and learned scales restore magnitude information while leaving the model architecture unchanged at inference.

The follow-up, LittleBit-2, adds opt-in Joint-ITQ initialization to better align the latent factors with the binary hypercube; it changes initialization, not inference. The repository includes QAT training and evaluation code for OPT, Llama, Phi, Qwen, and Gemma models. Reproducing the paper results calls for Transformers 4.51.x, and the code is licensed CC BY-NC 4.0.

The discussion centers on whether pushing quantization below roughly 4 bits per weight (bpw) is fundamentally viable, with commenters broadly skeptical of sub-1-bit schemes for production use.

**The ~4 bpw "hard floor"**
One camp argued that compressing models below 4 bpw inevitably leads to catastrophic capability loss. While a low-bit architecture can theoretically act as a universal function approximator, squashing a dense FP16 model down via post-training quantization destroys information: model capacity scales with total bits (Shannon entropy), meaning a 4B parameter model at 1 bpw simply holds the same capacity as a 1B model at 4 bpw. In this view, spending training FLOPs on a high-precision model only to aggressively prune its capacity is an inefficient dead end.

**QAT, selective compression, and counterexamples**
Defenders of ultra-low-bit approaches made crucial distinctions:
- **Training paradigm matters:** Post-training quantization (PTQ) falls apart, but Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) calculates loss against the actual low-precision states. Commenters pointed to ternary models like Prism ML's Bonsai-2-27B and non-LLM implementations (e.g., Moondream's Parakeet ASR at 1.58 bits) as evidence that native low-bit training can work.
- **Non-uniform quantization:** Blanket quantization fails, but isolated outliers can be preserved while compressing the residue. Several commenters argued the near-term future is hybrid: leaving early or critical layers at higher precision (e.g., 3-bit or 4-bit) and aggressively crushing later layers, or isolating "spiky" parameters before factorizing the rest.

**Targeting the wrong bottleneck?**
A recurring critique was that consumer hardware enthusiasts are chasing the wrong axis. Instead of crushing dense models to 0.1 bits and watching Wikitext-2 performance collapse from 80% to 47%, users are currently better served by streaming optimizations: running smaller, high-quantization models that don't hallucinate tool calls, or offloading sparsely activated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) parameters to system RAM or fast NVMe storage. Commenters viewed the paper less as an immediately deployable tool and more as a theoretical milestone proving latent factor alignment can extract signal at previously unusable bit budgets.
