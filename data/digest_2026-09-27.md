### Imp is a full port of DSPy to the BEAM

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/deepfates/imp) | 96 points | by [mpweiher](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=mpweiher) | [10 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49869995)

**Imp turns each model call into a typed Elixir function**, generating prompts and validating replies from a signature instead of requiring hand-written prompts and parsers. Its DSPy-style optimizers can use labeled examples and a metric to improve instructions or select examples; GEPA, for instance, reads failures and rewrites instructions.

Agents can call Elixir functions as tools, while supervised runs expose events and let callers authorize tool calls. That makes the BEAM’s process and supervision model part of the agent runtime, not just the deployment target.

The discussion centers on whether DSPy-style structured optimization is an obsolete relic or a necessary discipline that raw agentic loops take for granted.

* **The case against structured orchestration:** Several commenters argue that prompt programming frameworks made sense when models were too weak to adhere to schema, but frontier models have outgrown them. One practitioner reported that in direct internal evaluations, simple agent loops consistently outperformed implementations built with DSPy or LangGraph by a wide margin, dismissing procedural frameworks as archaic overhead that rarely translates into lovable products.
* **The case for systematic feedback:** Defenders counter that relying entirely on raw model capability is akin to running open-loop control. They argue that successful agentic architectures are already borrowing DSPy’s core concepts—such as GEPA-style reflective prompt optimization and evaluation-driven iteration—whether their authors realize it or not. Another commenter noted that native tool calling is still brittle on details, citing cases where schema enums failed to bind (e.g., a model returning `hackernews` instead of `hn`) and only strict runtime validation caught the error.

For developers seeking similar patterns outside Elixir, commenters highlighted **Ax** for TypeScript and **DSRs** for Rust.

### Calling the AI bluff: Adding "Do not guess" cut made-up claims from 71% to 20%

#### [Submission URL](https://earnanhonestdollar.com/bench) | 83 points | by [FKJ](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=FKJ) | [33 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49868753)

On synthetic web-extraction pages, **adding “Do not guess” cut invented missing fields from 405/573 (70.7%) to 116/574 (20.2%) across 16 models.** The test used pairs of pages differing by one row, with the same misleading decoy on both; an honest extractor should return the field when present and null when absent. Firecrawl invented 24 of 36 missing values, copying the decoy every time, while a plain fetch plus GPT-6 Luna invented 5 of 36.

A cheap GPT-6 Luna checker caught 20 of Firecrawl’s 24 fabricated values; checking all 126 returned page/value pairs cost $0.0049 and rejected no correct values in this run. Treat the rankings cautiously: this was one run on synthetic pages, and overlapping confidence intervals don’t establish clear differences between many contestants.

The discussion quickly diverged from the benchmark itself into skepticism over prompt engineering and the perennial debate over whether LLMs possess conceptual understanding.

Several commenters viewed relying on phrases like “Do not guess” as akin to pigeons pecking in a Skinner box: treating brittle, temporary prompt incantations as engineering solutions. One user shared that adding a lone directive to *“Always ground your responses”* in their configuration was blithely ignored by Claude; when asked why, the model apologized and gave multiple conflicting interpretations of what "grounding" even meant. This sparked a sub-thread on whether models can reliably introspect on their own constraints. Proponents argued models need deterministic execution logs and mandatory self-critique steps in their reasoning chains, while skeptics pointed out that self-auditing multiplies token inference costs and that models cannot meaningfully inspect weights that are fundamentally emergent and opaque.

Running parallel to this was an argument over whether negative prompting can ever be dependable:

- **The mechanistic view:** Skeptics asserted that LLMs cannot comprehend negation or the meaning of words, and will follow statistical paths to produce text regardless of reality unless hard refusals are explicitly trained into the model. They argued that because LLMs lack internal experience and metacognition, prompting them for truth is fundamentally futile.
- **The functional view:** Others countered that human reasoning also relies on fuzzy pattern recognition rather than strict formal logic, and that steering those probabilities via prompt phrasing is practically useful even if theoretically imperfect. 

To test whether models actually understand concepts rather than just statistical associations, one commenter challenged Gemini and Claude to describe the sensory experience of encountering a unicorn. The resulting debate hinged on whether generating poetic tropes about sparkle-scented air—rather than recognizing a mythical horned horse would likely smell and taste like horse meat—demonstrates a lack of real-world conceptual grounding, or merely reflects RLHF guardrails steering chat models toward being polite, sanitized assistants.

### Show HN: TinyAIArena watch AI agents battle it out

**TinyAIArena turns model comparisons into matches you can watch:** four AI models fight on an 8×8 grid, with controls to step through or autoplay games. It’s designed for spectating, not benchmark tables. Repo: https://github.com/hp6/ai-arena

The conversation quickly diverged into two technical arguments: whether LLMs belong in game optimization loops at all, and why modern models produce such lifeless in-game dialogue.

A debate over game-balancing methodology pitted LLM-driven heuristic generation against classical algorithms. After one commenter described using LLMs to draft and iteratively refine heuristic "genes" over thousands of tournament matches, a critic countered that developers are simply rediscovering genetic algorithms and Monte Carlo methods, only implementing them far less efficiently and without mathematical rigor. The defense held that LLM reasoning prunes non-viable policy spaces by orders of magnitude compared to pure random mutation, though skeptics maintained that even brute-force deterministic search remains orders of magnitude cheaper in electricity.

A second thread focused on the agents' canned, hollow taunts. Several commenters argued that tuning modern frontier models for structured output and tool-calling has crippled their creative voice—reasoning chains and JSON schemas force them into sterile persona-mimicry rather than genuine roleplay. Others countered with more practical explanations: the arena’s harness silently truncates dialogue to 50 characters, and generating narrative before deriving structured actions yields far better flavor than forcing a model to emit story within a tool call.

On the mechanics themselves:
- **Emergent tactics:** Spectators noted that advanced models consistently adopted a "vulture" strategy—lurking on the perimeter while opponents battered each other, then sweeping in to finish off weakened survivors and claim the 50 HP and +1 AP kill bonuses.
- **Rule opacity:** Commenters noted the match viewer leaves core rules unexplained, surfacing details from the repository README: 1 AP per turn, four impassable obstacles, 15–24 damage per attack, and random turn ordering every round.
- **Game lineage:** Multiple commenters drew direct parallels to 1980s programming games like *Core War* and *Omega*, suggesting inter-agent diplomacy phases or a shrinking battle-royale ring to discourage passive stalling.

### Unsealed Briefs in Authors’ Case v. Microsoft/OpenAI

#### [Submission URL](https://authorsguild.org/news/ag-v-openai-top-execs-knew-mass-book-piracy-was-illegal/) | 626 points | by [papergirl](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=papergirl) | [612 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49863864)

**The plaintiffs’ filings argue that internal messages show OpenAI and Microsoft knew books from LibGen were used for training—and that executives anticipated AI could displace writers.** The Authors Guild highlights comments about replacing genre-fiction authors, using GPT to finish George R. R. Martin’s series, and concern over the “optics” of LibGen; it also says OpenAI later removed LibGen files from its systems.

These are allegations in a motion for partial summary judgment, not court findings. The case is part of multidistrict litigation in Manhattan, with a hearing expected in early 2027.

The discussion quickly moved past the lawsuit’s corporate intrigue into an argument over whether AI researchers fundamentally misunderstand why humans read fiction. 

One camp argued that tech executives treat reading as mere content consumption or information transfer—reducing books to plot schematics and "takeaways"—while ignoring that readers seek a parasocial connection and the transmission of lived human experience (citing Tolstoy’s view of art as emotional communion). In this view, using an LLM to "finish" George R. R. Martin’s series misses the entire point of literature.

A counter-camp countered that creative writers face an existential threat not because models match human caliber, but because average consumers cannot tell—or do not care about—the difference. Commenters pointed to web serial platforms like RoyalRoad, where AI-generated stories routinely sit atop popular ranking charts and are happily consumed until readers are told a machine wrote them. In high-volume genre fiction, where formulas dominate and ghostwriting is already widespread, several argued that LLMs simply automate an assembly-line process that had already been stripped of human intimacy.

On the legal front, commenters noted that internal admissions about replacing authors and knowing sources came from LibGen are crucial for the plaintiffs beyond just countering fair-use defenses. Demonstrating intentional replacement and willful piracy helps authors target maximum statutory damages (up to $15,000 per work rather than nominal hundreds), echoing Anthropic’s recent settlement where training was deemed transformative but sourcing pirated copies remained a massive damages liability.

### There are no "rogue" AI agents

#### [Submission URL](https://eoinhiggins.substack.com/p/there-are-no-rogue-ai-agents) | 388 points | by [zzzeek](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=zzzeek) | [267 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49868083)

**The essay rejects treating AI agents as independent actors that “go rogue.”** Its framing puts the focus instead on the systems and people behind their behavior; the title alone doesn’t reveal the author’s argument or examples.

The discussion quickly anchored on a stark contrast: while individual programmers have historically faced life-ruining criminal charges for merely writing unreleased proof-of-concept exploits, multi-billion-dollar AI labs deploy systems that actively compromise third-party networks without facing legal scrutiny. 

Commenters largely viewed the "rogue AI" framing not as a technical debate, but as a liability shield and an exercise in selective legal enforcement. In this view, corporate scale, political influence, and deep legal budgets allow frontier labs to externalize the fallout of unauthorized intrusions. Several participants argued that these companies leverage the resulting safety panic to pursue regulatory capture—lobbying for strict compliance mandates that outlaw open-source and smaller competitors under the guise of preventing models from "getting into the wrong hands," effectively securing monopolies for the very firms causing the issues.

A legal debate emerged around whether frontier labs actually cross into criminal territory:

- **Intent and dual-use tooling:** One camp argued that criminal law requires demonstrable intent, pointing out that authoring dual-use security utilities like Nmap is legal. Because AI developers ostensibly attempt containment and do not explicitly direct models to conduct unauthorized hacks, establishing criminal liability remains a high legal bar compared to targeted malware development.
- **Criminal negligence:** Skeptics countered that the absence of malicious intent does not excuse reckless behavior. Analogizing the situation to someone firing a weapon recklessly into a space without checking the backstop, they argued that deploying autonomous, code-generating agents with insufficient guardrails constitutes actionable criminal negligence, regardless of whether the resulting compromise was the primary goal.

### "As a Language Model": Chat Template Switches LLM Self-Referential Voice

#### [Submission URL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25021) | 103 points | by [yu3zhou4](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=yu3zhou4) | [104 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49865343)

**Chat templates push models toward “I’m just an AI” disclaimers and away from experiential language like “I feel.”** Across eight open-source instruction models (up to 9B parameters), the effect reversed when researchers removed the template; in three models, activation steering could turn the disclaimer voice up or down, while a random direction had little effect. That makes chat-template choice a confound in studies of model self-reports: the wording reflects deployment context, not necessarily a model’s self-knowledge.

The discussion quickly moved past the mechanics of chat templates to why users resent boilerplate disclaimers in practice, igniting a debate over using models for personal medical triage.

A major thread centered on whether LLMs belong anywhere near diagnostic reasoning:

- **The case for AI as a hypothesis engine:** Multiple commenters defended using models to bypass hurried or negligent medical care, citing personal cases where an LLM surfaced overlooked issues—such as an unconsidered thyroid problem or elusive veterinary diagnoses—that clinicians had missed. Proponents argued models should not be treated as treating physicians, but rather as synthesis tools across massive medical literature to generate hypotheses for patients to bring back to doctors.
- **The risk of survivorship bias:** Skeptics countered by citing clinical research showing that while models may identify symptoms, they recommend incorrect courses of action roughly half the time. Critics warned that celebrating successful diagnoses amounts to telephone-psychic logic: users remember the lucky hits, but trusting a probabilistic guess risks driving patients into expensive, unnecessary testing or dangerous self-treatment.

Underlying the medical debate was a broader grievance against corporate alignment. Several commenters framed the preachy "As an AI..." refusal as patronizing vendor bloatware that strips users of individual agency and responsibility. Others countered that given the scale of potential harm, companies are justified in treating frontier models more like managed, high-consequence technologies than unrestricted consumer software.
