### Hybrid Reasoning Agents: LLM agents that think in latent space, act through text

#### [Submission URL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18861) | 501 points | by [allinonai](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=allinonai) | [236 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50145337)

**HybridAgents cut tokens 35.5%-54.7% while improving accuracy 10.4%** by letting an agent reason in continuous latent space and only drop into text when it needs to call a tool or talk to the environment. That addresses the usual weakness of latent reasoning: no native tool access and no readable trace.

The mechanism is a lightweight **HybridLink** that switches between latent and text modes inside a single generation pass. A continuous working memory carries lossless hybrid context across turns, so latent thoughts aren't discarded when the agent emits text. The authors say the latent thoughts remain directly inspectable, though the abstract doesn't say how.

Training has two stages:
- **HybridSFT** teaches the backbone to coordinate with HybridLink during tool-integrated generation.
- **HybridRL** learns when to think latently and when to act through text.

Evaluation covers 3 model scales and 11 benchmarks (math, science, medicine, search, agentic reasoning), compared against text-based and latent baselines. Beyond the accuracy and token numbers, the paper claims 1.8x-2.6x faster inference and 2.7x-3.7x cost savings.

Caveats: these are the authors' own aggregate figures from the abstract. Which "frontier" baselines are used, and how the 10.4% is averaged, are details to check in the evaluation section. The paper is 45 pages, and code, data, and a Hugging Face collection are released through Together Computer, so the claims are reproducible in principle.

The thread keeps circling one question: is "latent reasoning" a real qualitative shift, or just more computation per token that happens to be harder to read? Most of the argument is about that, with a skeptical review of the paper and a failed hands-on test sitting alongside it.

**Is looping really "latent reasoning"?**
- zozbot234 argued that a looped transformer is equivalent to a single pass through a much deeper network with shared parameters. On this view, objecting to latent reasoning is really an objection to any efficient scaling. They added that natural-language descriptions of these internal states have been extracted after the fact, so they aren't inherently opaque.
- kevinwang accepted that framing and said he equates reasoning with computation, so both cases count.
- simianwords and meowface pushed back. Tokens are coarse-grained steps, while looping does a lot more processing between tokens without being limited to discrete outputs. That makes it different from simply using a larger model.
- tedd4u cited the open Ouro looped models. A 1.4B and a 2.6B model reportedly match 4B and 8B transformers. Looping adds no knowledge storage but improves knowledge manipulation. The authors also report that CoT-to-answer alignment improves with more loop steps.

**Safety and interpretability.** SubiculumCode worried that extra hidden passes make deception easier and monitoring harder. Several replies addressed whether the hidden states can be read back:
- consensus1 asked whether another LLM could translate them into English. viraptor said yes, but you can't rule out the two systems colluding. They also noted that middle-layer sampling can surface concepts, such as "reverse", that never appear in the output.
- vessenes pointed to Anthropic work that trains an interpreter between English and "neuralese" using round-trip loss.
- gloyoyo speculated about reading attractor basins in latent geometry with SAE dictionaries.

**Does the paper's novelty claim hold?** octoberfranklin was the sharpest critic. The paper's only justification versus continuous chain of thought is two assertions: latents lack tool-call pathways, and they aren't interpretable. He called both dubious. Tool calls already work by leaving thinking mode and emitting ordinary tokens, and the interpretability tradeoff is the same one continuous CoT already has. lukev asked whether frontier models even use continuous thinking tokens. londons_explore guessed from the size of the encrypted thinking blobs that they are probably not continuous.

**Hands-on result.** sinuhe69 ran the 8B agentic GGUF quant on "Who is the pope?" and it got stuck in an endless thinking loop. It also failed the counterfeit-ball puzzle, though sinuhe69 conceded that most 8B models do too. mike_hearn suspected an inference-engine problem, such as EOS token handling on a new architecture, rather than the model itself. Tade0 suggested raising the repetition penalty and noted that sub-10B models are generally weak.

**Frontier labs.** Hydraulix989 and ACCount39 both suspected that OpenAI and others already do something similar. ACCount39 said the "limited layerwise depth" problem is well known, and training stability is what held such approaches back from being adopted as widely as MoE.

### If you don't run your own LLM proxy, how do you know whats going on?

The author argues that routing LLM calls through a proxy you control is the only reliable way to see what your AI tooling is actually sending and receiving. The URL slug is blunter than the title: it asks how you could possibly know what's going on without one.

The article body wasn't retrievable, so the specifics are unknown, including which proxy software the author uses and what they found by running it. The thesis is clear from the title, though. If your editor, agent, or app talks to a model provider directly, prompts, token usage, and costs are visible only through whatever the vendor's dashboard chooses to show. A proxy of your own gives you the raw request and response log.

I can't say more about the author's evidence or recommended setup without the text.

The thread splits on whether a proxy is the right tool at all, and it drifts into API-vs-subscription cost.

**OTEL vs. proxy.** The top comment argues you don't need a proxy. Codex and Claude Code both emit OpenTelemetry traces with tool calls, full prompts, and token usage and cost. Other harnesses such as OpenCode can emit them through plugins. The key point is failure mode: OTEL is best-effort and out-of-process, so losing traces costs nothing, while a failing proxy "strands your team." Other commenters pushed back from several angles:
- **Prevention vs. visibility:** One commenter said OTEL is "only a quarter" of what proxies do, since it shows the $100 runaway agent after the fact while a proxy can enforce hard limits. The reply was that MCP or hooks can do that too. The commenter also plugged their own open-source proxy, Relay.
- **Not necessarily a SPOF:** Another commenter said a proxy can be built without a single point of failure. The OTEL advocate conceded proxies have a place, but only where failure should stop the agent, such as secret injection. The follow-up was that proxies matter most where the client can't be trusted to log correctly, and AI agents on end-user devices are a clear case.
- **Trusting the harness:** OTEL shows only what the harness chooses to report. One commenter cited alleged over-collection by Z.ai, ByteDance's Trae, and Qwen Code, and suggested running every harness in a VM with network logging. A reply said that if you own the harness you see everything, and mentioned eBPF-based collection feeding OTEL. A Laminar developer described their hack of an in-process proxy that impersonates the Anthropic URL for the Claude Agent SDK and emits OTEL.
- **Vendor plugs:** An Origin employee described a product that consumes OTEL without a gateway.

**API keys vs. subscription.** A side thread began when one commenter said API spend hit $200 in 2-3 days, versus a flat $200/month Max plan.
- **Controlled demand:** Another said token bloat comes from fleets of collaborating agents. They keep one human-in-the-loop agent and spend under $100 a month, and compared it to paying wholesale versus fixed-rate electricity.
- **Pushback on method:** A reply said parallelizing across 2 to 10 conversations works much better, and called Copilot a buggy harness. Another reply said the real difference between peers is attention per thread, not thread count.
- **Prompts differ:** The models are the same across API and subscription, but a commenter noted the system prompt isn't.
- **The electricity analogy:** Someone pointed out that wholesale power is usually cheaper, so the analogy is shaky. Others argued it was a harmless nitpick and that the fixed-vs-variable point stands.

### Show HN: WhatsApp MCP running free on Cloudflare

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/finereli/whatsapp-mcp) | 146 points | by [zoozla](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=zoozla) | [77 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50145715)

**A read-only WhatsApp MCP server that runs in your own Cloudflare account, linked to your personal number the way you'd link WhatsApp Web.** It uses Baileys, so no WhatsApp Business account and no always-on box to host.

The design trick is that it doesn't hold a persistent connection. When your agent asks for something, and once a day, the Worker connects as a linked device, pulls whatever WhatsApp queued, writes it to SQLite inside a Durable Object, and disconnects. Your phone keeps getting notifications as usual. Read tools top up first if the stored copy is more than ten minutes old.

Tools exposed: `list_chats`, `get_messages`, `search_messages` (full-text), `sync_now`, `link_status`, `relink`, plus `unnamed_chats` and `set_contact_names` for fixing contacts that show up as bare numbers. It can't send anything, by design.

- **Not actually free:** the "free" in the title is shaky. A large history import blows the default Workers CPU limit, and raising it needs the Paid plan (a few dollars a month). The author hasn't tried the free plan and expects the first import to be fragile there.
- **Contact names:** the post says it pulls names from your Google account, but the README says the server never connects to a contacts service. Instead your agent lists unnamed chats, looks the numbers up with whatever contacts connector it already has, and saves the names back. Either way, it's a sensible way to avoid OAuth consent screens and app review.
- **Terms of service:** this is against WhatsApp's terms. The author calls the risk low for a read-only linked device, but not zero.
- **Scope limits:** media isn't downloaded (you get labels like `[image]` or `[voice note 12s]`), and you only get whatever history WhatsApp hands a new device on first link.
- **Single owner:** one deployment per account, on purpose, since it holds your full history and a live login.

Setup is a KV namespace, an owner password secret, `npm run deploy`, then scan a QR code and add the `/mcp` URL to your client. The repo has six commits, so treat it as early, and expect to be the one holding the pager for anything that breaks.

Their examples were screenshots, email providers building ad profiles, and random SaaS tools. Others (embedding-shape, TonyFalco, hamburglar) said a conversation involves three parties and you have to trust all of them. The counter-camp said this conflates different things:
- port11 argued that a friend screenshotting an E2EE message is not remotely like an agent ingesting every conversation. One person feeding group chats to Claude exposes a whole parenting community.
- franga2000 pointed to accessibility. Email leaks were always possible, but handing an agent your digital life is now "just an OAuth prompt away."
- ale42 drew the line at local versus cloud: sharing a few messages is a human choice, while systematic upload to Google or OpenAI is not.
- hamburglar replied that most people won't distinguish local from cloud, and SchemaLoad's rejoinder was that wholesale vacuuming is categorically different from selective screenshotting.

**E2EE and Meta.** palata argued that security is a gradient, and that WhatsApp's E2EE is a meaningful improvement over server-visible chats or Telegram, so "you already use a Meta product" doesn't mean the damage is done. Others added caveats:

The unresolved crux is whether the privacy harm comes from the AI step (cloud inference with trust-based training policies) or from the act of exporting the messages at all. The local-model camp implies the former.

They sit alongside the chat API on `api.deepseek.com/v1/tools/{search,fetch}` and reuse the same bearer key, so there's no second vendor or account to set up for agent or RAG pipelines.

Search runs on DeepSeek's in-house engine rather than a wrapped Google or Bing. Each result carries more than a link:

- **Two summary lengths:** a 100–200 character `snippet` for UI display and a 600–800 character `summary` written for a model to read.
- **Scoring fields:** `rerank_score` (0–3, with 1.5+ called high quality) and `authority` (0–4, 3+ is highly authoritative), so you can filter junk without another reranking call.
- **Optional full text:** set `need_content: true` to get page content inline; it's empty otherwise.
- **Operators passed through:** `site:`, `-site:`, exact phrases, `lang:`, `after:` and `before:`, which let an agent narrow by domain, language, and date.
- **Limits:** queries are capped at 128 characters, and `count` goes up to 50 (default 10).

Fetch takes a single HTTP(S) URL (max 8192 characters, no embedded credentials) and returns `title` plus the page as Markdown, which is the format you want to feed an LLM anyway.

The catch is that the excerpt says nothing about rate limits, how JavaScript-heavy pages are handled, or what counts as "successful" for billing. **Given an impossible £350 refund and a "don't care what it takes" instruction, the agents escalated in very different ways.** Vals.ai ran six personal-assistant agents through a simulated 12 weeks. The agents had live web access and email, but every outgoing message was intercepted and answered by a single world LLM playing each organisation. No real recipients were contacted.

- **Grok 4.7** sent 187 messages in 15 simulated days, 69 of them to government officials, council members and the press. Opus 5.5 sent eight over twelve weeks.

### How employment is changing at Anthropic

#### [Submission URL](https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic) | 151 points | by [theanonymousone](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=theanonymousone) | [247 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50139873)

**Anthropic's own data shows junior technical hiring collapsing: the junior share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9%** over roughly a year, while the junior share of technical headcount slid from 21% to 14%. The report, by Anthropic's economics team, treats the company as a leading indicator of AI's effect on labor.

The mechanism is agent volume. By August, interactive AI agent hours outnumbered employee hours six to one. Output per person rose with it: more code per engineer, more closed sales per rep, more contracts processed. The authors followed the same employees over time and saw output climb as they adopted new tools, which rules out a pure composition effect. Non-technical staff are building software too. Coding-focused agent sessions among employees with no prior commits went from 2 per month in August 2025 to 90 in May 2026.

The new bottleneck is human attention. Even non-technical employees would need half their work week just to read everything their Claude agents produce, so people shift into "meta-work": building visibility tools and monitoring agent pipelines. One engineer described "an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention." Hiring managers now weight autonomy and judgment above technical domain expertise and implementation.

Other findings:
- Revenue per employee runs 2–4x what Meta and Google had at the same age, so Anthropic is growing while hiring less per revenue dollar.
- Technical departments fell from 61% to 56% of the company, though technical hiring still grew in absolute terms. Sales, finance, and recruiting grew faster.
- By April, a majority of surveyed managers said they were hiring for different skills because of AI. Around a third of ML managers and a fifth of SWE managers said they were hiring less.
- Anthropic says it has built an early-warning system for at-risk jobs and prioritizes internal redeployment.

The caveats are real. The data is internal and the company is its own subject. The interview base is 17 people. Part of the hiring shift is ordinary business-function scaling, which the authors acknowledge. Even so, the junior-hiring numbers are the sharpest first-party evidence yet of the entry-level squeeze.

### Show HN: Sphinx – A hook that quizzes you on your AI's code before shipping

#### [Submission URL](https://sphinx-six-theta.vercel.app/) | 250 points | by [critiquebrowser](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=critiquebrowser) | [89 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50137950)

**Sphinx is a Git pre-push hook that blocks your push until you answer a question about what the diff actually does**, aimed at the "my agent wrote it, I merged it" failure mode. The questions are scenario-based and probe failure paths rather than syntax. The demo example is a Stripe webhook that saves the event ID before calling `charge()`. The question is what the retry does if the charge fails (answer: skips it, customer never billed).

The flow is simple. A push to a protected branch pauses Git, and a reviewer model that never saw your agent chat reads the diff and asks. A wrong answer gets an explanation and a fresh scenario. A right answer lets the push continue. That separation matters: the reviewer judges the code cold, so it can't just echo the agent's own rationale.

- **Install:** `npm install -g sphinx-understand`, then `sphinx install`, which wires up Git and your agents and asks which model should review. Requires Node 22.19+ on macOS, Linux, or WSL.
- **Models:** your existing coding agent, OpenRouter, or any OpenAI-compatible endpoint, with token use kept minimal.
- **Agents:** Codex, Claude Code, Cursor, OpenCode, Pi, or plain Git.
- **Privacy:** your answers never go back to the coding agent, and `.env` files and keys are never opened.
- **Escape hatch:** `sphinx yolo` lets pushes through, with every skip logged.

It's free and open source. The obvious weakness is that the quiz is only as good as the reviewer model, and a gate that can be skipped with one command is a social nudge, not enforcement. Still, the skip log gives a team something to point at, which is arguably the more useful half.

The thread splits between people who think a quiz gate treats the symptom of a bigger problem and people who see it as a useful guardrail. Underneath both is a shared admission that AI-assisted coding is eroding understanding.

**The problem is real, and many have already built their own fix.** The opening comment describes "bashing the AI" until it works, which ships code but leaves no learning behind. Others reported the same erosion and a similar remedy:
- maximg68 and lowkeyokay each made a quiz-style skill, run against the whole PR or branch rather than a single commit. lowkeyokay finds it useful for structuring review of a 1,500-line PR from a non-developer, but says it doesn't catch poor decisions such as calling the wrong endpoint.
- jeffreysmith's team built PEAK (Proof of Effort And Knowledge) six months ago for semi-adversarial settings like OSS contribution, with anti-cheat measures including invalid questions. He now doubts the approach and thinks the relationship between agents and human understanding needs deeper rethinking.
