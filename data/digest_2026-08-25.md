### OpenAI Jalapeño: Better than Nvidia Blackwell

#### [Submission URL](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/openai-jalapeno-better-than-nvidia) | 572 points | by [bmulholland](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=bmulholland) | [363 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49434378)

In head-to-head InferenceX runs, **Jalapeño led token throughput per all-in utility MW across almost all scenarios — without Multi‑Token Prediction —** while still feeling snappy: >700 tok/s/user at concurrency 1 on DeepSeek R1. It’s a generalized LLM inference ASIC, not just for OpenAI’s own models, built with Broadcom in ~16 months, using HBM4, and was announced at Hot Chips (they even had Doom running as a party trick). It excelled in both low-latency and high-throughput with single‑token prediction only (no speculative decoding or prefill/decode split); Kimi‑K2.5 and GPT‑OSS hit ~1,400 tok/s/user, and GSM8k accuracy matched Nvidia on the tested models.

OpenAI is optimizing for perf/W because datacenter power is the constraint, so tokens per MW is the scoreboard. Caveats: all figures were provided by OpenAI; SemiAnalysis verified specific InferenceX runs in-lab but didn’t run the full suite and saw no AgentX results, which better capture long‑context, multi‑turn production behavior. And the Blackwell comparison is only part of the story — the fair fight is Nvidia’s HBM4‑based Vera Rubin, which is already shipping, versus Jalapeño that’s still at the engineering‑sample stage — ideally measured on AgentX‑class workloads.

* **The SemiAnalysis credibility gap:** Commenters heavily criticized the source, characterizing the report as "access journalism" acting as an OpenAI press release. Multiple users pointed out that the author shares a house with several other prominent AI commentators, framing the analysis as localized "SF AI scene" hype rather than objective semiconductor journalism.
* **Apples-to-oranges benchmarking:** The thread disputed the article's core comparisons, noting that OpenAI's specialized inference chip was matched against general-purpose silicon. When compared to an inference-focused chip like AMD’s M1950X, Jalapeño is severely outmatched on raw compute (3.4 vs 20 FP8 PFLOPS) and memory bandwidth (15 vs 23 TB/sec), though commenters acknowledged OpenAI's chip operates at a fraction of the power (700W vs 2500W). Users also noted Google's TPUs were noticeably omitted.
* **Hardcoding weights in silicon:** A major sub-thread debated the viability of startups (like Etched, MatX, and Taalas) baking LLM weights directly into chips. Skeptics argued that models become obsolete too quickly to justify physical tapeouts, especially with general-purpose inference costs plummeting—one user cited a 125x cost reduction in 7 months. Proponents countered that using metal masked ROM requires changing only two metal layers, shrinking the manufacturing cycle to 2–3 months and offering massive efficiency gains for slightly older, high-demand models.
* **Datacenter hardware attrition:** The discussion of hardware longevity surfaced insider claims about Nvidia's NVL72/GB200 rack reliability. A user reported industry rumors that ~10% of racks fail on delivery, and that complex backplane or HBM failures result in "dead racks" sitting idle for months due to RMA allocation delays.

### Servus München: Waymo Is Coming to Germany

**Commercial robotaxi rides are slated for Munich by late 2027**, with Waymo beginning high‑definition mapping and validation drives in the coming weeks using trained specialists behind the wheel. Backed by over 20 million autonomous trips and hundreds of thousands of commercial rides each week elsewhere, the company frames Munich as its first planned regular commercial operation in Germany under the country’s existing autonomous driving legal framework. Waymo cites data from over 350 million fully autonomous kilometres showing 16x fewer serious‑injury crashes than human drivers, plus 14x fewer pedestrian‑injury and 6x fewer cyclist‑injury crashes, a safety pitch aimed at regulators and the public. The rollout is deliberately phased: local mapping and on‑road testing now, ongoing engagement with city, state, and federal authorities, then opening to the public if approvals land. Expect local fleet operations and hiring, and messaging that the service will complement public transit and improve accessibility, including for blind and visually impaired riders. The catch is time and regulation—service isn’t immediate—so near‑term sightings will be manually driven Waymo vehicles gathering data across Munich.

- **Transit realities vs. Market fit:** An assumption that Waymo will struggle against Munich’s "world-class" public transit was swiftly debunked. A commenter provided a litany of recent *Brandbriefs* (urgent appeals) from local mayors and transit officials detailing chronic unreliability on the local networks. Combined with the exorbitant cost of a German driver's license (upwards of $5,000 USD), users argue there is a clear local market for premium, door-to-door service, much like Uber's existing presence.
- **The congestion debate:** While optimists hope a shared AV fleet will reduce personal car ownership and clear up street parking, skeptics counter with Jevons paradox. They argue that easier access to rides will induce demand, and competitive pressures will result in empty robotaxis continually circling dense areas to optimize pickup times, ultimately worsening Munich's traffic. 
- **Shifting safety baselines:** Waymo’s regulatory safety pitch relies on collision multipliers benchmarked against American drivers. Commenters point out that European human drivers—and specifically those in countries like Norway—have a substantially higher baseline safety record, which changes the math on how impressive Waymo's current autonomous statistics actually are.
- **EU rollout context:** Munich is technically the second EU robotaxi deployment, following Uber and Pony.ai's recent launch in Zagreb. Users also highlighted VW’s ongoing MOIA self-driving project in Hamburg as a domestic parallel.

### Qwen 3.8-Flash-Next releasing tomorrow (125B a6B)

**Two parameter tiers — 125B and “a6B” — are slated to drop tomorrow, offering options across compute budgets in one release.** Beyond timing and sizes, the title doesn’t share technical details, licensing, or benchmarks.

The discussion fractured over whether current local LLMs are genuinely viable for professional software development. Optimists view high-RAM systems like the 128GB Strix Halo or Mac Studio as the long-awaited sweet spot for running 27B–31B models (like Qwen and Gemma) locally. Skeptics countered with war stories of massive 96GB VRAM setups failing at basic tasks—highlighted by a specific incident where Qwen 3.8 failed to check an empty `$id` variable and executed a catastrophic `rm -rf` on a parent directory. For these users, reliable local AI remains a $10,000+ hobby requiring 256GB of memory, with current models described as heavily "benchmaxxed."

The hardware debate centered strictly on memory capacity versus bandwidth. Critics pointed out that even older desktop GPUs (like the RTX 3080 Ti) deliver over 900 GB/s of bandwidth, massively outperforming the unified memory speeds of entry-level Apple M-series chips or AMD APUs for decode tasks. Defenders argued that raw bandwidth is useless without the capacity to fit the model; 8GB or 16GB discrete cards cannot run modern 27B+ parameter models without extreme quantization, making high-capacity unified memory the only affordable compromise.

Real-world benchmarks confirmed the performance tradeoffs. Users reported Qwen 3.8 27B crawling at an uncomfortable 10–16 tokens per second on Strix Halo hardware, though highly optimized software stacks (using NVFP4, Dflash2, and torch-compile) are pushing 44+ t/s on DGX setups. Finally, a technical correction resolved a noted Strix Halo limitation: while default BIOS and Windows configurations restrict GPU memory allocation to 90GB, Linux users can adjust the GTT to hand nearly the entire 128GB block over to the GPU.

### Headlong: A microharness for persistent agents

#### [Submission URL](https://www.laude.org/updates/headlong-a-microharness-for-persistent-agents) | 121 points | by [lbw1215](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=lbw1215) | [53 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49428882)

Unlike reactive agent frameworks, **it keeps thinking in a continuous inner‑monologue loop** and treats human messages as observations in that stream rather than sessions, which means the agent decides if/when to reply and can pursue self‑initiated projects between messages.

- **Single shared mind (multi‑player):** One global thought stream across all users; no per‑user sessions. Conversations on Slack/Telegram/mobile all land in the same timeline. Engaging for teams (it can review branches or ping people unprompted), but it’s bad at keeping secrets and conflicting instructions aren’t resolved—assume anything told to it is shared.
- **Bash‑first microharness (<10K LOC):** Core implemented in small Bash executables. The shellm tool is a Bash implementation of a recursive language model (RLM). Tools, memory, and skills are just executables/files, so the agent can inspect/modify itself and run shell commands.
- **How it works:** A Thinker loop calls shellm to choose the next thought; shellm calls an LLM to emit reasoning text and/or a Bash script that’s executed immediately, repeating until a FINAL env var is set. Context is assembled from the agent’s trajectory; external observations are injected into the stream.
- **Safety and cost:** Alpha research software. Run in a sandbox; use a dedicated, spend‑capped API key—because the agent thinks around the clock, it will both execute shell and consume tokens continuously.
- **Install:** One‑liner: curl -fsSL https://headlong.ai/install.sh | bash

The architectural bet is persistent agency: a never‑asleep loop with messages as observations, which makes the agent feel more like a teammate—and exposes all the trade‑offs that come with that.

- **Security as an authorization problem:** Critics zeroed in on the agent's lack of data isolation, arguing that failing to resolve conflicting human instructions isn't a memory quirk, but a fundamental authorization failure that will cause the agent to misbehave. Project author *andyk* responded that bypassing strict access control was an intentional trade-off for research velocity, treating the project as an experiment in whether an agent can learn to exercise human-like judgment over secrets rather than relying on hardcoded guardrails.
- **The illusion of continuous thought:** *vedtam* expressed disappointment that the architecture relies on a `turn -> FINAL -> schedule wake-up` cycle rather than a truly continuous, always-spinning loop. *andyk* clarified that the core engine originally ran without pauses; the scheduled wake-ups were introduced purely as a cost-control mechanism to prevent the infinite loop from burning hundreds of dollars a day on background API calls. 
- **Evaluating new harnesses:** Asked how to objectively test the flood of new agent frameworks, *andyk* pointed to public standards like Terminal Bench 3, but *embedding-shape* argued that public benchmarks are completely worthless for real-world workflows. They strongly advocated building private, automated evaluation suites based on personal chat histories and custom scoring criteria (like lines of code generated).
- **Installation hygiene:** The project drew standard infrastructure ire for distributing untested alpha software via a `curl | bash` installation one-liner.
