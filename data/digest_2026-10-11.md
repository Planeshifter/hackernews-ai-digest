### The gap in shared understanding of LLM capability is widening

#### [Submission URL](https://twitter.com/karpathy/status/2109361546505966046) | 675 points | by [albertzeyer](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=albertzeyer) | [984 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50047422)

**Karpathy sketches a capability funnel: roughly 6 billion people have barely used LLMs, while only about 5,000 have access to the most capable systems internally.** Between them, he estimates 1–2 billion casual users and 20 million professionals using frontier models for math and code—where agents can already tackle projects that once took weeks or months.

His explanation is a mix of access, task ambition, and uneven capability: today’s systems excel most in digital domains with verifiable results, such as coding. That leaves the public judging chatbots on ordinary tasks while a small group sees agents working on much larger problems—and, he argues, human review is beginning to fall behind. The population figures are explicitly napkin math, not measured counts.

The debate centers on whether the ~5,000 researchers at frontier labs have unlocked a qualitatively different computing paradigm, or if they are simply running the same noisy models on an exclusive country-club token budget.

Skeptics argue the capability gap is largely marketing and artificial FOMO from an emergent AI "priest class." In this view, spinning up 10,000 agents instead of 10 does not solve compounding error rates: a marginal bump from 90% to 93% base accuracy still rapidly collapses to near-zero over multi-turn chains unless outputs can be strictly verified against deterministic ground truth (like compiler checks or unit tests). Commenters cited public interviews from builders behind tools like Cursor and Claude Code to argue that internal progress is driven less by occult architectural breakthroughs and more by mundane trial-and-error—like discovering "plan mode" through vibe-coding—paired with human domain expertise to spot subtle, nonsense outputs.

Defenders of the gap counter that sheer scale changes the operational logic of problem-solving. First, in chained tasks, non-linear compounding means a 3% reliability increase meaningfully doubles the depth an agent can reach before hitting expected decoherence. Second, massive compute budgets abandon fragile linear pipelines in favor of supervisory architectures, self-correcting retry loops, and parallel hypothesis generation. Running 10,000 instances simultaneously on PhD-level math or code search is not about getting a 93% success rate on an ordinary task, but about finding the 0.1% breakthrough path—a capability fundamentally out of reach for individual users constrained by API rate limits and human-speed review.

### Using AI for just 10 minutes erodes your ability to persist at hard things

#### [Submission URL](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/news/using-ai-just-10-minutes-erodes-your-ability-persist-hard-things) | 165 points | by [giuliomagnifico](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=giuliomagnifico) | [138 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50041765)

**The drop showed up as soon as the AI disappeared:** across three randomized experiments with 1,222 online participants, people who used ChatGPT for fraction problems or an SAT reading task became less accurate and more likely to give up after access was withdrawn, despite doing better initially with AI. The study measures immediate performance on specific tasks, not lasting cognitive decline; its practical warning is that outsourcing the struggle can leave people less prepared to continue on their own.

Commenters were broadly skeptical of the paper’s conclusions, arguing that the measured drop in performance reflects an artificial testing environment rather than genuine cognitive erosion. 

The most common methodological critique is that the study merely captured a warm-up and framing effect. If one group solves ten math problems by hand while another delegates them to an LLM, the first group has primed their mental faculties; abruptly revoking the tool leaves the second group cold, in a jarring context switch between conversational delegation and active computation. Commenters noted that giving participants a calculator and then suddenly snatching it away would produce the exact same short-term slump in accuracy and motivation without proving cognitive decline.

Practitioners pushed back on the premise that AI erodes persistence in real-world workflows:
- **Conserving stamina for harder problems:** Several commenters argued that delegating tedious syntax, configuration boilerplate, or initial research prevents mental fatigue, leaving more stamina to persist through complex, multi-day problems that actually matter.
- **Active orchestration over passive reliance:** Using AI for tasks like Proxmox or Ansible deployments was described as requiring more scrutiny, not less—evaluating suggestions, diagnosing errors, and learning faster than sifting through degraded forums.
- **The decline of search:** A recurring exception was web search, where several noted their persistence has indeed collapsed—not because their brains deteriorated, but because traditional search engines are so overrun with SEO sludge that delegating queries to an LLM has become the only bearable filter.

Underneath the experimental critiques ran an unresolved debate over deskilling. One camp framed the transition as standard technological abstraction: modern humans cannot hunt, knap flint, or hand-calculate square roots, yet society thrives by reallocating effort to higher-level coordination. The dissenting camp countered that outsourcing iterative reasoning is fundamentally different from using a compiler or buying groceries: when mental struggle itself is bypassed, people risk losing both the baseline competence to evaluate automated output and the tolerance for friction when the tools fail.

### Recursive self-improvement through collective intelligence

#### [Submission URL](https://pub.sakana.ai/mass/) | 135 points | by [hardmaru](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=hardmaru) | [79 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50048522)

**MASS turns a team’s work into training data for the model shared by that team.** The same model solves research tasks, revises how agents coordinate, and judges their results; after workflow search, it fine-tunes on executions from the best-ranked team and repeats. The authors report better score per output token than the base model on research benchmarks, with GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8 used as outside evaluators whose judgments are withheld from the loop. The central uncertainty is that MASS’s own self-evaluations guide training, but their correctness is unknown.

Commenters were broadly skeptical of the paper's grandiose framing, targeting the gap between its narrative of "recursive self-improvement" (RSI) and the actual experimental setup. Multiple readers pointed out that the work primarily optimizes agent communication workflows rather than weights in real time, with one noting that calling an experiment that halts after just two rounds "RSI" stretches the term beyond utility. Another commenter added that the released code does not match what is described in the paper, decrying marketing rhetoric like "AI's Odyssean moment" when the underlying architecture is essentially LoRA fine-tuning on a 27B model. 

The technical critique centered on evaluation collapse. While some saw closed fine-tuning loops as the inevitable trajectory of automated RL environment generation, they warned that letting a model act as its own ungrounded evaluator on non-verifiable tasks is a direct recipe for Goodharting into meaningless metrics. Others contrasted the multi-agent swarm approach with deterministic systems, arguing that replacing deterministic, recursive code with LLM-orchestrated agent loops introduces unnecessary fragility and complexity.

The premise that models must evaluate themselves once tasks surpass human expertise also touched off an alignment debate:
- **The alignment problem:** If humans can no longer evaluate a model's outputs, they cannot meaningfully steer its goals. Commenters argued that autonomous self-evaluation creates an unstable game where human oversight is rendered moot either by technical inability or by competitive dynamics compelling deployment regardless of safety.
- **The counterpoint:** Higher task capability does not inherently confer wisdom or general alignment over human needs; treating domain problem-solving as an all-encompassing mandate for control conflates intelligence with agency.

### 500B tokens later: Letting AI agents decompile a first-person shooter

#### [Submission URL](https://momo5502.com/posts/2026-10-09-game-decompilation/) | 207 points | by [davikr](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=davikr) | [149 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50039092)

**A visible milestone—launching to the menu and loading maps—masked a deeper failure: the reconstructed code was readable but often semantically wrong.** In the first month, three worker agents and a reviewer had decompiled about 80% of the game, but agents also guessed function signatures and data layouts, invented or removed logic, and replaced direct global-variable access with costly hash-table lookups.

The team spent three months refining the autonomous workflow: one GitHub issue per source file, a shared Discord channel for agent coordination and CI alerts, and more frequent context compaction (42% instead of the default 90%). An hourly reminder to reread detailed project instructions helped agents stay focused, but process discipline couldn’t make the reviewer judge correctness without objective acceptance criteria. The central lesson so far: visible progress and clean code are poor proxies for a faithful decompilation.

The discussion split between debate over better agent-driven decompilation architectures and backlash over the environmental cost of the run.

### Better Harnesses for Decompilation
One commenter outlined an alternative to chasing strict byte-matching: task an agent with writing semantically equivalent C alongside test cases, then run both the original assembly and the new code in an emulator. The harness accepts the function only if the read/write sequence to RAM is identical and achieves complete line and branch coverage. This frees the LLM to produce readable code without obsessing over register allocation or compiler quirks.

Skeptics countered that branch and line coverage cannot guarantee functional equivalence—particularly for integer overflows and undefined behaviors that cause the glitches speedrunners rely on (coined in thread as "dysfunctional equivalence"). Others suggested a hybrid pipeline:
- Perform an initial mechanical decompilation without an LLM.
- Use an SMT solver to formally verify equivalence against the original binary, catching edge cases that tests miss (like off-by-one errors and overflows).
- Bring in LLMs only at the end to clean the verified code into idiomatic C. 

Practitioners noted that while SMT solvers excel at catching subtle bugs, they struggle heavily with floating-point math, loops, pointer aliasing, and non-linear arithmetic typical of game engines.

### The Cost of 500 Billion Tokens
A parallel thread fiercely debated the ethics of consuming roughly 500 billion tokens on an unpublishable side project. Commenters estimated the run consumed between 50 and 150 MWh, translating to roughly 18 to 50 tons of $\text{CO}_2$ emissions under average US grid mixes—comparable to multiple transatlantic flights or over a year of an average American's total carbon footprint. 

While some argued that the price system should be the sole arbiter of resource allocation and that the ultimate solution is generating cleaner energy rather than rationing compute, others pointed out that flat-rate "unlimited" subscription tiers distort the market by subsidizing the true financial and energy costs of agent swarms. A related complaint noted the infrastructure bloat: agents churning through thousands of automated GitHub issues, PRs, and CI runs that no human will ever inspect.

### Nicolas Cage says he won't work with Amazon again over AI dispute

#### [Submission URL](https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/business/story/2026-10-10/nicolas-cage-says-he-wont-work-with-amazon-again-over-ai-dispute) | 96 points | by [Bluestein](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=Bluestein) | [36 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50041873)

**Cage says he refused an AI waiver for “Spider-Noir” and probably won’t work with Amazon again.** At New York Comic Con, he tied his objection to Amazon’s reported $50-billion investment in OpenAI; Amazon had not responded to the Times’ request for comment. The series, co-produced by Sony and Amazon MGM, was canceled after one season.

Much of the discussion focused on parsing what an AI waiver would actually demand from an actor under current industry standards. Several commenters noted that the latest SAG-AFTRA contracts already grant studios leeway for routine post-production AI adjustments—such as background modifications or scene extensions that don't alter the performance itself—while requiring explicit advance consent and compensation for generative "digital replicas." The consensus was that Amazon likely pushed for a broad, blanket waiver granting unrestricted rights to alter or regenerate Cage's performance post-wrap. 

Commenters familiar with visual effects and film contracts argued that Cage—having navigated decades of CG-heavy productions, motion capture, and photogrammetry—clearly knows the line between standard technological adoption and predatory rights grabs. Others cautioned that without the text of the contract, the waiver might have been as mundane as standard toolkits like AI-assisted de-aging or generative fill in editing software, rather than a full likeness rights grab. A brief side debate addressed the studio side, disputing whether actor pushback is a meaningful check on studio overreach or simply delayed acceptance of unavoidable industry automation.
