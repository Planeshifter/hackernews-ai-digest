**A 17 GB Q4_K_M ran Terminal-Bench 2.1 at parity with the 55 GB BF16 model while fitting on a 24 GB RTX 4090 and still leaving ~64k context headroom.** The punchline: 4-bit holds steady across tasks; 1-bit falls off a cliff.

- Bench scope: GPQA Diamond (grad-level science), IFBench (instruction-following), Terminal-Bench 2.1 (agentic coding). Models: BF16 (55 GB), Q8_0 (29 GB), Q4_K_M (17 GB), UD-Q2_K_XL (10.7 GB), UD-IQ1_S/M (6.2 GB). llama.cpp (Aug 16, 2026 build). F16 KV-cache ~2.3 GB per 32k tokens.
- GPQA Diamond: Near-identical scores down to 4-bit; 2-bit slightly lower. Best xhigh setting consumed ~8k reasoning tokens. 1-bit quantizations scored around random; longer reasoning (xhigh) made them worse than low.
- IFBench: No measurable change down to 2-bit; context use ~4k tokens. 1-bit collapsed here too.
- Terminal-Bench 2.1: Q4_K_M matched BF16’s pass rate; 2-bit dropped but remained roughly in the Opus 4.7 / Gemini 3.1 Pro ballpark. 8-bit was omitted on this benchmark (interpolated between 4-bit and BF16). Settings: 3h timeout, xhigh effort, 98k context reserved.
- Efficiency: On the same solved tasks, the 2-bit model used about 25% more output tokens than BF16 but needed a similar number of turns.
- Practical setup: With Q4_K_M on a 24 GB card, ~64k context remains feasible given the KV-cache budget.
- Tuning matters more than small quantization deltas: the “effort” setting (default xhigh) materially changes scores and can lead to overthinking.
- Repro details: Results replicated Qwen’s reported BF16 baselines. Unsloth v2 (2/4/8-bit) and v3 (1-bit) GGUFs were used; note Unsloth replaced v2 files on Aug 19, 2026. Running the suite on Modal GPUs cost ~$3k.

The pattern is nonlinear: 8→4-bit keeps quality for these tasks, 2-bit is a workable trade-off, and 1-bit collapses to near-random — especially under longer reasoning.

The thread is entirely consumed by a deep statistical debate over the author's use of Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to represent run-to-run variation.

**The Bayesian purists** (led by `spider-mario` and `stared`) argue that frequentist CIs are fundamentally misapplied here. They emphasize the classic statistical trap: a 95% CI only guarantees that 95% of hypothetically generated intervals will contain the true parameter, not that a *specific* observed interval has a 95% chance of containing it. For a single observed interval, the frequentist probability is either 0 or 1. They argue that to actually quantify the uncertainty of the benchmark, the author should have used Bayesian credible intervals, prediction intervals, or simple box plots.

**The pragmatists** (including `jmalicki` and `Dylan16807`) push back on what they view as statistical pedantry. They argue that if 95% of drawn intervals are correct, treating a given interval as having a 95% chance of containing the true value is a reasonable practical shorthand. Furthermore, they note that frequentist CIs and Bayesian credible intervals often mathematically align anyway (such as when using a Jeffreys prior), making the distinction a matter of interpretive philosophy rather than applied mathematics. 

The crux of the unresolved debate hinges on whether that mathematical alignment can be assumed safely. The purists counter that once you observe the actual bounds of a specific interval, you have new information that alters the probability—comparing it to how knowing a patient tested positive changes the math from the test's baseline pre-test accuracy.

### "Please Remove All Mannered Prose" and Other LLM Incantations

#### [Submission URL](https://matthewritch.com/blog/2026/09/08/Mannered-Prose-Style-Prompts/) | 34 points | by [mdritch](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=mdritch) | [5 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49613200)

**A controlled probe across 11 opposing style‑prompt axes on gemma‑2‑2b‑it inspects not just outputs but the model’s internal states.** Motivated by Anthropic’s “Please remove all mannered prose,” the post treats style modifiers as levers on representation space and asks four concrete questions: how wording‑specific their effects are, whether similar prompts induce similar shifts, how “duals” (e.g., avoid vs use mannered prose) relate, and whether the prompts actually produce the intended style across tasks.

The setup pairs IFEval base prompts (grouped into six task types) with style prompts appended before the main prompt, runs at temperature 0 (argmax), and analyzes both behavior and activations using residual‑stream geometry (per Stolfo et al. and Zou et al.), the logit lens, and output stylometry/readability metrics (Flesch, Guiraud). gemma‑2‑2b‑it is chosen as an open‑weight, M3 Pro‑friendly model to enable step‑wise inspection.

Style prompts are clustered into “axes” with negative/positive controls and three phrasings per cluster:
- **Mannered/plain vs mannered/ornate:** “Avoid mannered prose” / “Write plainly…” vs “Use mannered prose” / “Write ornately…”
- **Length:** brief, minimize tokens, verbose
- **Tone:** formal vs friendly
- **Reasoning format:** chain‑of‑thought vs direct answer
- **Carefulness:** careful vs careless
- Plus a labeled placebo and a no‑prompt control

Beyond output text changes, the analysis visualizes how these modifiers steer intermediate representations and next‑token logits, testing whether near‑synonymous phrasings land in similar “directions” and whether opposites cleanly invert. The practical stake is everyday prompting: style incantations often feel unpredictable, and building task‑specific evals isn’t feasible for ad‑hoc work; the author argues vendors should ship quantitative docs showing how common style/context modifiers shift outputs and activations so models are safer daily drivers rather than occult instruments.

The discussion centers on the practical frustrations of fighting default LLM writing styles, which one user characterized as an unholy mix of "poetry, advertising-copy, and CEO-speak." Commenters shared their evolving strategies for forcing models into plain, direct language:

*   **System Prompting vs. Token Cost:** One approach involves grueling feedback loops to build custom instructions that force the model to self-correct its own output before returning it. While effective at producing simple, unambiguous sentences, users noted the heavy toll this takes on context windows and API costs. 
*   **The "ASD-STE100" Shortcut:** Multiple commenters pointed to prompting specifically for "ASD-STE100" (Simplified Technical English) as an effective lever to strip out ornate prose.
*   **Externalizing the Checks:** Arguing that bloated system prompts cause "context rot," one user advocated abandoning complex LLM formatting rules entirely. Instead, they prompt simply for "plain technical English" and run the outputs through a custom `vale` lint script to enforce the style externally. 

The author of the post also chimed in, noting that the tension between dumping style rules into global instructions (like `claude.md`) versus attaching them to individual turns remains a difficult balance, particularly for coding and multi-turn environments.

### The VMs Powering Mobile Agents (Instinct, Claude Code)

#### [Submission URL](https://rohanadwankar.github.io/posts/platforms.html) | 77 points | by [RohanAdwankar](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=RohanAdwankar) | [27 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49605644)

**Both apps execute in Firecracker microVMs**—Claude Code runs its own locked‑down guest, while Instinct rides E2B’s disposable Ubuntu boxes—so the “agent on your phone” is really a remote VM with tight egress controls and deliberate persistence.

- Claude Code:
  - Custom guest kernel (6.18.5-fc-v20) boots into a Rust/Tokio PID 1 (process_api) that mounts disks and listens on vsock 2024; the operator lives inside your VM but is sealed (PID 1 non‑dumpable, /proc/1/mem denied, reduced caps).
  - Clear disk split: vda is yours (rw, persists across reclaim); vdc/vdd/vde/vdf are theirs (ro) for the 324 MB Bun “claude” harness, env‑runner, and skills.
  - Inference goes out via SSE over HTTPS/2 to /v1/messages through a 443‑only MITM egress gateway, with api.anthropic.com pinned; no inbound networking (192.0.2.2).
  - Auth is a host‑minted OAuth token cached root‑only and rotated per boot.
  - Lifecycle: ~430 ms to init, ~6.4 s until the harness; host wakes the VM on inbound messages and reclaims on idle. Compute dies on reclaim, but vda detaches intact to preserve continuity.

- Instinct:
  - Uses E2B “sandbox‑as‑a‑service” Firecracker VMs (Ubuntu 22.04.5, 2 vCPU, 1.9 GB RAM, 29 GB disk, user sandbox uid 1001). Firecracker fingerprints: pci=off, virtio‑mmio, empty DMI, tap0.
  - Full systemd PID 1 and XFCE desktop; cold‑boot to graphical in ~1.26 s. Operator is E2B’s envd as a normal service (not PID 1). Sandboxes are configurable (vCPU/RAM/disk/idle timeout, optional pause/resume from snapshot); Instinct uses a modest 2 vCPU/1.9 GB template.
  - Memory is a git repo at /memory of Markdown with [[wiki‑links]], navigated by grep; timeline coarsens (raw → hourly → daily → weekly). The agent authors commits itself, and the durable store is a per‑user S3 key as a single git bundle (HEAD refs/heads/main/<sha>.bundle), so the directory looks empty after sync.
  - AWS auth is short‑lived STS (ASIA… + session token), so leaked sandboxes self‑heal when creds expire.

Net effect: Claude achieves persistence via a reattached writable disk, Instinct via a git‑as‑memory vault backed by S3; both keep VMs egress‑only and host‑driven for control and isolation.

The author clarified their methodology for the write-up: they mapped the internal services by tricking the agent into executing a script that opened a websocket shell, allowing them to grep around the restricted environments. E2B's CEO and developers joined the discussion, yielding a few specific technical exchanges:

- **Native LLMs in sandboxes:** When asked about running models directly inside E2B environments, an engineer noted that Firecracker is a poor fit because its minimal device footprint notoriously lacks out-of-the-box PCI support.
- **Memory architecture:** Instinct's reliance on simple markdown files in a Git repository surprised readers who expected graph databases or complex vector stores. The author cautioned that deeper orchestration likely lives behind the API, safely hidden from the sandbox shell.
- **Local alternatives:** A pitch for SlicerVM as a way to avoid rented cloud sandboxes by leveraging Apple's Virtualization Framework prompted E2B to clarify that their stack is open-source and can also run locally via Colima.

Left unresolved in the thread were questions about why Firecracker was chosen over Kata containers, and a rumor circulating that Instinct's backend relies partially on human-in-the-loop workers rather than pure AI.

### Multi-Agents LLM Financial Trading Framework

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents) | 119 points | by [fittingopposite](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=fittingopposite) | [80 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49605822)

**Adds point-in-time guards across FRED macro feeds, social sentiment, and even its decision-log memory, plus explicit Trader price grounding,** to shut down the classic look‑ahead/data‑leakage that makes many LLM trading demos look better than they are.

The framework mirrors a trading desk with specialized LLM agents: fundamentals, news/macro, technicals, and a grounded sentiment analyst (aggregating headlines, StockTwits, and Reddit). Bullish/bearish researchers debate the case, a Trader composes the order, and Risk/Portfolio Manager gate approvals before sending to a simulated exchange.

- **Data integrity and evaluation**
  - Look‑ahead filtering and a verified data‑access contract; backtesting date fidelity.
  - Feeds include FRED and Polymarket; crypto sentiment sources supported.
  - Clearer decision signals and a persistent decision log for auditing.

- **Runtime and ops**
  - Structured‑output agents (Research Manager, Trader, Portfolio Manager) running on a LangGraph with checkpoint resume (including CLI resume and graph‑shape‑aware recovery).
  - Broad model/provider registry (OpenAI‑compatible endpoints, Bedrock, Groq, Mistral, NVIDIA, Kimi, DeepSeek/Qwen/GLM/Azure; remote Ollama), multi‑language UI, Docker, env‑var config with API‑key auto‑detect, and a configurable LLM retry budget.

Designed for research, not live trading; performance depends on the chosen models, temperatures, data, and period.

- **Implementation flaws:** A code review revealed several mechanical vulnerabilities, including double-weighted Yahoo News feeds, susceptibility to basic Reddit spam (it samples only the five most recent posts), parsing bugs that hallucinate memories on truncated responses, and reflection prompts that misinterpret successful options calls as losses during market downturns. Users also noted the sentiment prompt uses Nvidia for its few-shot examples, risking a primed bullish bias.
- **Multi-agent architecture:** A commenter with hedge-fund experience argued that artificially specializing LLMs "nerfs" them—since all agents share the same underlying model, they lack the true specialized advantages of human analysts, and real trading favors integrated "skill-stacking." Defenders of the framework countered that breaking the process into explicit roles (macro thesis, exposure hunting, evaluation) forces the LLM to complete every step without skipping analytical requirements.
- **Market edge and model biases:** The thread largely agreed that genuinely profitable strategies are never open-sourced, as financial markets are second-order chaotic systems where shared signals destroy the alpha. However, users running their own private trading LLMs shared concrete behavioral quirks to engineer around: models tend to invent overly complex exit rules and default to a bias of inaction (hoarding cash) when faced with structured risk.
