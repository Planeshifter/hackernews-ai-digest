**Dessa is a full-duplex speech-to-speech model trained from scratch on raw human conversation audio, with no text and no pretrained models.** It comes from a small research lab at Block that reports to Jack Dorsey, staffed by alumni of the Toronto deep learning startup Dessa. The pitch is that every cascaded voice stack (speech to text to LLM to speech) throws away emotion, sarcasm, laughter, and timing. Dessa never had text to lose.

Claims from the page:
- It listens and talks simultaneously, so it backchannels ("yeah", "got it"), interrupts, and laughs mid-sentence on its own.
- Inference runs faster than real time and it runs locally on a MacBook Pro M4 Max or better.
- It learned language from "hundreds of times fewer words" than LLMs, and training took a single NVIDIA GB200 NVL72.
- It picks its own voice and personality, and the demo clips are unsteered live recordings with subtitles added afterward. One clip has it talking to GPT-Live-1, with both voices AI-generated.

The catch is that there are no numbers beyond those. No benchmarks, no model size, no training-data hours, no paper, and no release: access is a waitlist, plus early access for a small group of AI safety researchers. "World's first" is hedged with "as far as we know," and the evidence is a set of curated demo clips. How much factual knowledge a model can absorb from sound alone is one of the open questions the team itself lists.

#### [Submission URL](https://corgi.computer/) | 121 points | by [eiopa](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=eiopa) | [42 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214373)

**Corgi is a terminal multiplexer built around coding agents**: it tracks which of Claude Code, Codex, Pi, or OpenCode is working, finished, or waiting on you, and F1 jumps to the next unread one. Like tmux, agents and processes keep running in the background after you detach, and you reattach by typing `corgi`.

The author, inspired by Herdr, has used it daily for a few weeks. The differentiators are mostly ergonomic:

- **Multi-account launcher** for Codex, Claude Code, and Pi, with fast session search and resume plus inline usage tracking. Useful if you juggle several accounts.
- **Activity feed and replay**: hover an agent to see what it did, and view every file it touched in one scroll. There's also a "top for your tokens" view of where usage went.
- **Per-workspace CPU, memory, and GPU usage**, plus worktree management.
- **Agent-run housekeeping**: it reuses your existing agents to label what a link is, what a worktree is for, and what each agent is doing. The author assumes you have cheap inference (Haiku-class) to spare for this.
- **Terminal quality of life**: click to copy links that wrap across lines, thumbnail previews for images, and Ctrl-Shift-T to undo a tab close. ⌘V pastes images even over SSH.

The catch is that SSH image paste requires **Ghostty Nightly**. It can also be driven from any terminal, and `corgi --skill` teaches your agents to do so.

The author says there are rough edges and asks for feedback. They also say they built it while on sick leave, partly to test whether they could return to work, with a "more mixed than I had hoped" answer. The page doesn't state a license or pricing, and the homepage's comparison grid (tmux, Herdr, cmux) is the only competitive framing.

- On eight of twelve benchmarks, the gap is inside the run-to-run confidence interval, and five come out ahead of the base model.

Looping, where the model gets stuck repeating itself, also drops from 3.64% of responses to 0.76%. On HMMT Feb 26 it falls from 17.0% to 4.5%. Truncation rates fall too, so some of the saved tokens come from responses that no longer run out of budget.

The pipeline runs in four stages: find the entity types, find the entities, build the page, verify. It is temporal. Each page has a slider, and dragging it shows what was known on that date. Humans did two things. A person kept 54 of the 57 entity types the pipeline proposed. The facts are dated, sourced sentences rather than CRM-style fields like headcount or revenue. The authors position this as a layer beside the system of record, holding what changes too often or doesn't fit in a field.

Enron suits a demo because the mail is public (released by FERC, maintained by Carnegie Mellon) and the history is settled, so a claim like "Dynegy agreed to acquire Enron in November 2001" can be checked against the record. ### New AI Is More Than Twice as Good at Diagnosing Diseases as Doctors

#### [Submission URL](https://gizmodo.com/microsoft-says-its-new-ai-is-more-than-twice-as-good-at-diagnosing-diseases-as-doctors-2000829562) | 64 points | by [njx](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=njx) | [57 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50214185)

**The claim comes from Microsoft, not an independent evaluation.** The URL slug frames it as "Microsoft says its new AI is more than twice as good at diagnosing diseases as doctors," which makes this a vendor-reported result rather than a peer-reviewed finding.

The article text wasn't retrievable, so the benchmark, case set, model, and definition of "twice as good" are all unknown here. Those details decide whether the claim means anything. Comparisons like this depend heavily on who the doctors were, what resources they had, and whether the cases were curated test vignettes or real patients.

Treat the headline as a pointer to Microsoft's methodology, not as evidence of clinical performance.

The thread splits less on whether the AI is good than on what a benchmark win actually measures, and on what happens to physician training if it's adopted.

**Benchmark vs. real visits.** Several commenters argue the test setup flatters the model. viraptor points out that the system only sees answers and asks specific questions, so it can't notice a trembling hand, regional disease patterns, or a patient who is nervous around their partner. Beating doctors at a turn-by-turn decision tree isn't what a typical visit looks like. FireBeyond, a paramedic, adds that the hard part is drawing out a structured history, not encyclopedic knowledge. Patients forget a penicillin allergy until they reach the hospital, and "GIGO" applies to humans and AI alike. Harvey9 agrees and notes that checking for pitting edema would take video and a robot arm. DoingIsLearning lists specific weaknesses: inputs bias outputs, multimodal image classification is weak, training data is mostly English, and the medical-records privacy concerns are serious. Others note this is a Microsoft claim, not a peer-reviewed study. nitwit005 quotes the article's finding that GPT-5 gave the strongest results, and wonders why a hospital would pay Microsoft rather than ask ChatGPT to simulate a physician panel. opo recalls that MYCIN beat specialists at meningitis antimicrobial choice in 1979 and never became standard practice.

**Training and deskilling.** pmpmpmpm, a physician, says the tool is great for rural or older doctors but terrible for trainees, who need unassisted practice on common cases. That fear is of a broad decline in clinical expertise. The replies were:
- SJC_Hacker asks why patients should accept worse care so juniors can practice.
- ryan_n answers that you'd still pick the 20-year developer over an LLM-reliant novice who doesn't understand the work.
- seanmcdirmid counters that in an overloaded ER you get residents anyway, and harvey9 replies that most ER work follows established protocols.
- dooglius and tim333 propose that trainees diagnose independently, then compare against the AI.
- toomuchtodo asks how you "make corn when you've eaten all the seed." eqvinox argues medicine has few practice substitutes for learning to interview a human, and that AI could entrench existing biases, such as women being diagnosed worse.

**Missed diagnoses.** A large camp says doctors routinely miss things. NotGMan says doctors gaslight patients about rare diseases. keybrd-intrrpt notes that common conditions get missed too, and rendaw describes four dermatologists failing to identify perioral dermatitis. rightbyte and viraptor defend base-rate reasoning ("horses, not zebras") as a way to avoid overtreatment and limited-resource waste. harvey9 and 1shooner respond that averages don't help the patient who was missed.
