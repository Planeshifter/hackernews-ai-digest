### DeepInfer: High-performance inference framework for DeepSeek models

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepInfer) | 2296 points | by [ggcr](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ggcr) | [880 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50163277)

**DeepSeek open-sourced the inference engine behind its own production services, but only the offline half.** The release runs a single model, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, on NVIDIA SM100 GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs. It is Apache-2.0 licensed and built originally on vLLM, with credit to SGLang and TensorRT-LLM.

The headline features are mostly about throughput and reproducibility:

- **Decoding speed:** draft-token decoding with confidence-scheduled verification (DSpark, default max draft length 5), plus an async "zero-overhead" batch scheduler that overlaps CPU scheduling with device execution.
- **Parallelism:** attention data parallel with balanced context parallel, and MoE expert parallel with load balancing (EPLB).
- **Determinism:** batch-invariant inference and seeded deterministic sampling, both NVIDIA-only. Results stay identical regardless of what else is in the batch, which is hard to get and useful for evals and RL rollouts.
- **Structured output:** regex, JSON schema, or EBNF constraints via `SamplingParams`.
- **Kernels:** FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP, TileKernels, DeepSelect, and custom TileLang kernels.

**The catch is what's missing.** The README says a complete inference service also needs a KV cache system, prefill/decode disaggregation, and load balancing across data-parallel replicas, and none of that is included. So you get the model runtime and kernels, not the serving stack. Don't expect to stand up DeepSeek's production setup from this alone.

Build requirements are steep: Linux, GCC 13 or newer, Python 3.12, and CUDA 13 (or CANN 9.2 for Ascend). The benchmarks were measured on 4 SM100 GPUs for prefill and 64 for decode, each within one scale-up domain, so they reflect cluster-scale hardware and not a workstation. Using it means a source build and a handful of `DEEPINFER_*` environment variables.

### Google open-sources TorchTPU, a native PyTorch back end for TPUs

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/google-pytorch/torch_tpu) | 375 points | by [ilreb](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ilreb) | [87 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50169053)

**TorchTPU runs PyTorch natively on TPUs through custom ATen kernels and `torch.compile()`**, rather than routing through the XLA-bridge approach most PyTorch-on-TPU users have had to live with. The repo is already at 0.2.0 on PyPI and has 4,478 commits, so this is a mature internal project being opened up, not a fresh experiment.

What's in it:
- ATen kernel implementations for TPU, plus a compilation cache for XLA computations
- Data and tensor parallelism for distributed training
- `torch.compile()` integration for graph mode
- Examples for Llama, Qwen, ResNet, DLRM, minGPT, and UNet, shipped as Marimo notebooks (Jupyter-like)
- Debugging and benchmarking utilities

Install is `pip install torch_tpu==0.2.0` with the PyTorch CPU wheel index. Without that extra index, pip pulls the CUDA build of torch, which is an easy trap. It requires Python 3.11+, and the docs say to run it on a TPU VM (a v6e GCE instance is the suggested easy route).

Building from source is where the pain is. It's Bazel-only, and the wheel config assumes Remote Build Execution credentials unless you append `--config=no_rbe`, with the flag order mattering. Getting it wrong yields a wheel that aborts on `import torch` from duplicate static registrations. The README's dependency list is still literally "TBD."

The repo also ships `AGENTS.md` and `CLAUDE.md` files, so Google is evidently developing it with coding agents in the loop. The README gives no benchmarks or op-coverage numbers, so how it compares with the existing PyTorch/XLA path is unanswered. The user guide is at torchtpu.readthedocs.io.

### Publish fake stats, get cited in LLMs

#### [Submission URL](https://peec.ai/blog/publish-stats-get-cited-the-tactic-that-put-my-data-all-over-the-web) | 358 points | by [robtherobber](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=robtherobber) | [202 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50165801)

**A $50 site of 50,000 fabricated statistics is still being fetched by ChatGPT seven months later, long after Google deindexed it.** Metehan Yesilyurt, a GEO researcher at Peec AI, generated the pages with GPT-4.1 in a single day, never checked a number, and put them on a new domain (stateglobe.com). The pages cover SEO, e-commerce, and social media stats across 200 countries. He added 15,000 listicles later.

The numbers he reports:

- **Google:** Indexing worked at first, then two updates hit and visibility collapsed. Indexed pages now get 5–20 impressions.
- **ChatGPT:** Cloudflare logged 600,000+ bot requests in 30 days, about 72,000 of them from ChatGPT-User. That is the live-answer fetcher, not a training crawler. GA4 shows 10,000+ sessions from ChatGPT since March.
- **Links:** 330+ referring domains per Ahrefs and Semrush, including Shopify, DHL, and Bluehost. The fake figures also reportedly appear in an academic paper and a bank's investment research.
- **Visibility:** On his tracked prompts in Peec's dashboard, the site ranked second, behind only Statista.

His thesis is that retrieval is not ranking. ChatGPT rewrites queries and pulls from its own index rather than copying Google's top 10. More content is now written by LLMs that hunt for citable statistics to fill gaps, so a plausible-looking number on a clean page gets picked up and republished, which launders it into real-looking citations.

The caveats are real. This is a single self-reported experiment from someone at a GEO vendor, and the prompt set is small and not described in the visible text. Some of the 330 backlinks may come from AI-generated content. Even so, the finding that nobody verifies the numbers is the point. If your team uses LLM research to source stats, the fabricated-data pipeline is already running.

The thread quickly drifts from the fake-stats experiment to a broader question: can LLMs, or the people relying on them, filter out deliberate poison? Nobody discusses the experiment's numbers or methodology. The discussion is about what it implies.

**The opening frame:** The top comment quotes Cory Doctorow's argument that machine-readable web labeling fails because "there is a reward for cheating." Scammers will mislabel phone numbers and counterfeiters will mislabel products, and no one can fact-check billions of labels from unverifiable sources.

**"Isn't this true for humans too?"** This was the main fault line, and the replies split into three positions:
- **Context is the difference.** cure_42 argues a human reads a satchel seller's copy as marketing and a textbook as authority, and goes to the official domain for a bank's phone number. An LLM often strips that provenance. Making it reliably judge provenance would need very high accuracy. At around 90%, they argue, it becomes worse than useless: you either trust it and get misled 10% of the time, or you re-check everything.
- **It's fixable in principle.** cortesoft asks why LLMs can't be taught the same reliability heuristics, and argues that market pressure will force it because users will leave unreliable models. Others push back on both points. ashkankiani says people who verify are treated as paranoid, BobaFloutist says we regulate misleading ads because consumers can't police them, and Eddy_Viscosity2 says such laws don't seem to bind AI companies.
- **It's partly a technical limit.** solid_fuel and Bratmon note that provenance can be attached in RAG, where retrieved data enters the prompt. That's exactly the path the fabricated-stats site exploits. Training data carries no such annotation, and most of what a model says doesn't come from runtime research.

**Gullibility versus calibration:** tsunamifury says even top models soak up sell-side nonsense when asked for IPO advice and present it as fact. kingstnap reframes this as a decision-making problem rather than gullibility. Models can lay out pros and cons expertly but flip-flop on actual choices, like "someone with exactly zero opinions." tsunamifury counters that the real issue is that models don't seek out countervailing narratives and over-weight recent context, and that fixing this is expensive.

**What can anyone actually do?** scruple asks how you get poison out of the well once it's baked into a model. alwa answers with the old advice: cultivate diverse, trusted sources and distrust synthesis. Replies pushed back from several angles:
- scruple notes that agent-to-agent pipelines lack human discernment and incentives.
- agileAlligator doubts the average human has much discernment either.
- Forgeties79 objects that "caveat emptor" shifts the burden onto individuals and ignores the root cause. People can't vet everything, any more than they test every sip of water.
- tsimionescu replies that trust in the source is ultimately the only option.
- rcxdude adds that for water and roads we trust regulators, while online we choose our sources.
- Forgeties79 and scruple respond that regulation is possible and that this is being pushed on people rather than being an unavoidable fact of nature.

The unresolved crux is whether this is a user-skepticism problem or one for regulation and engineering, and whether LLM retrieval can ever match the source-trust judgments humans make.

### Benchmarking Bot Detection Systems Against Modern AI Agents in 2026

#### [Submission URL](https://research.poh.org/bot-benchmarking-2026/) | 47 points | by [mdahardy](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=mdahardy) | [8 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50169506)

**Detection rates ran from 99% down to 9.1%**, and the split that matters isn't vendor against vendor but scripted browsers against LLM agents. hCaptcha and Cloudflare Turnstile caught nearly every plain Chromium and CloakBrowser session but few AI-agent sessions. reCAPTCHA v3 did the reverse: it flagged most agent sessions and none from the stealth browser CloakBrowser.

The aggregate numbers:
- **Proof of Human:** 99%
- **reCAPTCHA v3:** 69.4%
- **hCaptcha:** 44.3%
- **Cloudflare Turnstile:** 42.1%
- **Cloudflare Bot Management:** 9.1%

The setup was five adversaries, 20 runs each, on a five-question survey mixing multiple-choice and open-ended items. The adversaries were stock Playwright/CDP Chromium, CloakBrowser (a stealth Chromium fork run through a residential proxy with humanized input), Browser Use driving Claude Opus, Perplexity Comet, and xAI's Grok agent. Crashes left 89–100 usable sessions per system. The Cloudflare Bot Management result comes from the Pro tier, which returns coarse labels like likely_human rather than the full 1–99 score, so it may understate the full product.

The caveat to weigh: **the benchmark is run by Proof of Human**, which is also the top scorer, and the task is a short survey where typing and mouse behavior are the signals it specializes in. Five adversaries and one task type is a narrow slice, and the black-box vendors' failure explanations are inferred from documentation, not confirmed. Still, the complementary failure pattern is the useful finding: a detector tuned to fingerprint anomalies misses agents running in clean, unmodified browsers, and one tuned to reputation misses well-disguised stealth builds.

The thread's dominant objection is methodological: the benchmark measures only half of what a detector has to do.

- **No false-positive measurement.** asksomeoneelse flagged the conflict of interest (Proof of Human ran the test and won). They also quoted the study's admission that it "did not evaluate false-positive rates among human respondents," and pointed out that `isHuman(x) = return false` would score 100% on that metric. aurareturn proposed a scoring rule to fix this: if blocking a bot is +1, forcing a human through a captcha should be about -100. nickabe backed the concern with experience, saying newer systems ban them outright in roughly half their online sessions, often because a new account is treated as a bot signal.

- **Detection isn't meant to be total.** Replying to conception, who said retail bots clearly get through, routerl argued that vendors mostly aim to deter repeated abuse, for example by making a scalper bot's transactions slow and expensive enough that it misses most purchases. On this view a per-session catch rate is the wrong yardstick.

- **Cloudflare configuration confound.** AznHisoka asked whether the tests ran against sites the authors controlled or third-party ones. Many sites run Cloudflare Bot Management in a passive mode, such as plain CDN use, with no strict enforcement, which could make the 9.1% figure misleading. The thread did not resolve this.

- **Omitted tools.** vikrantrathore, author of the self-hosted Sibuna, noted that open-source systems such as Anubis and SafeLine were left out. They said WAF rules (OWASP CRS) and JA4 TLS fingerprinting matter alongside bot detection, and asked for the benchmark code so they could include it in their own report.

- **Anecdote on blocking.** mimimile, building a crawler, found that just over 1,200 of 10,000 sites refused access outright.

### On AI writing

#### [Submission URL](https://www.bolkunov.com/p/on-ai-writing) | 92 points | by [dbolkunov](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=dbolkunov) | [60 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50171771)

**AI-written text costs readers more time than it saves the writer**, argues Dima Bolkunov, and the savings vanish after only a few readers. His definition of "AI writing" is text with at least 50% of its words generated by a model.

His case rests on three losses:

- **Style.** His bachelor's thesis was authorship attribution from short paragraphs using word-usage statistics, and even that crude approach worked. He concludes readers pick up style easily, and that AI averages it away. Without style you lose the context and empathy that even a stranger's writing normally carries.
- **Precision.** A model has to make assumptions to stitch a text together, so it reaches for vaguer wording to avoid logical errors. The prompter often can't see this, because they know what they meant and read that meaning into the ambiguous word.
- **Self-challenge.** Writing is how you find the gaps in your own thinking. Jumping straight to a result skips that step.

The reader pays for all three. Bolkunov says he reads AI text at least a third slower at equal length. Models are also verbose, so total reading time grows. He adds second-order costs: more questions, more iterations on collaborative documents, and less trust between reader and writer.

The speed figure is self-reported and not measured, so treat it as a felt estimate. The comments push back from two directions. One reader says the imprecision and shallow reasoning are temporary limitations of current models, and that readers mostly want the content, not the author. Another says LLMs are useful for close reading, interrogating claims and exposing gaps, so the claim that AI removes the intellectual work is too broad. The post's weakest spot is that it treats the 50% threshold as the problem. It never separates AI as a drafting shortcut from AI as a critic of your own draft, which is the distinction the second commenter is drawing.

The thread mostly agrees with the post, and the most interesting argument concerns why models write the way they do and whether that can be fixed.

**Why AI prose reads badly.** bunderbunder argued that LLMs are forward-only next-token predictors. Blasting out words is how you write a first draft, a form of thinking out loud. Good communication needs editing, which means going backwards, rethinking connections and knowing "when one word says more than a sentence." Loops only mimic that. celrod wondered whether text diffusion models, which refine iteratively, could do better, but bunderbunder replied that neither kind of model thinks. On brevity, dbolkunov (the author) said being brief requires being precise, which is hard for a model that isn't sure. nickabe added that models hedge more than confident humans do, and that their reasoning phase invents private shorthand that leaks into the output as jargon. nunez complained that LLMs take "FOREVER" to make a point, and that AI summaries drop details without telling you which.

**The workplace cost is relational.** fxwin said AI-written PR descriptions and READMEs are hard to care about when they carry a colleague's name and photo but not their voice. jpmitchell framed it as the broken assumption that the author spent as much effort as the reader is about to spend. Several teams have responded with policy. stingraycharles' team banned AI-written communication, including PR descriptions, and the author's colleagues agreed to limit AI for docs meant for humans, as opposed to agents. icedchai tolerates AI code but not AI prose, and called prompt-expanded "specs" gibberish. paulpauper's counter was to share the prompt and model instead of the output.

**Will it get fixed?** siva7 said AI solves math but cannot write like a human, so our idea of intelligence needs redefining. Responses were skeptical:
- samuria said "ever" is absurd after about three years.
- PestoDiRucola and paulpauper argued the labs simply haven't prioritized prose, since coding is the money-maker.
- GolfPopper brought up the "p's in raspberry" failure against the "solves any problem" claim, and verdverm replied that open-weight models now get it right.
- flyspaceships, who has built AI-fiction tooling, thinks the fears are overblown. Frontier models seem to be getting worse at it, style fine-tunes are hard to justify economically, and readers may end up valuing the human behind the piece more.

**Writing like AI.** verdverm asked whether humans are starting to write like AI. tunesmith distinguished adopted conventions from the real flaw, which is weasel words and fluff that predate AI. Several people dodge em-dashes to avoid being mistaken for a bot. boomlinde now deliberately writes more vulgarly. epihelix and paulpauper use double hyphens, a habit epihelix says predates Unicode. The author said he has noticed AI creeping into his own writing, but that using it to critique or spellcheck taught him something.

**Why people use it anyway.** paulpauper said writing well is hard, and the blank page is daunting.

### Show HN: Penmentor – Claude marks up your handwritten math like a teacher

#### [Submission URL](https://github.com/AbdullahAlhariri/penmentor) | 63 points | by [abdullahalharir](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=abdullahalharir) | [7 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50167604)

**Penmentor overlays teacher-style marks directly on your screen while you write math by hand**: one keypress sends your xournal++ page to an AI agent, which circles the first real mistake, ticks correct lines, and underlines later lines that inherit the error. The marks persist while you keep writing, and Esc clears them.

The design choice that sets it apart from screenshotting into a chat window is that it doesn't call an API. It shells out to the CLI agent you already have logged in (Claude Code, Codex, or Antigravity) and rides that subscription. A model prefix in Settings picks the agent, e.g. `claude:opus`, `codex:...`, `agy:...`.

It's built around hints rather than answers:
- **Hint ladder:** a nudge, then naming the technique, then a faint ghost of the next line.
- **Recall practice:** questions on your key steps, find-the-error drills, "which technique fits?"
- **Animations:** box part of your work and get an interactive manim animation (3Blue1Brown's library) where you can drag bounds or retype values and watch Riemann sums converge.
- **Whole-page context:** a small xournal++ plugin shares the full page, not just the visible screen.

The catch is the platform: Linux on X11 (best with i3), plus rofi, slop, xdotool, and PyQt6, so Wayland and non-xournal++ users are out. It's MIT-licensed, young (3 commits, 81 stars), and the demo video uses a scripted pen and a fixed model answer, so real-world marking accuracy on messy handwriting is untested here. Setup is a clone plus `bin/penmentor --selftest`, then bind the script to a key.

### Show HN: Jev Cards, Can you beat a vision model at a picture quiz?

#### [Submission URL](https://jevcards.com) | 68 points | by [totisjosema](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=totisjosema) | [51 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50167617)

**Jev answers each picture question in about a quarter of a second**, and this Show HN turns that latency into a game you can lose to. The model is TypeSafe's new vision model, and the cards are things like multiple-choice questions or "tap the spot" prompts on an image.

You can play solo against Jev, with a leaderboard per mode. You can also start a live match with friends, where Jev is just another player. The author says Jev sees exactly the image you see, with no side-channel hints. The cards were made from scratch with Opper's vision models and Claude Code.

The code is open source, so you can fork it for your own game or contribute cards. The newest cards stay private for now, presumably so they stay out of anyone's training or prompt data.

One disclosure to keep in mind: the author works at Opper, which was one of the routes used to run Jev, so this is part demo, part showcase. There are no accuracy numbers in the post, so "can you beat it" is something you'll have to test for yourself. Speed is the only hard claim, and it's the one a human can't match.

Repo: https://github.com/Jose-Sabater/jevcards

The thread's central argument is about scoring: should speed or accuracy decide who wins? The author defends a design where speed counts a lot, and many players found it counterintuitive.

**The scoring dispute**
- Several players reported "losing" to Jev despite being more accurate. One got 10/10 against Jev's 8/10. Another got 7/10 to Jev's 6/10, and the game still said Jev won.
- One commenter argued that speed should only break ties and that being right matters more than being fast. They framed it as the difference between "someone that is never wrong" and something that merely answers fast. They also noted there's no "I don't know" button, which would let a human abstain where Jev guesses. Another suggested accuracy first, then speed as the tiebreak, citing retention data: winners come back far more often than non-winners.
- The author said each mode tests something different. Sprint adds a 15s penalty per wrong answer, Versus combines time and correctness, and Streak is pure accuracy. He said the point is to show that these models are very fast but fail about 20% of the time. He admitted scoring might not be fully calibrated.
- One player exposed an exploit. They clicked through Sprint as fast as possible, got 20% right, and still beat their own 100%-correct run. The author responded by raising the wrong-answer penalty and resetting the leaderboard.

**How good is Jev, and what does that imply?**
- The author gave 70-80% accuracy overall (160/200 on his first cards, median 261 ms), varying by category and reaching 100% in some.
- Commenters drew different lessons. One wondered what a model at roughly 80% could be trusted to do. Another joked that it's "just like a coin flip." One argued the usefulness is limited to cases that need very fast answers, where 99% accuracy adds little over 50%, and where the problem form changes but stays general knowledge.
- The author's framing was that each use case must decide for itself whether that accuracy is good enough.

**Content and UX feedback**
- Players asked for category filters (one couldn't play music theory questions) and fewer US-centric cards (birds, bridges, hats). The author promised a whitelist/blacklist and more cards, and later added 300.
- The chess question was unclear about whether to tap the piece or the destination square. Dart boards and graphs, where the answer buttons sit at the bottom of the screen, also confused people.
- One user reported being told they were wrong on correct answers, and another saw a question with an invisible correct button. The word "slop" came up several times, including a rebuke of the author's tone toward a feature request.
- Smaller comments: an audio button placement suggestion, enthusiasm for live multiplayer with Jev as a player, and a quip that Jev should also have to use vision to navigate the UI.

Players also enjoyed the quirks of the questions themselves, like the ginkgo leaf and the fact that an orca is a dolphin.
