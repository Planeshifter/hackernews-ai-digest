### Neural networks are clean

#### [Submission URL](https://already-clean.goodfire.com/) | 791 points | by [themgt](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=themgt) | [315 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105647)

**Goodfire's Nick Cammarata argues the polysemanticity that drove the field toward sparse autoencoders was mostly a set of ordinary, fixable training problems, not superposition.** If he's right, you can study a network's own neurons directly instead of first training a second model to unfold them.

The key evidence is a rebuild of InceptionV1, the early field's model organism. Its last layer originally had almost no interpretable neurons. With architectural changes meant to help deep layers learn, and no change to model size or training data, **99-100% of last-layer neurons became easily interpretable**. Cammarata trained versions across a 300× size range and says all stay legible. Smaller ones don't fold unrelated things together; they just use coarser concepts, like one neuron for all cars that splits into specific car types when there's more room.

He calls the real motif **recursive generalization**, and he reads the canonical examples through it. The car-in-dog-neurons circuit from *Zoom In*, which he co-found, was the only example of that shape he ever saw. Nothing downstream reads the supposed hidden combination, so it was ordinary generalization and composition. He says he knows of no published second circuit with the right shape.

On language models, he reports preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B:

- About 90% of neuron poles have one main job.
- About 7% have two related jobs.
- Only 2-4% are not yet understood.

That compares with a median score of 0 out of 14 in Anthropic's 2023 one-layer study, and about 1.7% of neurons interpretable in the 2023 OpenAI automated-interpretability work (which he helped start). The headline claim of understanding most neurons in models up to trillions of parameters rests on this kind of audit. The source text cuts off mid-footnote on how that share varies, so how the "one job" figure is defined matters a lot here.

The title is stated as a working hypothesis, and the piece is a 65-minute essay, not a peer-reviewed result. The InceptionV1 result is the strongest part, since it's a controlled fix you can browse. Extending it to frontier language models rests on preliminary audits, and tooling quality is itself one of the author's named confounds. That cuts both ways: weak tools once made networks look worse than they were, and generous tools could now make them look cleaner.

### Using AI to reverse engineer hardware

#### [Submission URL](https://blogs.gnome.org/uraeus/2026/10/15/aiandreverseengineerhardware/) | 242 points | by [ErenayDev](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ErenayDev) | [233 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50104299)

**A Red Hat engineer's AI agent, given a Windows VM and a USB trace tap, went from stuck for months to a working Linux scanner driver in a few hours.** The author's mother's negatives needed a Plustek OpticFilm 8200i, whose newer chipset (Genesys GL128, USB 07b3:1825) had no Linux support. Claude Code alone got nowhere. What changed was giving it a harness modeled on a tweet from Glorious Eggroll: full control of a Windows guest to run the vendor driver while the host captured the traffic.

The method, now extracted into a generic **hw-harness** project:

- Run the vendor's Windows driver in a KVM VM with the real device passed through.
- Capture USB traffic on the Linux host with usbmon, below the passthrough layer, so the guest can't see it.
- Repeat the operation with the Linux driver and capture it the same way.
- Diff the two traces; the differences are your bug list.

For devices with no bus to tap, like an internal MIPI camera, it falls back to Windows-side inspection of INF files, registry data, ACPI tables and device properties. The author argues this is clean-room-ish, since it observes behavior and extracts configuration rather than copying code. That's his framing, not a legal opinion.

Results:

- **Scanner backend:** supports color, gray, infrared and color+infrared at 300–7200 dpi, 8 or 16 bits, with IR dust removal. It ships as a Fedora RPM. He also had Claude write a negative-scanning app, Negative Vibes.
- **Upstream collision:** SANE's genesys backend gained support for the scanner in September 2026, while he was working on his own. Compared against it, his driver resolves the same detail at 3600 dpi and is faster at full resolution (34.6 s vs 54.4 s) and adds IR dust removal. Upstream offers more resolution steps. It's one unit and one frame, so treat it as a single data point.
- **MIPI camera:** reusing the harness, he got kernel and libcamera patches for his laptop's camera in a single day.

Caveat: the author says hw-harness was not used in this form for his projects. It's a generalization of bespoke scripts, so expect rough edges. The core claim is that reverse engineering is tedious rather than conceptually hard, which suits an agent that never gets bored reading hex dumps. The human still picks the goal and verifies on real hardware.

The thread is dominated by one commenter's alarm: valdiorn, who runs a small pro-audio company, says Claude decompiled the firmware of three competitors' flagship effects in 72 hours (including the Strymon Big Sky). He had it emulate the original ROM and then compare a C++ port against the emulator, so the algorithms are the real ones, not imitations. His fear is that anyone with a subscription can now do this to his products too. Replies split over whether that actually destroys the moat.

**Camp 1: the algorithm was never the moat.**
- CoolestBeans argues that people buy Strymon for the interface, the brand and the known-quantity box on the floor. They point out that analog pedal circuits have been cloned for decades without sinking the originals.
- fnordlord cites Behringer, whose business is cloning successful gear without putting anyone out of business.

**Camp 2: the moat was always security, and it was never there.**

- rpcope1 and ndiddy push back that this protection is weak. rpcope1 recovered firmware from an STM32F103 without delidding it. ndiddy explains that RDP2 can be glitched down to RDP1 and then read out via known errata, so read-out protection stops only casual hobbyists.

### More on Anthropic's Scion ripping off Mononoke

#### [Submission URL](https://sunshowers.io/posts/anthropic-mononoke-2/) | 249 points | by [sunshowers](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=sunshowers) | [83 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50106738)

**The author, a former Meta source control engineer, now says Scion was an intentional copy of Mononoke**, not an accidental homage. His argument that the resemblance goes beyond "derived data is just an index":

- **The whole data model matches.** Anthropic's description of Scion changesets reads, to him, identically to Mononoke's Bonsai changesets, and "Scion" is "Bonsai" with the syllables reversed and the "B" dropped. He allows that the name may be coincidence.
- **The model was contrarian.** A write-ahead-log-style Merkle DAG was far from Git-like designs in 2018 and needed new algorithms such as server-side rebases. He had to defend it to several layers of Meta management.
- **The server-side query and mutation API** is similar too, though he declines to claim novelty there since earlier systems had one.

His complaint is attribution, not copying. Crediting Sapling for "hard-won lessons" doesn't count, because Sapling means both the hg-derived client and the whole system, and anyone from Meta's source control team knows the difference. He expects the changeset section to name Bonsai.
