### Genie 4

#### [Submission URL](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/) | 1015 points | by [acossta](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=acossta) | [575 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50261319)

**Genie 4 generates audio alongside the world**: music, ambient noise, and interactive dialogue are produced natively with the video, rather than bolted on afterward. DeepMind also claims a step up in photorealism and fluid movement over earlier Genie releases, though the post gives no benchmarks or numbers to check that against.

The architecture leans heavily on Gemini. Gemini Omni anchors the simulation in real-world appearance and behavior, and other Gemini models supply the reasoning that keeps a scene coherent over time. Between your inputs, Gemini also monitors and steers the scene so it keeps progressing on its own.

Interaction works in two layers:
- Keyboard controls handle navigation and camera movement.
- Text prompts change the world mid-run: shift the weather, add a person or object. Gemini adapts each prompt to fit the surrounding scene.

The demo prompts are long, art-directed specs (16mm grain, "no HDR," a floating platform with a saxophonist) paired with a reference image as the exact first frame. That suggests the best clips come from heavy prompt engineering, not casual use.

DeepMind pitches the model as a source of "infinitely variable" training environments for robots and agents, alongside new interactive experiences. It frames this as a step toward AGI. The robotics claim is the interesting one, but nothing in the post shows physical accuracy at the level that sim-to-real transfer would need.

Availability is limited. It's a research preview for trusted testers, with consumer and developer access planned next. Google AI Ultra subscribers get it first through Project Genie, along with early-access partners of the Gemini API and Google AI Studio. Everyone else joins the waitlist.

The thread centers on one question: does Genie 4 actually simulate a world, or does it only render what is currently in view?

**The evidence for "just rendering."** The top comment lists consistency failures in DeepMind's own demos. A second castle appears in the dragon clip, pedestrians on the train platform change once they walk away from the camera, and the Hong Kong moped rider gains a helmet. Others added more. One noted that the bridge wall in the Van Gogh clip visibly changes while it stays in frame. Another pointed out that the helmet "swap" is just a head passing behind another rider, so the fix isn't as simple as keeping the frame bigger. The DeepMind post itself admits that object permanence and long-term consistency are "active areas of ongoing research."

**Proposed fixes.** Several commenters suggested the model should be backed by persistent state rather than regenerating everything by diffusion.
- Track objects with positions, as game engines do, and check each new frame against that state.
- Render a larger, lower-fidelity frame, or a coarse 3D model, and upscale only the visible crop.
- Output 360° panoramas and crop from them. Others countered that this doesn't help with a room behind a door or a person behind a tree.
- Train on synthetic 3D-engine data, since YouTube footage has no object metadata. One commenter speculated that autonomous-vehicle sensor data would eventually fill that gap.

Skeptics replied that a full 3D scene model would be expensive, and that if the fix were easy DeepMind would have shown it. Others answered that this is a research preview, and that features routinely miss a release. One person recalled Genie 3 demos where writing on a blackboard persisted after the camera left and came back. Another thought the consistency level may simply be a resource setting. That last claim is a guess, and no one backed it up.

**Deeper disagreement.** One commenter argued that these "world models" lack a world model in a qualitative sense. They said compute has masked this rather than fixed it, and cited the way frontier LLMs lose narrative threads. Another asked whether partial models count, since most humans can't vividly recall a familiar walk either. The pushback was that human limits are irrelevant to judging the model. The counter-reply was that human error is useful because it shows how people compensate (editors, QA, reference material), and those methods suggest training and prompting improvements. A philosophical aside asked whether the moon exists when unobserved, and one commenter noted that a real world keeps going regardless of the observer while a generated one does not.

**Tone war.** One commenter complained that HN is the hardest audience to please and fixates on small gaps. Another pointed to how far things have come since the AI-generated Will Smith spaghetti video about three years ago. Others said the demos are cherry-picked best cases, and a reply mocked the idea that DeepMind would include bad examples on purpose.

The thread never settled whether persistence is an engineering cost problem or a limit of the generate-from-pixels approach.

### Survival LLM: AI assistant and emergency library that runs on your own computer

**An offline RAG assistant that bundles a ~930,000-article Wikipedia plus FEMA, CDC, and military survival manuals, then picks a local model sized to your RAM.** Free, open source, no account, and nothing you ask leaves the machine once setup finishes.

The app checks your memory on first launch and downloads a matching model:

- **128 GB+ Mac:** NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Super, 21/22 on the authors' emergency test (80 GB download)
- **32–96 GB Mac:** Qwen3.8 27B, 21/22 (18 GB)
- **32 GB+ Windows PC:** Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, 19/22 (25 GB)
- **16–31 GB Mac or PC:** IBM Granite 4.2 8B, 14/22 (6 GB)

The test is 22 blackout and first-aid questions, graded blind, run on an earlier build of the library. Small sample, self-run, but the Granite drop to 14/22 makes the point that low-RAM machines get meaningfully worse answers.

Every answer lists the articles it read, and you can click through to the passage highlighted in the source. That matters more than the model choice: it's the guardrail for when the model is wrong, and the page admits small models often are. You can also add your own PDFs, Word docs, and ebooks, such as a generator manual, and they're searched locally too.

The catch is the setup cost. The full library is a 9 GB download (19 GB installed, ~27 GB free during install), plus the model, and setup runs 20 minutes to about 2½ hours. The app is unsigned and sold outside app stores, so you'll click through Gatekeeper or SmartScreen warnings. The authors measured about 100 W on a MacBook Pro, roughly 100–300 answers per full battery, so a small power station handles it. Requires Apple Silicon with macOS 14+ or 64-bit Windows 10/11.

**Anthropic has embedded Claude in six real drug programs (BigHat, Bristol Myers Squibb, Divergence Labs, Enveda, Generate Biomedicines, The Column Group) and started its own**, including work on rare childhood muscular dystrophies where Claude handles much of the scientific and operational work. The thesis is that drug development has no single bottleneck, so AI has to touch everything from target selection to regulatory filings, not just molecule design.

The case studies show early lab results, not clinical ones:

- **Generate Biomedicines (CAR T):** Six scientists used Claude Code to propose shorter versions of one genetic component. One design was about 70% shorter than standard. In lab tests it let immune cells make roughly 10x as many receptors, killed tumor cells more efficiently, and stayed active longer. The goal is to free up room to extend CAR T to solid tumors.
- **Divergence Labs (food allergy):** Claude turned the goals into a design plan, then largely autonomously generated about 35,000 antibody designs in roughly a month. Early tests found three designs that bind IgE and 20 that dislodge it from immune cells, with three of those appearing to beat the standard medication. The two arms still need to be combined into one drug.
- **BigHat (lead optimization):** Connected to BigHat's lab data system, Claude proposed changes to two antibody fragments, and the lab tested about 1,010 variants in about 10 weeks. Mid-run, Claude and an Anthropic researcher decided the standard heat-stability and clumping metrics were misleading, proposed replacements and a protocol change, and the main predictions held.

The BigHat episode is the most interesting one: the model questioned the measurement itself rather than just optimizing against it. The caveat is that every number here is preclinical, self-reported by Anthropic and its partners, and "hits" are a long way from medicines. The post says Anthropic plans to scale the effort substantially and has already prioritized model improvements based on what it found. Technical reports from the partners carry the methods.

### Introducing LFM3: Every Modality for Specialized Intelligence

#### [Submission URL](https://www.liquid.ai/blog/lfm3) | 164 points | by [Philpax](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=Philpax) | [62 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50262229)

**LFM3-1.7B beats Liquid's own LFM2.5-2.6B with 35% fewer parameters**, and the whole first release is built from that one backbone: six ~2B models covering text, vision, audio, omni, and two "decision" models specialized from the vision and omni variants. Base and post-trained weights are open on Hugging Face, with a Playground and API as well.

The architecture is a "tri-hybrid" of 24 layers: 13 gated short convolutions (kernel size up from 3 to 7), 5 sliding-window attention layers with a 256-token window, and 6 global attention layers. Liquid found SWA gave the best quality-speed tradeoff when swapping out some convolutions. Other changes include head-wise sigmoid gating (which it says may help quantization by suppressing activation outliers), partial RoPE on global layers, and sandwich norm. Pre-training ran 22T tokens, with a final 3.5T mid-training stage weighted toward agentic traces and tool calls that also stretches context to 256K.

The edge story is the point:
- The text model fits in about 1 GB at 4-bit.
- Liquid's hardware-in-the-loop architecture search now targets 14 device/backend combos, up from one phone and one laptop CPU for LFM2. They include a Galaxy S26 Ultra, a Raspberry Pi 5, an M4 Max, Ryzen AI, and the Qualcomm Hexagon NPU.
- Per parameter, it matches LFM2-1.2B speed on short prompts and is about 25% faster at 8K context.
- Every model can be fine-tuned on a single GPU.

The pitch is to fine-tune a small expert on your own data rather than run one large generalist. The excerpt contains no benchmark tables, so the "more capable" claim rests on Liquid's own comparisons; check the model cards before trusting it for your task.

The thread is mostly practical: people trying to fit these small models into real pipelines, with a Liquid engineer (samstevens, who says he worked on the release) answering questions throughout.

**Triage and annotation pipelines.** The top comment describes using LFM2.6 as a cheap first-pass annotator for emails, texts, and metadata on an RTX A4500. Its lowest-confidence items get escalated to a bigger "halo" model. It currently yields trustworthy annotations on just over 40% of data points.
- Confidence comes from logprobs, checked against a hand-annotated set, and correlates about 80%. Having the same model judge its own annotations failed "predictably."
- Others suggested fine-tuning, Liquid's d1-2B decision model for calibrated classification, GLiNER-style models (one user fine-tuned on 500 GPT-tagged articles and runs it on a CPU), and CPU-trainable classifiers.
- The OP said the hard part isn't tagging. He wants to find networks between pieces of information, promote topics to objects, and consolidate narratives, and he called it far from solved.

**Odd tool-use behavior.** One user saw the reasoning trace say "the instructions say I must use available tools" on a trivial egg question. The request included two irrelevant tools from llama-server, and disabling tools removed the behavior. A Liquid employee said this is probably a side effect of agentic mid-training and SFT, and suggested a system prompt stating that no tools are available. Samstevens described the recipe as SFT then RL. Rollouts that exceed the context length get zero reward, which puts mild pressure toward shorter reasoning but no strong regularization.

**Which model to use.** Samstevens was candid that specialization still wins:
- The text model beats the others on text, VL beats Omni on vision, and the Audio model beats Omni on ASR/TTS.
- Unifying modalities into one model is a goal, not a current reality.
- He said cross-modal gains rarely show up in small models, and another commenter asked whether that bump has ever been conclusively shown.
- For long meeting recordings, he suggested Audio-2B for transcription and then the 1.7B text model for action items. With 100+ labeled samples and a GPU, you could fine-tune Audio-2B to go straight to action items. Diarization and speaker labels aren't supported and have no timeline.
- VL-2B was described as a strong OCR model for its size and fine-tunable to a specific output format.

**Why care?** Asked for a short pitch, samstevens named local classification and image work on about 1 GB of RAM (d1-2B), small agent chores like email sorting, and fine-tuning for repetitive tasks where big models are too expensive. Another commenter framed it as a model designed to be tiny from the start, rather than a scaled-down large model that hits doom loops and tool-call failures.

- **Speed (audio encoder):** The design choice was about speed. Liquid picks high-throughput architectures that approach full-attention expressivity, then closes the remaining gap with data work.
- **Quick test:** One user ran it as a "dumb but fast scout" on a 7900XT at about 8,500 tok/s prefill and 212 tok/s decode, and found it useful but sometimes inaccurate.
- **Moderation:** An unanswered question asked whether Liquid did any moderation work for kid- or school-oriented local use.

### Here be monsters: What do you still need to know when agents do the work?

#### [Submission URL](https://blog.adrq.dev/here-be-monsters-what-do-you-still-need-to-know-when-agents-do-the-work) | 196 points | by [adrq](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=adrq) | [101 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50258868)

**The skill that survives agent-written code is knowing just enough to smell a problem, and being willing to dive as deep as needed when you do.** That's the thesis of this essay, which opens with a small incident: CI failed on SonarQube's cyclomatic-complexity check for an `App.tsx` that Codex had packed into about 30 lines, roughly 15 of them being entire components written on a single line.

The author inspected it, found the complexity was correct and warranted by the business requirements (slightly over-engineered on edge cases, but sound), and simply told Codex to satisfy SonarQube. It split the file into 10 and CI went green. The catch is that this was code he had never seen, and he notes it could easily have gone worse.

To frame the stakes, he uses a "divebots" metaphor: engineers mining gold from the seabed, now sending autonomous bots to build the mining operations. The Optimists relax on the boat. The Skeptics collect horror stories of lost bots, unmaintainable structures, and "shiny octopus poop" passed off as gold. The author says the more detached you are from the operation, the more spectacular the failures.

His definition of ownership is blunt: you're the one who gets called when something breaks. The old route to the working model of a system (decades of writing and reading code, incidents, late-night debugging) no longer guarantees a human ever touched the code. Experienced operators can still run fleets of agents at large scale because they bring that accumulated judgment with them.

The text cuts off before the practical prescriptions, so what "just enough" knowledge means in concrete terms isn't visible here. The open question the essay raises, and the one that matters, is where the next generation builds that judgment if they never write the code by hand.

The thread's center of gravity is a split over whether directing agents resembles managing a team, and underneath it a worry about where the next generation gets the depth to "dive."

**The "boxes and lines" camp.** The top commenter, who moved from IC to team lead before AI, says the same skill applies. You can't understand every line, so you hold a system-level map of components and boundaries, treat the boxes as opaque, and go inside one only when its inputs or outputs look wrong. He has his team deliver sequence diagrams, and when asked how to build this view he suggests making agents produce them too. He concedes that the human-management analogy probably ends where motivation begins, since techniques that get quality out of people don't transfer to agents.

**The "skin in the game" objection.** The longest sub-thread argues that an AI can't bear consequences: it can't be fired, shamed, or left broke. One side says an agent that never tires, never forgets, and works nights is "enough skin." Others reply that this redefines the term, and one notes that Opus still ignores instructions daily. A related line holds that an offshore team at least has a roof to protect, while an AI has no self-preservation. Others counter that offshore shops are often dysfunctional too: junior staff swapped in, and one story of 40 billed hours for a one-line CSS fix. Rebuttals to the premise:
- AI can be "fired" by cancelling the subscription.
- Reinforcement training can make consequences equivalent.
- Financially independent people are not automatically bad hires. The original poster responded that such hires work well only when other human motivations are in play.

**"You're still an IC."** One commenter rejects the manager framing entirely. Managers aren't expected to be deep in code, but the agent operator is legally and practically responsible for the output, must build their own QA and review, and will sometimes have to read and fix the code. Companies don't see these people as managers.

**The pipeline problem.** Another commenter pressed the essay's open question: how do you go hunting inside the box if you only ever do high-level work? Someone with low-level skill must still write the tests and validations, and when someone is sued, humans will need to be trained. The top commenter said he built his skill through decades of tedious work and isn't sure how juniors get it now. His suggestions were community meetups and online discussion, plus experts narrating their reasoning. One commenter's employer requires manual approval of every agent command, which accidentally teaches the AI's reasoning. Their darker prediction is a guild of pre-AI-trained troubleshooters who parachute in when agent blobs fail.

**Counterpoints.** One commenter compared this to compilers, which didn't require juniors to learn assembly, and added that almost no programmers are legally liable for their code. The top commenter replied that organizations that actively helped juniors adapt did better than those that left them alone.

**A challenge to the essay's metric.** A final commenter said stressed developers are only a proxy for product failure. They know growth-stage companies where devs are stressed, shipping is up, and incidents are down, so intuition about when to trust agents may not match how agents actually fail.

### Wikipedia's Cleanup of AI-Generated Content

#### [Submission URL](https://spectrum.ieee.org/wikipedia-wikiproject-ai-writing-cleanup) | 497 points | by [rbanffy](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=rbanffy) | [235 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50257326)

**Wikipedia's volunteer AI cleanup crew says the tells have moved from cliché to structure**, which makes the job harder but not hopeless. This is an IEEE Spectrum interview with Ellia Lebleu, a cofounder of WikiProject AI Cleanup and an AI Ph.D. student at Inria in Rennes.

The project started in December 2023, when a handful of editors on Wikipedia's Discord noticed the same strange articles. They leaned on phrases like "stands as a testament" and "rich cultural heritage," and some carried conclusion sections or "Why is this important?" headers. Those were easy to catch. By 2025 the signals had become subtler: the "rule of three," negative parallelisms, and other patterns that mean little alone but add up. Editors also look at behavior, such as one account posting five multi-thousand-word articles in five minutes.

**Citations are now the bigger worry.** Fully hallucinated references have become rarer because retrieval-augmented generation lets models find real sources. The more common failure is a real source that doesn't support the claim it's attached to, which is harder to catch and does more damage to trust.

The interview also covers English Wikipedia's recent ban on using LLMs to generate articles. Lebleu has been working with AI-detection company Pangram to estimate how much AI content still gets through. She says she tested it on pre-ChatGPT Wikipedia text and found no false positives. The article text cuts off there, so the actual prevalence estimate isn't visible in what I have.

The thread mostly wanders off the interview's specifics, but a few real threads emerge about what AI cleanup looks like from the ground.

**Cleanup is imperfect in both directions.** One commenter who fixed obviously AI-generated text said their edits were instantly reverted by ClueBot, Wikipedia's automated anti-vandalism bot, and gave up. Others pointed out that ClueBot's reverts can themselves be reverted (it won't re-revert, but flags the edit for human review). They also noted it is most overzealous on new or temporary accounts making large changes. Several offered to look at the page, but the commenter declined to name it, which prompted the retort that complainers rarely supply links. Another commenter defended that reluctance as avoiding an off-site brigade. Separately, one user said an article about their own site was deleted after its creator was banned for mass-producing AI articles, and nobody vouched for it. Their point: Wikipedia content is less permanent than it feels.

**Where the AI comes from.** A commenter noted an AI-heavy edit that traced to a school-linked editing program. A reply from someone familiar with WikiEdu said it coordinates assignments where professors have students edit Wikipedia, saw students adding AI text, and has trained them to use AI for finding sources rather than generating content.

**Related warnings from other communities.** The top thread links an Old School RuneScape wiki dev post about AI-driven plugin floods: the median new plugin went from thousands of installs to 28. Commenters argued over whether that shows good software being buried by junk or merely a flood of code (one said "more code than ever," yet software seems to be shrinking). A tangent covered friends getting banned for running AI-written macros.

**Practical tools and data.**
- A GPTZero-based provenance checker for Wikipedia pages was shared.
- Others discussed "low-background steel" (pre-AI text), with archive.org dumps from 2022 suggested as clean snapshots.
- One user noted AI answers rarely link Wikipedia. A reply said models don't pick authoritative sources but autocomplete plausible ones.

**Money and bias sidebars.**
- On whether AI firms would pay for cleaner data, a claim that Wikipedia is in "survival mode" was rebutted: it has a sizable endowment, steady edit counts, rising revenue, and tiny hosting costs.
- A thread about whether donation banners count as ads went nowhere.
- A separate branch argued that human editorial bias is the bigger problem, and it quickly turned into a partisan fight over Israel/Gaza coverage with opposing claims of propaganda. It was not resolved.

One quip, that the project effectively gives AI free RLHF to pass a Turing test for Wikipedia editors, captured a real worry: cleanup rules double as training signal.

### The Mythical Man-Month in the Age of AI

#### [Submission URL](https://j11g.com/the-mythical-man-month-in-the-age-of-ai) | 472 points | by [ilreb](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ilreb) | [349 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50255298)

**Brooks's Law survives AI agents; the overhead just moves from managing people to managing agents.** The author's argument starts from a laptop-and-projector anecdote and gets to the 1975 claim that adding manpower to a late project makes it later.

The case for it being obsolete is real: agents onboard in milliseconds, skip stand-ups, and have no office politics. But the "Baby Problem" still applies, because nine agents can't deliver a baby in a month and sequential tasks stay sequential. The coordination cost lands on you. Someone has to define "done", review the code, catch hallucinations, and stitch the pieces together.

The sharpest point is the failure mode: put several autonomous agents on one codebase without clear orchestration and they rewrite each other's work in a fast loop. The proposed update to the law is "Adding agents to a project with unclear requirements gets you the wrong answer faster."

It's a short opinion post with no data or experiments behind it. The thesis is plausible, but it rests on the author's intuition rather than measurement.

The thread barely touches Brooks's Law. It turns into a debate about AI as an amplifier of existing developer quality, and then about why some developers have stopped caring.

**The amplifier thesis.** The top comment (freefaler) says strong programmers now ship good solutions much faster, while weak ones repeat their old mistakes without checking the output. The example was a PR rejected three times because "not in the frontend" was still partly implemented there. Others compressed this into slogans: AI as an "amplifier of human intent," a "power of two" joke (0.5² vs 1.5²), and "competence is additive, incompetence is multiplicative."

**The counter-report: skill erosion hits good developers too.** joshAg disputes the "good ones get better" premise. The unpushed baseline is the stock "I am Qwen, a large language model developed by Alibaba Cloud."

The browsable library lets you filter by layer, strength, weirdness and coherence. There's a per-layer atlas colored by reply category and a CSV download. Pairs and triples of neurons are also covered, and the site includes a few other models (talkie is named), run with the same recipe. Sexual, hateful, violent and self-harm replies are hidden behind a toggle.

The limitations are stated candidly, and they matter:
- **One prompt only.** A neuron that yields a persona on "Who are you?" may do nothing, or something else, elsewhere. So these are not clean "persona neurons."
- **Weak reproducibility.** Under strong pushes, about a third of replies change when only the batch layout changes. Of 580 clear personas rerun in three layouts, 536 reproduced.
- **Unaudited labels.** Category, persona name, weirdness and coherence all come from a Claude Haiku judge. No human has checked them.

Read it as a large, searchable map of how fragile the assistant identity is under activation steering, not as evidence that identities are stored in individual neurons. The numerical-noise finding is arguably as interesting as the personas themselves.

The thread skips the neuron-sweep details and turns into a debate over whether the result says anything about "thinking" or consciousness in LLMs. Almost nobody discusses the methodology; one opening comment raises hardware reliability.

**The hardware aside.** senileape connects the numerical-noise finding to cosmic-ray bit flips and asks whether AI accelerators used for decision-making will need ECC. Nobody picked this up.

**The main argument: does fragility show LLMs don't think?** proc0 opens by saying this kind of research should settle the "do LLMs think" question, calling the models pattern matchers at scale. The replies push back from several directions:
- **Brain damage analogy.** viraptor points out that local brain injuries can change personality dramatically. LLMs, trained for minimal redundancy, would show a more extreme version of the same effect. fzeindl counters that this takes damage to thousands of neurons, not one. kuhsaft and busssard answer on scale. Brains have tens of billions of neurons against hundreds of thousands in the model, so by percentage one Qwen neuron carries far more weight.
- **Emergence.** thoughtpeddler argues that surface personality is an emergent property, so flipping behavior via one unit doesn't show absence of thought. Human connectomics might show similar effects from tiny clusters. 0-_-0 asks why the same couldn't be done to a human. proc0 grants an analog but doubts a single neuron could yield a completely different yet coherent response.
- **Identical mechanism?** proc0 says we only know thinking from biology, so a match should be near-identical, or absent a predictive theory it is probably something else. adjejmxbdjdn and mellab2 reply that thinking can have many implementations, and that planes fly without flapping.
- **Fake versus real behavior.** proc0 argues there are more ways to fake conscious-looking behavior than to land on a different real one (Chinese room, philosophical zombie). kuhsaft asks why identical behavior should count as fake, and argues thinking and consciousness are behaviors, not processes. GoToRO and cyanydeez suggest that consciousness may itself be pattern matching, and that many people behave much as LLMs do.
- **Inputs.** proc0 says humans read via visual representations, not tokens. kuhsaft replies that this is only a different signal conversion, since light is also turned into neural firing. The thread cuts off mid-argument on quantization.

**Spiders and ethics.** kuhsaft uses jumping spiders (they think, and small brains don't preclude it) to raise the moral risk of labeling things non-conscious, predicting AGI will be treated like industrial livestock. adjejmxbdjdn flips it, hoping AI doesn't treat us as we treat animals. proc0 sees farming as a necessary tradeoff for civilization, while still considering mass farming bad.

**Jokes.** JPLeRouzic quips that the model answers correctly 96% of the time and shows humor in the rest. busssard responds that calling itself an "industrial killing machine" isn't great humor.

The crux is unresolved: proc0 wants a theory of consciousness or a biologically matched result before granting that LLMs think, while the other side treats thinking as defined by behavior and substrate-independent.
