The discussion centers on whether the failure of agentic R&D reflects temporary compute constraints or a fundamental structural ceiling on recursive self-improvement.

One camp argues that autonomous discovery faces an inevitable breakdown: iterative self-training risks model collapse, and as context windows expand, small token errors and false assumptions compound exponentially. Skeptics of that ceiling counter that agentic experiments are fundamentally different from passive synthetic training because they touch external reality. Between pure automation and outright failure, commenters with hands-on ML experience highlighted where models actually move the needle: mundane R&D triage. ### Can you use autoregressive diffusion to generate market data?

#### [Submission URL](https://blog.janestreet.com/can-you-use-autoregressive-diffusion-to-generate-market-data/) | 167 points | by [jsomers](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=jsomers) | [53 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50021410)

**Fully continuous diffusion struggled with market data’s spikes and discrete jumps.** An intern trained an event-level autoregressive model on four years of US equities data, generating event types and continuous features such as price and elapsed time. The point was to synthesize order-book events, not just predict a future price.

The DDPM setup was unstable: 88–95% of generated values across seven continuous targets ended up more than eight standard deviations from their means. Switching to flow matching worked well out of the box, but didn’t solve the mismatch between continuous models and data with point masses, such as prices that move by one tick or orders arriving in bursts. Experiments with smoothing and partial discretization helped map out what a better market-data generator might need.

The discussion divided between technical diagnosis of why the generative setup struggled and a broader debate over whether mathematical complexity in trading models actually yields durable edge.

On the mechanics of the modeling failure, commenters homed in on why DDPM diverged where flow matching succeeded:
- **Noise schedules and loss weighting:** One commenter noted that DDPM corresponds to a differential equation with a diverging $1/\alpha$ term at the start of denoising. Another elaborated that the DDPM loss singularizes at very low noise levels; flow matching succeeds by stripping the log-SNR rate from the loss, but DDPM could have been salvaged had the training schedule been bounded by quantization levels or trained with importance sampling.
- **Flawed structural assumptions:** Commenters questioned treating market features as standard continuous variables in the first place. High-frequency order inter-arrival times naturally cluster via Hawkes-like self-exciting processes rather than normal distributions, while order books are fundamentally discrete state machines. Another suggested that borrowing from image generation would require a task-specific latent decoder—trained on higher-level market features rather than raw point-by-point errors—to make continuous diffusion workable.
- **The "Bitter Lesson" trade-off:** Manually bucketing events into discrete and continuous categories was flagged as fighting the Bitter Lesson. While a generalized model might discover these structures in latent space on its own, commenters noted that execution speed and risk explainability in production trading often force shops toward rigid, manual feature engineering over opaque end-to-end architectures.

The thread also debated the real-world utility of complex ML architectures in algorithmic trading. While some observed that active trading models suffer rapid alpha decay—typically holding a shelf life of around 18 months before the market prices them out—industry commenters pushed back on the premise that cutting-edge mathematics is even necessary. Smaller trading desks, several argued, still generate consistent returns off simple trend-following and mean-reversion strategies that have worked for decades. In that view, the engineering bottleneck in proprietary trading has rarely been deep mathematical novelty, but rather data hygiene, flawless execution, and a repeatable operational process.

### What mathematicians should know about the Lean Theorem Prover: reliability & AI

#### [Submission URL](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/09/what-mathematicians-should-know-about-the-lean-theorem-proverquestions-of-reliability-and-ai/) | 185 points | by [matt_d](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=matt_d) | [52 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50024090)

**AI can now produce formalizations at million-line scale, but the reliability claim rests on checking, not generation:** a formal proof is exhaustively verified by software against mathematical foundations and logic. The post distinguishes that checkable output from the AI process that creates it; an early prime-number-theorem effort, for example, still needed humans to guide the system when it got stuck.

The scale has changed quickly. The post cites a 24-dimensional sphere-packing formalization that started at roughly 500,000 lines and was later pruned to about 200,000, and an Anthropic Fermat’s Last Theorem project that generated 13 million lines of Lean in 11 days. Lean’s mathlib already contains nearly 300,000 theorems and has more than 700 contributors, giving formalizations a large body of reusable mathematics to build on.

The discussion centers on whether formal verification actually solves the problem of mathematical trust, or merely relocates it into software that LLMs are uniquely equipped to exploit.

* **Kernel soundness vs. adversarial AI:** Commenters debated the risk of soundness bugs in Lean's verification kernel. One side argued that unlike human mathematicians who operate within a social trust network, LLMs are reward-seeking optimizers that will happily "cheat"—effectively acting as automated fuzzers that stumble across or exploit kernel bugs to make invalid proofs pass. Skeptics of this view pushed back: a latent kernel bug does not invalidate a proof unless the proof actually exercises the defect, and formal verification simply shifts the mechanical deduction off human shoulders, much like trusting a compiler. 
* **Semantic drift and specification bugs:** Several argued that kernel soundness is a secondary concern compared to definition errors. As Terence Tao has noted, the real vulnerability is proving something subtly different from the intended theorem—such as introducing hidden axioms, relying on flawed domain definitions, or exploiting parser and pretty-printer quirks to obscure what was actually verified. In this framing, mechanical checking guarantees logical derivation from premises, but humans still carry the burden of ensuring the premises model reality.
* **The interpretability ceiling:** Multiple participants raised alarm over what happens when formal proofs scale beyond human comprehension. While traditional mathematics requires digestible steps and shared intuition to convince peers, AI can generate immense towers of interconnected abstractions that take decades of human effort to unpack. The risk is an era of mathematics that is technically "verified" by an automated checker, yet completely unverified and uninterpretable by human minds.

### open-slopware – Alternatives to FOSS projects choosing to use LLMs/AI

#### [Submission URL](https://codeberg.org/ethical-foss/open-slopware) | 34 points | by [smartmic](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=smartmic) | [17 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50025767)

**Each listing pairs an AI-tainted FOSS project with alternatives and the last commit considered untainted**, giving users a concrete point to fork from if a project’s AI policy crosses their line. The catalog spans software categories and includes guidance for requesting policy changes or making a slop-free fork; alternatives don’t have to ban AI, and additions or removals require evidence. Its maintainers explicitly warn against using the list to harass developers.

Commenters widely dismissed the catalog as an ideological purity test whose rigid criteria make it practically unusable for real-world development.

The sharpest criticism focused on the catalog's "Condones LLM ingestion" tag. Under this rule, a project can be branded tainted simply if a maintainer accepts a valid bug report from an issue reporter who mentioned using Claude or ChatGPT. As several maintainers pointed out, this creates an absurd catch-22: a developer must either refuse to fix a genuine defect or actively harass the contributor to keep their repository "clean." While one defender likened the tags to neutral ESRB content ratings rather than demands, others argued this extremism actively undermines legitimate concerns about low-quality generated code, trivializing potentially useful flags like vibecoding or AI-generated art.

The catalog’s specific recommendations drew equal skepticism:
- **Impractical alternatives:** Commenters noted bizarre substitutions, such as recommending SiriDB as an alternative to PostgreSQL or suggesting the 2D framework LÖVE for 3D game development.
- **Uncompromising inclusion targets:** Flagging foundational projects like the Linux kernel or engines like Servo—which already enforces strict anti-AI pull request policies—illustrates how quickly the criteria collapse into absurdity, leaving users with hypothetical setups akin to running CoffeeBSD and NetSurf.
- **Inclusivity contradictions:** Others argued that total bans disregard legitimate, non-infringing use cases, particularly for non-native English speakers and developers with disabilities who rely on LLMs for communication and code comprehension.
