### Claude reveals ECDSA curve origins

#### [Submission URL](https://www.anthropic.com/research/ecdsa-curve-origins) | 996 points | by [ahlCVA](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ahlCVA) | [370 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122800)

**Claude traced the NIST curve seeds to `lrand48` seeded with Unix timestamps, not to the hash of a joke passphrase NSA staff had long hinted at.** Anthropic says an internal research model solved it in under half an hour, without being given the passphrase rumor, after several employees had burned time chasing that lead.

The mechanism, as described, works like this:
- The generator draws 20 bytes per iteration as a candidate seed. P-256 needed 1,842 iterations to land on the published seed.
- Claude guessed 1990s C library PRNGs were involved and wrote a scanner that slid bit by bit along each seed, testing 34 generator and output-slice combinations.
- The giveaway was the top bit: four of the ten seeds had a zero there in all 20 words, a 1/5,810 chance. That fits `lrand48`, which returns only the top 31 bits of a 48-bit state. Brute-forcing the 2¹⁷ hidden bits recovered the state.
- The prime-curve seeds kept fewer bits (`lrand48() >> 23`), so Claude recovered the state with LLL lattice reduction over every bit window. Five NIST prime-curve seeds and five ANSI X9.62 seeds all matched, each regenerating the full 160 bits.

The post covers P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521. Anthropic is explicit that this is not a break or a new attack. It argues the result makes a deliberate backdoor less plausible, since the seeds came from a common PRNG initialized from the clock, which anyone can now reproduce. The post also tells readers there's nothing to change. Most researchers already trusted the curves.

That "more confident" claim is an inference, not a proof. Reproducible seeds show how the numbers were generated, but not why the NSA picked those particular outputs. The post links Filippo Valsorda's write-up for independent coverage.

The central argument is whether a seed that can now be reproduced actually says anything about how it was *selected*, and Filippo Valsorda (FiloSottile) gave the strongest reply in the thread.

**The selection-bias worry.** fidotron noted that the discarded candidates are invisible. The authors might have searched timestamps for a curve with a hidden property and simply never disclosed the timestamp, since "doing and saying nothing" costs nothing. schiffern added that a confusing cover story (the "hashed passphrase" history) is standard spy-craft, and that NSA employs enough mathematicians that a real breakthrough could be hidden behind a boring story. 12aft5l asked whether other generator functions might also have been tried before settling on `drand48`.

**The counter: freedom is too small.** FiloSottile argued that even if someone tried every 1997 timestamp and picked among the first 16 viable candidates, that is only about 30 bits of freedom. A class of weak 256-bit curves dense enough to hit with about 2^30 random tries would itself be a cryptanalytic breakthrough on the scale of breaking ECC outright. He pointed to section 3.1 of Koblitz and Menezes, which found this implausible even with 160 bits of freedom. mswphd added that unless the weakness correlates with LCG structure, LCG draws are effectively random, and such a correlation would be stranger than the random search. edelbitter proposed one route: an indirect weakness at the implementation level, such as side channels exposed by querying many related curves. That would involve the LCG and key derivation, not ECC itself.

**Why the seed counts grow.** Asked by a self-described layman, cyphar answered that most small `n` values aren't valid curves, so a larger `n` reflects searching for valid seeds. Also, the smaller curves (prime192, prime239) needed larger `n` and used an earlier timestamp. About half of the valid seeds were discarded for reasons not yet understood, though a buggy validity checker seems plausible to cyphar.

**NSA's track record.** The thread then turned to history. Dual EC DRBG, the DES key-length compromise and 3DES were cited as reasons to distrust NSA. tptacek said Dual EC is a utterly different construction and that we now know the seeds were essentially "nothing up my sleeve", just undisclosed. His long counter on IPsec and CBC IVs held that the predictable-IV design came from standards-body culture, not NSA. He said Phil Rogaway's petition was mocked, so "all NSA usually needs to do is sit back." rurban claimed NSA paid IPsec developers to include weak algorithms, and tptacek disputed this. duskwuff and tptacek also pushed back on the claim that TLS's CBC design was a deliberate backdoor. afiori noted that a shorter key length isn't automatically sinister. teiferer objected to quoting AI-generated text as if it were authoritative.

**Other threads:**
- feanaro asked why Valsorda would now lean toward the NIST curves over Curve25519. commandersaki guessed it's the prime-order versus cofactor distinction, and the question went unanswered.
- bob1029 asked why not generate a new curve openly. mitxela replied that ed25519 already exists.
- defen observed that the community can now vary the seed inputs and compare the resulting curves for any anomaly.
- A couple of comments complained about flagging and "wrongthink", and one linked a Bernstein post criticizing the rhetoric. These were mostly meta-argument, not substance.

The thread never settled whether "reproducible seed" implies "innocent selection," but the strongest argument was quantitative: the room for hidden selection was too small to plausibly hide a break.

### 90% of US working hours are spent on tasks not covered well by AI benchmarks

#### [Submission URL](https://www.mercor.com/research/labor-index/benchmark-map/) | 578 points | by [speckx](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=speckx) | [525 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121179)

**Only 208 of 923 US occupations have even one task covered by at least 5 benchmark prompts, and Mercor estimates that about 90% of American working hours go to tasks with little or no benchmark coverage.** The map is Mercor's first pass at a continuously updated index of what AI evals actually measure across the economy.

The method: take the 18,797 O*NET tasks across 923 occupations, then have an LLM judge tag each prompt from a curated list of 29 benchmarks (924 prompts-sets in the index) with the O*NET tasks it corresponds to. A task counts as "covered" at 5 or more mapped prompts. Coverage is then weighted by LLM-estimated time shares per task (from Hatgis-Kessell et al.) to get the working-hours figure. Six of 22 job families have no covered occupations at all.

Coverage isn't random. A task-level logistic regression finds that benchmark coverage skews toward occupations with large wage bills, and the long tail of smaller, middle-income knowledge work is undercovered. Their example: benchmarks heavily cover AI-exposed lawyers' tasks but barely touch legal secretaries or court clerks, whose work is similarly exposed. By family, Computer & Math is at 29/36 occupations, while Healthcare Practitioners is 12/89 and Education & Library is 9/62.

Caveats worth holding onto:
- **The 29-benchmark list is Mercor's own selection.** It favors public, agentic, economically realistic, not-yet-saturated benchmarks, caps benchmarks per job family, and includes Mercor's own APEX set, so the 90% number is conditional on those choices.
- **Two LLM-derived layers sit under the headline:** prompt-to-task tagging (validated by human experts only on the APEX subset) and task time shares.
- **"Covered" is a low bar.** Five prompts says a task is touched, not that a model's performance on it is well characterized.

The direction of the finding is plausible and useful; the precise 90% deserves a wide error bar.

### It Ain't AGI Until H100s Go for $30 an Hour

#### [Submission URL](https://corbt.com/posts/it-aint-agi-until-h100s-go-for-30-an-hour) | 24 points | by [gmays](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=gmays) | [8 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50118605)

**Corbitt's thesis: if AGI means an agent that can replace a white-collar worker, the H100-hour should price at about $30, roughly 8.5x today's $3.52.** That's the conservative case; looser assumptions push it to $75.

The mechanism is a supply-and-demand equilibrium. A full-time Opus 5.5 agent costs about $8.21 an hour at API list price. Assume half of that is compute, and one H100 supports roughly 0.86 agents running around the clock. If each agent is worth at least $35 an hour (the wage of the 95 millionth-highest earner in the world), an H100-hour is worth 0.86 × $35, or about $30. At today's price, he says, AGI agents would want about 28x more compute than exists.

Key inputs, all of them estimates:
- **Supply:** AI 2040's figures put the world at about 50M H100-equivalents by October, with about 25M serving customers. That is enough for about 95M full-time workers once you account for agents running 8,760 hours a year versus 2,000 for a human.
- **Capability:** An AGI-complete agent needs no more hardware than Opus 5.5 does today, justified by capability cost falling 5-10x a year.
- **Margin:** The 50% compute share is called "wildly conservative." At an 80% margin (SemiAnalysis's estimate for Anthropic's API), one H100 runs 2.1 agents, the fleet covers about 240M workers, and an H100-hour is worth about $41.

The post includes an interactive calculator where you can change the wage, compute multiple, margin, and price.

The framing is useful because it gives a falsifiable market signal instead of a benchmark argument. The H100 rental price is public, and it has already risen 18% this year, which is odd for aging hardware. The weak points are the assumptions the whole result hangs on: that today's frontier model footprint is enough for AGI, and that every top-95M earner's job is substitutable. The post is co-written with Claude Opus, and its wage-floor estimate for non-US earners is admittedly a guess.

The thread mostly attacks the post's method, and one commenter turns the calculator on its own thesis.

**The calculator can say "we're already there."** fred_dawg argues that a first-generation AGI would probably be inefficient, since engines, rockets and programs are built for results before efficiency. They assume it needs at least 10x Opus's compute. Plugging that in gives a GPU price of $3 an hour, which is today's price, so by the model's own logic AGI has already arrived. They think the author has the direction right, but that collapsing many supply and demand curves, each with its own research frontier, into one equation may fit past data and still fail as a forecast. jhylands makes a related point from the other side. At a $7 minimum wage and a roughly 10% Anthropic margin, they say "we are already there."

**Whether supply and demand can be multiplied out.** kelseyfrog gives the most fundamental objection. Empirical supply and demand curves for any real good don't exist; the textbook diagrams are teaching tools. Treating one as fact, they argue, undermines every downstream conclusion.

**The definition of AGI.** Two commenters challenge the premise that this definition is the one that matters.
- clbrmbr says a jagged capability frontier could produce AI that is vastly superhuman in areas that matter, up to existential or disempowerment risk, without meeting the "replaces a white-collar worker" bar.
- brianwawok replies that nobody agrees on what AGI means anymore, and that we're close enough to call it.

**If the compute is so valuable, why not use it?** hyperhello asks why AI labs don't spend the compute to get rich themselves. K0balt answers that intelligence doesn't equal instant wins: putting the 100 smartest people in one room doesn't guarantee profit, and AI is no different. They call it the underwear gnome problem.

**Substitution is already happening.** xyzsparetimexyz dismisses the post as "idiot economics." They say AI is already taking jobs by undercutting them, much as offshoring did, without needing to hit a threshold.

The thread doesn't settle whether a single threshold price is meaningful, or whether the substitution is already underway at lower prices.
