### Show HN: A decent text-to-image model trained for ~$20K (Apache 2.0 Weights)

#### [Submission URL](https://www.linum.ai/field-notes/pjit-21k) | 115 points | by [schopra909](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=schopra909) | [13 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50368208)

**A 2B-parameter pixel-space diffusion model, trained for about $21K on 660M samples, ships with Apache 2.0 weights and code.** It's a pressure test: Linum, a two-person lab, wanted to see whether their Pyramid-JiT (P-JiT) architecture holds up at 6-12x the training length of their earlier 50-100M-sample experiments.

The architecture is the real story. P-JiT drops the VAE entirely and lets the diffusion model learn its own compression, using 32×32 patches instead of the usual 16×16. That cuts attention context by 4x: a 512² image takes 256 image tokens, the same count a 256² image needed before. They report 11x faster convergence than their VAE+DiT predecessor, Linum v2.

Two training tricks make it work:
- **A noise schedule** tuned for pixel-space models, which helps them pick up fine-grained detail.
- **Low-res readout heads** along the DiT trunk, so early blocks get stronger gradient signal. A PixelREPA head aligns to DINOv3 features. All of these heads are training-only (~0.24B params) and are dropped at sampling.

Other specs:
- 2.18B params at sampling
- 22 DiT blocks
- Frozen Qwen3.5-4B text encoder
- 512² and 1024² stages

The motive is video. Attention is O(n²), so shrinking the token budget is the cheapest lever on video inference cost, and image models are the prerequisite ("learn nouns before verbs"). This is a stepping stone toward their Linum v3 video model, not a finished product, and the post itself labels it an experimental release with a section on limitations. The headline claim of "decent" quality comes from their own 249-sample gallery, so independent comparisons against other open models at similar budgets are what's missing.

The thread is mostly admiration plus a cluster of questions the authors haven't answered in the visible comments. The most persistent one is **training data provenance**: mkl, Robin_Message, and robotswantdata all ask what the 660M images and prompts are, where they came from, and what they cover. mkl notes the README says text was excluded, and robotswantdata asks bluntly whether the data is open or scraped like the big labs'. No answer appears.

**Quality skepticism** runs alongside the praise:
- mkl says it looks good but "almost every hand is wrong" and guesses a training-data cause.
- gunalx says it shares typical text-to-image failures: over-smoothing, uncanny shine, and mangled limbs.
- micw jokes that the finger counts look right, then wonders how much cherry-picking went into the showcase, given the failures visible elsewhere.

**Why this work is rare:** tensor argues big labs are disincentivized to build efficient models because compute access is a moat. Chinese labs, by contrast, have no such incentive, which tensor offers as the reason most breakthrough efficiency work comes from China. yhjc2692 is surprised that dropping the VAE works so well and points to a paper on training a LLaMA2-13B-beating LLM for under $0.1M as a similar cheap-training result.

**Follow-ups and pointers:**
- fzysingularity asks about driving cost down to roughly $1K, an "arena" for small experiments, and the minimum viable budget.
- Valk3_ asks about the hardest challenges and where video-efficiency research is heading. yhjc2692 (not the authors) points to efficient-attention work like DeepSeek's and Kimi's, to fewer-step sampling such as reflow, and to filtering the CVPR 2026 video-generation papers.
- gigel82 wonders about quantization and running it in-browser via WebGPU. Swapping the text encoder for something like T5-small or CLIP could, by their estimate, bring it under 2.5GB at INT4.

### AI is forcing us to pay off 40 years of technical debt

#### [Submission URL](https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-in-cybersecurity-php2shell-vulnerability) | 1255 points | by [pseudolus](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=pseudolus) | [733 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50364336)

**A crash bug reported to the PHP Foundation in 2024 sat unfixed until an AI-assisted audit turned it into remote code execution.** Triagers had deprioritized it because an out-of-memory error normally just crashes the program. Then, per the article, a Calif researcher using AI tools found a way to exploit it within two weeks of starting an OpenAI-sponsored audit of PHP.

The result is PHP2Shell, which exposed sites running WordPress, Drupal, and other PHP stacks (the article cites NASA, Wikipedia, and the White House) to data theft and arbitrary code execution on the host. Researchers reported it responsibly and a patch shipped before anyone is known to have exploited it.

The author's argument is that this is a preview, not a one-off. Matt Suiche of Tolmo says 40 years of human-written legacy code is "being exposed" and the technical debt is coming due. Columbia's Suman Jana explains why: turning a crash into a working exploit means painstaking reasoning about jumping to a specific memory location in a particular running program. That is tedious work that people skipped, and AI handles it well.

The practical implication is that "just a crash" bugs in your backlog can no longer be safely ignored, because the cost of proving them exploitable has dropped. The excerpt cuts off before the article's remedies, so I can't say what it recommends beyond that.

The thread leaves the PHP bug almost immediately and becomes a debate over whether AI-driven bug hunting leads to provably correct software. The substantive pushback is that "proven" does not mean "safe."

**The optimistic case.** Arbortheus hopes the flood of AI-found CVEs also flushes out zero-days that nation-states and spyware vendors have been sitting on, leaving more robust software in the long run. Loquebantur extrapolates further: if AI can find every bug, the only defense is provable code, which realistically requires AI to produce. That would force "software engineering" to become real engineering, though only if humans actually understand what is being proven. eru says verified code "gets you pretty far already," and jopsen compares verification to "a slightly more aggressive fuzzer" whose aim is fewer bugs, not none.

**The skeptical case.**
- **Knuth's caveat:** Quimoniz invoked it, and raphlinus supplied the exact quote ("I have only proved it correct, not tried it"). Others offered Dijkstra and Hoare lines.
- **Proving the wrong thing:** Loquebantur conceded that you can prove the wrong property, so the LLM can't be the only party that understands the domain. Quimoniz added that a misconfiguration, such as a driver set to `sqlite` instead of `sqlite3`, can still produce unexpected behavior.
- **Memory safety only goes so far:** skydhash noted that it doesn't stop malicious but valid input, logic errors, or problems in kernel code. moomin doubted that something like Kubernetes could be proven at all, calling it "pure frustration."
- **Proof in practice:** hinkley said researchers "proved" his company's code-signing system sound, yet he kept finding security holes for months afterward.

**Language choice as a cheaper route.** svachalek argued that you don't need full formal proof. Languages without pointer arithmetic, unchecked array access, or use-after-free remove whole classes of holes, and eru said using them is "pretty close to a mathematical proof." skydhash replied that every language ultimately sits on an ISA and an OS.

**Side debates.**
- **Configuration:** one sub-thread asked whether software is "incorrect" if it breaks under a user-chosen option. MisterMunchkin said yes. wolrah pointed to the zero-one-infinity rule. hinkley countered that software only has to support the configurations its makers intend, and the Porsche analogy ran several replies without converging.
- **Coverage mandates:** a tangent on testing culture followed. annzabelle described teams gaming 80% coverage rules with tests that asserted nothing, and hinkley said such mandates backfire, especially at 90%. Both argued that inconclusive tests are worse than none because they create false trust.
- **LLMs and attacker skill:** solyin argued LLMs mean we must assume any user can learn JTAG or shellcode tricks. wizzwizz4 replied that books and librarians always provided this, so the threat surface isn't much bigger.
- **Knuth:** hinkley closed on a critique of Knuth's idealized cost model. Algorithms that treat memory access as constant ignore physical limits, so practice has drifted far from theory.

Nobody disputes that AI lowers the cost of finding and exploiting bugs. The open question is whether verification can keep pace, given that proofs cover only what someone thought to specify.

The architecture change worth noticing: Moonshot swapped K3's full-attention Gated MLA layers for Gated DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) to make long context cheaper. The stack is now 69 KDA layers plus 24 Gated DSA layers (93 total). Each token routes to 16 of 896 experts, plus 2 shared. Weights are MXFP4 with MXFP8 activations, trained quantization-aware, so the released checkpoint is the low-precision one rather than a post-hoc squeeze. The vision encoder is the 401M-parameter MoonViT-V2.

**Moonshot's benchmark table is mixed against closed frontier models**, and it's honest about it:

The catch is methodology. Most coding numbers use the Claude Code harness for Kimi but leaderboard or vendor-reported maxima for rivals. At 2.8T parameters, "open weights" still means a multi-node deployment, and the model card text I saw doesn't cover hardware requirements or license terms.

### Ripple-65B Preview: U.S. Open-Weight SOTA on Agentic Coding

#### [Submission URL](https://www.ricursive.com/ripple) | 331 points | by [Philpax](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=Philpax) | [120 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50369925)

**A 65B dense model trained for $32M total R&D claims the top U.S. open-weight score on TerminalBench 2.1: 80.52**, narrowly ahead of Reflection Beam 501B (80.1) and well above Meta Muse Spark Open 816B (76.4), Thinking Machines Inkling 975B (63.8), and NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Ultra 550B (56.4). Ricursive Intelligence, a lab building AI chip design tools, says this is a general-purpose variant of the proprietary model behind its design platform.

The cost claim is the headline. $32M covers everything: ladder experiments, data mixing, synthetic data generation, debugging, and the final pretraining and posttraining runs. Ricursive puts that at one-tenth the cost of the prior open-weight SOTA, though by its own admission that comparison is an estimate "to the best of our knowledge."

What they did differently:
- **Dense, not MoE.** They report about 40% MFU throughout training with simple sharding, no custom kernels, and no quantization. The architecture is thin and deep (d_model 5120, 80 layers) with sliding-window attention mixed with global NoPE layers, gated attention, and value residuals.
- **Small RL budget.** They hit 80.5 with 1.4M rollouts, roughly 70x fewer than Reflection Beam. The recipe uses truncated importance sampling, score centering, a linear length penalty, and self-built synthetic environments. They bought no posttraining data.
- **Ladder-driven pretraining.** Starting from Olmo 3's model ladder, they stacked changes that cut compute needed for equivalent code loss (dolma_stack CE) by up to 16x at 4B scale. One idea, 2-stream mHC, was rejected after it failed to improve at 4B. They also report the gap should widen with scale.

Pretraining used 12T tokens with up to 1M context. The small footprint means it can run locally on hardware like NVIDIA DGX Spark. Weights are on Hugging Face, Together AI, GMI Cloud, OpenRouter, and Fireworks.

The catch: this is a "Preview," and the evidence is one benchmark plus self-reported cost and efficiency numbers. The 80.52 vs. 80.1 margin over Reflection Beam is within noise-level territory for a single agentic eval. Whether it holds up on real coding workloads is for independent testing to settle.

### Nvidia RTX Spark encrypted firmware slows inference speed to 35% on Linux

#### [Submission URL](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/linux-nvidia-rtx-spark-laptop) | 608 points | by [novaRom](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=novaRom) | [285 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50362905)

**Native Linux on an RTX Spark laptop runs GPT-OSS-120B at 13.9 tokens/sec, versus 65.4 on Windows**, because the GPU gets pinned near 780 MHz on wall power instead of the ~2.2 GHz it hits on Windows. Temperatures stayed low (the cutoff in the text leaves the exact figures unclear), so this isn't thermal throttling.

The cause, per the write-up, is a Windows-only component, the Nvidia Platform Controllers and Framework, which manages CPU/GPU power delivery. Its firmware power table is encrypted and only the Windows driver can decrypt it, so Linux never learns it's allowed to clock up. Nvidia's open kernel driver handles GPU acceleration fine; it just can't see the power policy.

The benchmarks on the MSI Prestige N16 Flip AI Plus:
- **GPT-OSS-120B:** Windows 65.4 tok/s, WSL 65.1, DGX Spark 61, native Linux 13.9.
- **vLLM, 8 concurrent clients:** WSL 127 tok/s, native Linux 39.5.
- **Long run:** Windows finished in about 8 minutes at ~2.2 GHz; Linux took 37 minutes parked around 790 MHz.

Installing is its own chore. Omarchy gave a flickering display on the MSI, and Debian lacked Wi-Fi, keyboard, touchpad, and GPU support in a vanilla kernel. Omarchy's bundled kernel with the open driver fixed that. Linux also initially saw only 28GB of the 128GB unified memory, since firmware reserves 96GB for the GPU and that split is adjustable only from Windows. Dropping the reservation to 4GB exposed 120GB.

**The practical answer today is WSL**, which rides the Windows driver stack, keeps full boost on or off battery, and still runs Linux-first tooling like vLLM. A real native fix requires Nvidia to port the platform controller's power logic into its Linux driver. No sign in the article that it has committed to doing so.

The thread splits on whether Nvidia (or the OEM) owes Linux users a working driver on day one, with a side argument about whether the encrypted power table is malice or a bug.

**"It's a Windows laptop" vs. "Nvidia sells Linux everywhere else."** The top comment tells people to read the article. In its view this is missing Linux support for firmware built for a new Windows-only platform, and it points to Dynamic Boost, where Nvidia shipped Linux support about two years after Windows. Critics replied that hardware should work equally well everywhere, or at least that Nvidia should publish enough specs for users to fix it. One commenter argued Nvidia claims the best Linux support in the industry and gets most of its business from Linux, so it should deliver. Another countered that OEMs don't have to certify every OS, and others in the chain pointed out that HaikuOS and ReactOS are still developed. A commenter with driver knowledge added detail:
- Nvidia already supports Linux, FreeBSD, and AArch64 CUDA.
- DGX Spark gets this power handling through UEFI.
- The RTX Spark breakage lives in SoC devicetree drivers, because Nvidia skipped standard ACPI/UEFI power management here and has to port the rest by hand.
- This is not a catastrophic oversight.

Others defended "Windows hardware" as a real concept. They cited WinModems and noted that the PC platform is effectively defined by Microsoft's hardware compatibility program.

**Malice or bug?** Zak noted that encrypting the power table does nothing except frustrate third-party driver work. Yunnpp said Nvidia's history of crippling Linux drivers justifies suspicion, and floated, speculatively, that Microsoft requested the nerf as part of the partnership. Keybrd-intrrpt pushed back: DGX Spark and DGX Station run Ubuntu-based Linux, and this launch was a Microsoft partnership focused on Windows. Dist-epoch suggested Nvidia wants to stop people from putting these laptops in data centers. Several commenters rejected that as uneconomic. DGX Sparks are cheaper and more powerful, WSL has minimal overhead, and jasomill noted that Linux runs at full clock on battery and downclocks when plugged in, the reverse of Windows. That looks like an inverted conditional rather than a deliberate lockout.

**Side threads:**
- **Monopoly:** One commenter blamed a "monopoly" that had been allowed to form. Another said Nvidia earned its position through risky bets, such as CUDA costing it a large chunk of its valuation. The argument then turned to whether monopolies decay naturally. U.S. Steel, IBM, and Kodak were cited as examples that eroded through technology shifts rather than competition.
- **Open driver:** The "open" Blackwell kernel module mostly moves logic into signed proprietary GSP firmware, which makes the system less inspectable. Commenters then disputed the blob's size (~500MB claimed, ~160MB total in Arch packages, ~50MB for the biggest blob) and compared AMD's 27MB.
- **Hardware spec:** Roark66 was disappointed that memory bandwidth is still under 300GB/s, where a 6-year-old RTX 3090 had over 900GB/s.
- **Meta:** Some commenters complained that HN reacts to headlines without reading the article.

### Progress on provable inference for large language models

#### [Submission URL](https://www.anthropic.com/research/provable-inference) | 235 points | by [ltononro](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ltononro) | [121 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50368626)

**FlashProve generates zkSNARK proofs that a response came from a specific model, at ~110 tokens/second on a single H100 for Llama 4 Maverick** (400B-parameter MoE, 4-bit weights, integer arithmetic in prefill). Proofs run ~19 MB per 1,024-token request, and proving costs roughly 70-95x the GPU time of running the model on vLLM. The post says prior open-source systems are orders of magnitude slower.

The problem it targets is that users have no way to tell whether the model they selected answered, or whether a router swapped in a cheaper one or an attacker tampered with the token stream. A provider commits to the weights, then proves each response against that commitment. The proof hides the weights but not the architecture, and the verifier needs no access to the model and only modest compute. Evaluators who test models before and after release, and anyone paying for a specific model through a third-party router, are the obvious customers. The authors also pitch it as one piece of a toolkit for verifying compliance with AI pacing requirements.

Claude did much of the optimization work, and the authors admit its design decisions are hard to audit in a system this complex. Their answer is formal verification: top-level security statements are proved in Lean, with an interactive visualizer for auditors, alongside two verifiers, a fast C++ one and a provably correct Lean one.

**Released under Apache 2.0:**
- the blog post and a technical report
- a Claude-written protocol spec
- the prover and both verifiers
- the Lean proofs

The authors call this intermediate progress, not a finished research agenda, and invite others to build on it. A ~70-95x proving overhead rules out proving every production request today, and the 19 MB proof per request is far from the "few megabytes" the intro uses as its illustration. The thread's main fault line is whether zk proofs are the right tool at all, with most of the pushback coming from people who think cheaper mechanisms already cover the use case.

**Cost versus alternatives.** The 70-95x overhead dominates the skeptical side. vessenes notes that a probabilistic check, such as four independent providers running the same unbatched prompt, is far cheaper than a 90-100x compute hit. nemo1618 goes further: for open-weight models you can simply redo the computation, which needs only deterministic inference (something the ZKP approach also needs). For closed models, the options are ZKPs, signed responses, or cross-provider comparison. IanCal countered that the client-side check (17s on a CPU, no GPU) looks cheaper than re-running the request. PeterisP replied that the client still pays for the prover's compute. **Hardware attestation instead of ZKPs.** mike_hearn argued ZKPs have "always been something of a dead end". ### Letting users trust their own MCP clients, without opening a phishing hole

#### [Submission URL](https://streamvessel.com/blog/mcp-trusted-redirect-uris) | 34 points | by [isalexlog](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=isalexlog) | [6 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50367004)

**An open MCP OAuth registration endpoint is a one-click account takeover, but a fixed operator allowlist locks out anyone running their own agent.** Stream Vessel's post is about carving out a middle path for self-hosted clients, such as a Slack bot on a VPS that needs access to your own S3 bucket through the MCP server.

The attack is simple. An anonymous attacker registers a client called "Claude" with a redirect URI on `evil.example`, then sends a signed-in user a legitimate-looking `/oauth/authorize` link. The user sees the real consent screen on the real domain and clicks Approve, and the authorization code goes to the attacker. PKCE doesn't help, because the attacker started the flow. A self-declared client name proves nothing, and `claude-connect.app` passes a glance.

This is already happening:
- A May 2026 study found registration endpoints accepting arbitrary redirect URIs on **114 of 119 OAuth-enabled MCP servers (95.8%)**.
- Obsidian Security reported the chain against Square's MCP server in January 2026, exposing a merchant's profile, transactions and bank details. Square's fix covered only newly registered clients.
- A server that accepts any redirect address also becomes an open redirector on its own domain, since error responses are sent to that address (RFC 9700 covers this case).

The MCP spec requires exact redirect URI validation but leaves "trust" undefined, and its 2026-07-28 revision deprecates Dynamic Client Registration in favor of Client ID Metadata Documents. The post's point is that CIMD tells you who published a client, not whether this particular user meant to connect it. Servers today sit at two poles: a fixed allowlist (safe, but closed to you, as Atlassian and GitHub users have found) or accept-anything (open, but phishable).

The excerpt cuts off just as it reaches the author's actual design for letting users trust their own clients, so I can't say how Stream Vessel does it. The framing of the problem is the strongest part of the piece, and the "post office delivering your house keys" analogy is a good way to explain why the allowlist exists.

The thread is short and splits into three strands: whether the attack is realistic, whether MCP is just rediscovering old OAuth problems, and whether OAuth itself is the culprit.

- **Is the attack realistic?** ImPostingOnHN questions the premise: why would a user click a random link, then approve OAuth access to their own account? The only plausible route they see is a poisoned agent handing over the link mid-session, so the user assumes it's related to their work. foolswisdom effectively answers this from the other side.
