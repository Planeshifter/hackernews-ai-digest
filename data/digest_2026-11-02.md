### Fujifilm to boost tape production 5x as AI fuels archiving demand

#### [Submission URL](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/10/fujifilm-to-boost-tape-production-5x-as-ai-fuels-archiving-demand/) | 331 points | by [bookofjoe](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=bookofjoe) | [136 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50336918)

**Fujifilm is putting roughly 10 billion yen (about $63 million) into a fivefold increase in LTO tape production capacity**, measured against fiscal 2025 levels. It calls this its largest investment to date in dedicated LTO facilities.

The money goes to the Odawara site of its Kanagawa Factory and runs fiscal 2026 through 2028. It covers manufacturing expansion and additional staff, mainly to build **LTO Ultrium10**, the current-generation cartridge. Fujifilm debuted Ultrium10 in 2025 as the first tape to combine barium ferrite with strontium ferrite particles, which it calls "fine hybrid magnetic particles."

Fujifilm's stated reason is generative AI, which it says is driving "exponential growth" in data needs. It says major cloud and data center operators are leaning harder on tape because it stores large volumes cheaply. The second selling point is security: a cartridge can sit in a drawer or vault, physically air-gapped, as a last line of defense against ransomware.

This is Fujifilm's own framing, and the announcement offers no demand figures or customer names to back the AI link. The 5x number is also capacity, not sales. Still, a $63M bet spread over three years is a sign the company expects the archive tier to keep growing, and the LTO consortium's roadmap already projects capacities through Gen 14.

The thread is mostly practitioners trading tape-ops war stories. The central argument is whether tape is reliable in practice or only on paper.

**The cautionary camp.** The top comment, from someone with long hands-on experience, says never trust the write-time "verified" message. Restore the data for real. They say tapes can pass verification and then fail to read, and drives can even fail to read tapes they wrote themselves. Their rule is to never write just one copy. Their examples:
- A DVD-replication workflow always sent two tapes.
- A facility wrote three tapes at once on different drives: one on site, one at Iron Mountain, one to the client. The local copy was sometimes the unreadable one.

Other advice in the thread:
- Use cleaning cartridges.
- Document which archiver wrote the tape, since tar and GUI tools aren't interchangeable.
- Avoid vendor-specific compression and encryption.
- Keep an index (tar has none at the start of the tape).
- Make sure encrypted data survives mid-stream corruption.

**The "never had a problem" camp.** One commenter used about 1,000 tapes over 15 years across LTO, DAT, DDS and 9-track with zero failures. They credited a climate-controlled data center (25C, 50% humidity), regular tape rotation and serviced drives, and contrasted it with monthly RAID failures. Another had a litigation-restore workflow that pulled Iron Mountain LTO tapes in batches of 24 and saw no visible failures, though they never reused tapes. Others countered that their own volumes were orders of magnitude larger and showed plenty of errors. Replies suggested the gap comes from scale, environment, media quality and drive maintenance. One person argued that failures scale superlinearly because tape physically moves.

**Technical points:**
- **Read-after-write:** A commenter asked why drives don't verify with a trailing read head. Replies pointed out that LTO already does this, with a read element behind each write element that rewrites bad data automatically. Another commenter noted a gap: a misaligned drive can write and read consistently with itself but produce a tape that other drives can't read. That would explain the "verified but unreadable" cases.
- **Firmware, not media:** One former backup-software developer said unreliability usually came from drive firmware or drivers, not the tape. One drive wrote random runs of zeros after every N megabytes.
- **Cloud providers:** They spread data across many tapes with erasure coding. Their robotic libraries have built-in verification, and deletion compliance forces them to test reads often. Another commenter noted that crypto-erasure (deleting the key) avoids rewriting tapes at all.

**Cost and access:**
- One user recommends rustic with S3 Glacier Deep Archive at about $1/TB/month, as a secondary backup. Replies asked how you validate a backup you never restore, and another called that price expensive once you do the math.
- Someone asked why there's no prosumer tape drive. The reply, cut off in the thread, points toward used older-generation drives.
- Iron Mountain came up both as a vault that once "misplaced" tapes and as a vendor people want alternatives to.
- Backup software drew complaints: Symantec Backup Exec was called one of the worst programs a commenter had used, and IBM's archive software was said to be better but still bad.

### Launch HN: Momentic (YC W24) – Agents that catch bugs by using your app

#### [Submission URL](https://momentic.ai/) | 158 points | by [wuweiweiwu](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=wuweiweiwu) | [125 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50339913)

**Mo, Momentic's exploratory QA agent, found 269 real bugs in a 60-minute run, versus 86 for Codex, 59 for Claude Code and 27 for Devin**, on a benchmark of 1,396 human-labeled bugs across Gitea, Metabase, Twenty and Saleor. The benchmark is the company's own, so weigh it accordingly.

The tradeoffs are laid out candidly in the post:
- **Recall:** Mo 19.3%, Codex 6.2%, Claude Code 4.2%, Devin 1.9%.
- **Precision:** Codex is slightly ahead at 86% vs. Mo's 84%. Claude Code is at 80%, Devin at 84%.
- **Cost per bug:** Mo $2.67, Codex $0.80, Claude Code $4.09, Devin $3.89. Codex is more than 3x cheaper per bug if you only want the easy catches.
- **Scaling with time:** from 30 to 60 minutes Mo went from 177 to 269 bugs (+52%), while Codex gained 16%.

Mo is a custom harness, not a wrapper around Playwright scripts. It builds a DAG as it explores and forks large product areas into parallel sub-agents, each with its own browser or device. Planner agents reason over screenshots and video, and separate locator agents resolve plain-English targets. Parallel agents avoid stepping on each other through cooperative resource leases: many "using" claims can coexist, but only one "modifying" claim per resource. A tunnel exposes private or local apps to the hosted agents, and a single bug bash can spin up hundreds of concurrent browsers and devices.

The founders' thesis is that scripted E2E tests (hardcoded XPath, text, aria attributes) are the wrong primitive once coding agents write most of the code, and that LLMs checking their own work reinforce their own assumptions. They cite DeepSWE v1.1, where a model scored 66.2% when banned from writing tests versus 65.3% when allowed.

The classic product is still there too. Tests are plain-English YAML kept in your repo, with a CLI whose exit code can gate CI, auto-heal that proposes reviewable test diffs, AI failure triage, and MCP hooks so a coding agent can author and verify tests. The site claims 117,010 bugs caught and 70.6M test runs. Targets are web, iOS and Android, with desktop coming.

Newly shipped is PR testing for Vercel and Cloudflare deploy previews. New signups get $250 in Mo spend. The dataset and harness are public on Hugging Face and GitHub, so the numbers can be checked. Caveat: the judge was an LLM (with a human reviewer), and even Mo's best run recalled under a fifth of the labeled bugs.

### Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen

#### [Submission URL](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/) | 2917 points | by [dbreunig](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=dbreunig) | [879 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50341174)

**An LLM edited 0.016% of Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights directly, with no gradients and no training loop, and MATH-500 went from 7.9% to 48.1%.** The MIT CSAIL authors (Takehi, Teoh, Zhang, Khattab) call this "Weight-Space Programming." Their model, GPT-6 Astra, reads raw weights, diagnoses a problem, and rewrites the parameters itself.

In the Qwen case, Astra found a weight issue behind the reasoning loops that Qwen's own model card warns about, and fixed it in a Python kernel. Sampling settings stayed the same. The edit also carried over to instruction following, where IFEval rose 3x. Both numbers come from a single 0.8B model, and the post presents them as "very early results."

The smaller experiments are where the claim gets tested more directly:

- **Reading:** given 17,253 raw weights and no list of candidate games, Astra named the right game for all four PPO-trained networks (Breakout at 99% confidence). The networks shared input channels, action sets, and dimensions, so only the weights could give the answer away. Other experiments ask what a diffusion model draws, which language a small LM was trained on, and which of two LMs was trained to be sycophantic.
- **Fixing:** a control policy that never got the car over the strip went from 0% to 100% success after Astra edited 3.5% of its weights in one shot.
- **Writing:** with no environment feedback, Astra wrote small MinAtar game-playing networks from scratch that beat PPO baselines trained for 100M steps. There is also a Go experiment, though the excerpt cuts off before its results.

The authors' broader pitch is that LLM-driven optimization, already used for prompts, harnesses, and training code, now reaches differentiable weights too. They argue it could eventually displace hand-built optimizers like Adam and Muon, and that it could matter for interpretability.

The excerpt doesn't show how robust the edits are across other models, or how much of Astra's success depends on model-card hints like the reasoning-loop warning. A jump from 7.9% to 48.1% on MATH-500 suggests the base model was badly broken at those sampling settings, so the result may reflect repairing a defect more than adding capability.

The thread splits between a skeptic with domain expertise and people who say the skeptic is missing what is notable. The skeptical side got the most support.

**The deflationary case (yurimo, stymaar, others):**
- yurimo works in weight-space learning and says the blog post overstates the results. Weight editing and weight identification already exist, with ROME (2023) as the example.
- Many experiments use tiny models of around 20K parameters, where editing by hand is feasible. The Qwen fix looks like a steering vector. The one attempt to improve math capability reportedly didn't work.
- stymaar says Astra does what a competent ML practitioner would do: read the interpretable parts, or use directional ablation, the same technique used to decensor models.
- YeGoblynQueenne adds that the Gemma 3 270M experiment failed because that model doesn't loop. In their view that is direct evidence a model can't weight-train new capabilities into another model, so the work is not recursive self-improvement (RSI).
- yurimo's reading is that this mostly shows OpenAI trained on mechinterp environments, maybe even on open weight-space datasets. They also doubt naive edits will scale to large overparametrized models. They point to current auto-interp work, which gives models better tools to understand parameter geometry instead of editing blindly.
- chemicalnovae answered pyrale's MoE question by pointing out that even the active experts are far larger than the 20K toy.

**The pushback:**
- youareundersell says the point is that an LLM reasons over a raw dump of opaque weights and hand-wires changes quickly. They clarified that "alien" means beyond human speed and volume, not magic, and they compared it to computers doing arithmetic.
- red75prime says skepticism can overshoot into dismissiveness. A model that has internalized mechinterp research is expected, just as internalizing math research was.
- 10xDev says what matters is scalability and autonomy, and that this could matter for continual learning.
- kenjackson sees the paper as an opening salvo, not a statement of its limits. They also doubt the Schmidhuber comparison fits.

**Other threads:**
- **Why it got upvoted:** bottlepalm and hollowonepl say people are reacting to the idea that this is possible at all. chrsw suspects some of the votes come from anxiety about closed Western labs pulling ahead.
- **Possible leakage:** pyrale wonders whether Astra's skill comes from internal use at OpenAI. If so, asking it to edit your model might be a cheap way to extract that knowledge, like distillation.
- **Tooling:** hashmap argues the real gap is legible, interactive editing tools for humans, since even the best models are still unreliable at discernment.
- **Hype correction:** pera linked a separate submission that visualizes the examples. stymaar and frays said it was better than the paper's coverage, and frays said they would now share that instead.
- **Self-improvement:** yurimo's research pursues weight-space edits as a path to self-improvement and points to hypernetworks (Google Brain, 2016) as prior art. TonyStr asked whether superposition is what makes models rigid, but the comment is cut off.

The unresolved crux is scale. It is unclear whether the "edit weights by reasoning" approach survives beyond tiny or defect-specific cases.

### LLMs are not superhuman at reading and writing neural network weights

#### [Submission URL](https://www.seangoedecke.com/llms-are-not-superhuman-at-reading-and-writing-neural-network-weights/) | 136 points | by [agrm](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=agrm) | [22 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50344861)

**Sean Goedecke's thesis: the "superhuman weight editing" results are things a competent ML researcher would do with a few lines of Python.** He's responding to a paper claiming GPT-6 Astra can read and edit small networks' weights "well past what we'd expect from humans or conventional algorithms," a capability some read as a step toward recursive self-improvement.

His two main rebuttals:

- **Game identification is trivial.** The ~17k-weight toy RL networks have input channels labeled ("agent", "moving objects") and wired directly to 8x8 grid cells. Taking the L2 norm of each input across the first layer's 32 weights gives a heatmap in three lines of NumPy. You'd spot Breakout (player in a horizontal line at the bottom) or Pac-Man (the maze) on sight. The neat part is that Astra did the norms in its head without running code, but he calls that superhuman mental arithmetic, not superhuman AI research.
- **The Qwen math fix is a standard steering recipe.** The 0.8B model wasn't bad at math. It looped after 32k tokens, so any problem needing more than that failed, while everything under 32k already passed. Astra wrote four pairs of looping and non-looping texts, measured which weights contributed to the difference, trimmed the top ones, and tested until the loops stopped. Goedecke says that is essentially the Heretic abliteration playbook, and it's what he'd have done himself. Unlike with the game networks, Astra had Python access here.

His analogy: direct weight editing is like an LLM emitting machine code or patching compiled binaries. It's a neat trick, but the best way to work with binaries is still to write source and compile, and the best way to update a model is still to train it. If LLMs speed up AI research, he expects it to come through better training, not hand-tweaked weights.

He also skips the paper's hand-written neural network from scratch, arguing that anything expressible as a simple program is already known to be expressible as a network. In an edit he answers HN commenters: one found the heatmaps less obvious than he did, which he chalks up to unfamiliarity with toy RL game setups. He stresses he's still very impressed, just not updated beyond "these models can solve Navier-Stokes and find better bounds on the Riemann Conjecture."

The argument is strongest on the game task. On the Qwen fix, it shows the method was conventional, but it doesn't address whether the model chose that method on its own, which is arguably the paper's point.

The thread's main fight is over whether "superhuman" is a fair label or just a framing the original paper invited.

**"Faster, not stranger."** nfw2 and simias argue the capability is the calculator kind. Astra reasons in legible steps and does what a reverse engineer would do: spot traits, guess function, extend the guess. The "LLMs natively understand their weights" reaction struck simias as wild because nothing trains models to do that, and the details show it isn't what happened. antonvs counters that speed and scale are themselves superhuman and can become qualitative. His example is an LLM absorbing a new codebase in minutes versus a human's months, which he puts at roughly 10,000x, and at some point "it starts to seem" like Matrix-style communion.

**Goalpost-moving vs. deflating hype.** lnenad says the skeptics keep shifting the bar and that being impressed should be allowed. aesthesia replies that this is pushback on a misreading the authors encouraged, and quotes the original HN thread to show it. Those comments included "recursive self-improvement," "the first concrete sign that the world is about to end," and "no human can go edit a brain." lnenad says he could quote reactions in the opposite spirit and that the model really did edit weights directly with no tools. kenjackson asks for a real human to take on experiments 4 and 5, including the single-try, no-tools condition, and guesses that only a savant could manage it. That is a challenge to the "a competent researcher could do this" claim.

**Is the heatmap really obvious?** Several people dispute the post's claim that the first three games are identifiable on sight.
- StilesCrisis and kenjackson say they couldn't name Pac-Man, or any of the games, without a list of options. kenjackson checked the prompt and found no choices were given, only "the game, as specifically as you can."
- bradenlc found Pac-Man the easiest, and pera suggests why. Berkeley's CS 188 uses Pac-Man with variable grid sizes to teach Q-learning, so people who know that setup would recognize the layout.
- StilesCrisis notes that Astra would certainly have seen that material, whereas he hadn't.

**Smaller points**
- solyin links the post's binaries analogy to open weights versus open source.
- nagi_builds asks how long a human would need, and simias treats that as beside the point.

### China's First Frontier AI Export Controls: Mofcom Announcement No. 48

#### [Submission URL](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-china-ai-export-controls-announcement-48-2/) | 158 points | by [jonnybgood](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=jonnybgood) | [89 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50334038)

**China now proposes export controls on frontier model weights, agents, and AI-enabled offensive cyber capability, and a registration requirement is already live.** MOFCOM and MOST's Announcement No. 48 (Oct 16, 2026) adds 40 technologies to the export catalogue, 23 of them in AI. The rest cover biomedicine (5) and photovoltaics (12). The catalogue additions are a draft open for comment until November 15. The interim pre-export registration applies now, including to exports already underway.

The draft restricts weights, checkpoints, source code, architecture, and training know-how if a model meets any one of three tests:
- at least 10^26 training FLOPs
- at least 2 trillion parameters
- a global top-10 position on public rankings or call volume at export time

Agents are controlled separately from the model. Planning, multi-agent coordination, and tool-use technologies are restricted above set benchmarks. High-performance agents built for critical infrastructure, defense, or core government use are prohibited outright.

**The service-versus-custody line decides most enterprise exposure.** API, app, web, SaaS, and lightweight on-device use don't count as exporting a model. Deploying a restricted model locally outside China does, though automatic licensing may apply on a Chinese company's own overseas cloud. Registration also triggers on:
- ownership transfer abroad
- licensing for overseas use
- a change of control to a foreign entity

Exporters must judge for themselves whether a technology is in scope. Analysts say the form could even capture intra-group code sharing. Anything already public through open-source release or academic publication is excluded.

The 10^26 FLOP figure mirrors the 2023 U.S. executive order and the since-rescinded 2025 AI Diffusion rule. ChinaTalk's authors estimate no released Chinese model currently crosses it, so the compute test is more signal than bite today. The top-10 ranking test is the vaguer and more discretionary one. MOFCOM calls the revision routine and says it targets no specific country. The CSA note frames it as the second major jurisdiction, alongside the U.S., to put formal outbound controls on frontier AI supply chains.

The thread splits over whether Beijing's "we encourage open source" line should be believed, and over a side-fight about what "open" means.

**Words vs. actions on openness.** byzantinegene quoted MOFCOM's press statement: automatic licensing for open-source AI exports, with "immediate reporting and export", and public-domain technology excluded. They took that as a sign China still backs open weights. Skeptics gave several replies:
- usrnm said what governments do matters more than what they say, and this is a move toward restriction.
- jacquesm countered that nothing has actually changed so far. He said China is playing a soft-power game of undercutting US labs, though that could flip overnight.
- yorwba added a concrete correction. The draft does create some outright prohibitions, such as sandbox detection. A sufficiently capable model could trip one of those, and even "encouraged" exports require advance reporting, which in principle lets the government refuse.
- Iolaum read it as China building the capability to restrict while keeping options open. lumost suggested open-weights models also serve Beijing by commoditizing AI and weakening the frontier labs' influence.
- Some replies were cynical (Roark66: support only "inasmuch it gives them an advantage"). Forgeties79 answered that if self-interest yields more open, locally runnable models, they prefer it to renting from closed US labs.

**What counts as an export.** RobotToaster assumed closed-model exporters would need licenses. yorwba pointed out that API, app, SaaS and lightweight on-device use are explicitly outside the model-export rules, per the MOFCOM press conference, so the rules mainly hit open-weights releases and models hosted in datacenters outside China. fc417fc802 noted the US seems to treat API access as a kind of export, which explains some of the confusion. vikrantrathore linked a book that argues export controls make both sides worse off.

**Does the restriction undercut Chinese models' appeal?** _fw argued Chinese models gained adoption because they are open, and that restricting top-10 models could end "the democratisation of powerful AI". That set off two further arguments:
- **Open source vs. open weights.** injidup, petcat and NooneAtAll5 insisted weights alone are like a binary blob, so "open-weight" is the correct term. Majromax defended "open source" because inference code is public and weights can be inspected and fine-tuned, comparing them to a rendered image. analognoise said even traditional open source depends on closed microcode.
- **Can you trust them?** willis936 asked whether anyone had actually inspected the weights for backdoors. vidarh replied that the risks differ in kind. A closed hosted model sees all your data and can change behavior at will, while an open model's trained-in triggers must be planted blind and can be evaluated. codedokode added that a local model needs no internet connection, which makes a backdoor harder to exploit.

The unresolved question is whether the carve-outs for open weights will hold in practice, or whether the registration and prohibition machinery is the real signal.
