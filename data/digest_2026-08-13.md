### Text AI watermarks will always be trivial to remove

#### [Submission URL](https://www.seangoedecke.com/text-ai-watermarks/) | 139 points | by [pseudolus](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=pseudolus) | [177 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49287153)

**EU rules will soon require AI text to be “detectable,” but every practical scheme either harms quality or breaks under light edits.** Text is too compressed to hide signal like you can in images; any constraint that biases token choice trades off capability (and compute) for a fingerprint a user or post-processor can disturb. Simply “rerunning the model” to check if it likely wrote a passage fails: the space of plausible outputs is huge, leading to false positives, and it’s too expensive to offer as a public verification service.

Google’s SynthID sidesteps that by biasing sampling: assign a per-token score from recent context (e.g., a simple hash/mod over token IDs) and, at each step, pick among the top candidates the one with the highest score. You can then flag text by its aggregate score pattern, and detection is cheap. The catch is obvious to practitioners: zero-temperature decoding leaves no room to embed a fingerprint unless you override user settings, and any watermark that depends on sampling patterns is inherently fragile to edits.

The “one exception” to visible text changes is invisible Unicode, which is why the author suspects some labs quietly swap in homoglyphs (e.g., alternate space characters) as a post-process tag. That kind of signature doesn’t touch semantics—but precisely because it lives in formatting, it’s easy to disrupt, which makes compliance a box-check rather than a durable provenance guarantee.

Much of the discussion frames the upcoming EU watermarking rules as the AI equivalent of GDPR cookie banners: political theater where labs will implement easily bypassed systems just to legally operate in Europe. Commenters debated whether this dynamic constitutes "malicious compliance" by the AI vendors, or just a reflection of lawmakers fundamentally misunderstanding how text generation works.

The crux of the technical disagreement centers on the utility of a fragile watermark. Skeptics argue that anyone actively trying to deceive—such as a professional passing off LLM output as an expensive expert report—will simply use a secondary tool or open local model to paraphrase the text and scrub the signature. Defenders counter that a watermark doesn't need to be cryptographically secure to be useful. If it catches the "low-hanging fruit" of lazy students copy-pasting raw outputs, it successfully reintroduces risk into casual cheating. 

The focus on academic integrity spawned a contentious tangent on testing methods. Some argued that schools must return to supervised pen-and-paper exams to eliminate AI entirely, while others strongly objected that mandatory handwriting rolls back necessary accommodations for students with motor disabilities. 

Ultimately, the thread circles a persistent, unresolved paradox in the regulation: if a detection signal is made public enough to give users transparency, it immediately becomes trivial for bad actors to erase.

### How AI text watermarking works

#### [Submission URL](https://declaude.org/watermarking/) | 126 points | by [padolsey](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=padolsey) | [95 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49292932)

**The watermark doesn’t live in the characters; it lives in a slight, secret-key bias over the model’s next-word choices** — the text reads normally but encodes a pattern. Generation is a sequence of plausible-word “dice rolls”; the key privately splits each step’s candidates (computed from a short local prefix) into green/red and nudges the odds toward green, so the same word can be green in one context and red in another. With the key, detection is just recounting: recolor any text and count greens; unmarked text lands ~50/50, while marked text leans green often enough to reject chance. The catch is strength versus length: production tilts are mild, so confidence comes from many tokens — in the example, even a 1,500‑word document might show only ~55% green under a gentle lean.

Editing attacks break the evidence where wording changes, because only short, untouched windows retain the original key-dependent coloring; paraphrases dilute rather than delete until you approach a full rewrite. On measured implementations (MarkLLM’s KGW/EXP on an open model, run through a full‑rewrite pipeline), ~0.5% of windows survive and detection falls from near‑certain to a coin flip. Variants differ in how they apply the bias — Google’s SynthID uses a tiny secret tournament that preserves the model’s overall odds, while Aaronson’s OpenAI scheme derives the “dice rolls” from the key — but they exploit the same slack in choice. The piece also notes deployment claims: Gemini app/web outputs have been watermarked since 2024 (API excepted), and newer Claude models mark at the model level, which makes marks invisible, copy‑paste resilient, and only detectable by counting with the right key.

The thread is dominated by intense frustration with Claude’s current writing style, which users characterize as patronizing, "streetside Socrates," and structured like a series of "Reddit mic drop" moments. Multiple commenters speculate that these universally disliked prose quirks might actually be the watermarking mechanism manifesting in the text. The stylistic degradation in recent versions is reportedly severe enough that feeding Claude’s convoluted tech-jargon back into a new session immediately triggers model collapse.

A secondary debate centers on whether watermarking is practically enforceable in a market with open weights. One camp argues that users can easily bypass detection by running local models or choosing non-compliant providers, meaning the marks are primarily just a tool for companies to avoid training on their own synthetic exhaust. The counter-argument stresses that the worst offenders of low-effort AI text—students and casual users—lack the technical literacy to deploy local CLI models and will default to watermarked web UIs. The unresolved crux is whether easy-to-use, non-watermarked wrapper services will emerge to shield those non-technical users from detection. 

Other commenters focus on the technical and behavioral side-effects of the scheme:
- **Creativity tax:** Nudging token probabilities inherently degrades a model's creativity, raising questions about how the watermark operates in low-entropy domains like code generation where optimal token choices are narrow.
- **Manual evasion:** Users point out the irony that the most reliable method for stripping a watermark—manually transcribing and rewording the output—effectively reinvents the traditional job of a human writer.
